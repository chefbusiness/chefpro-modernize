<p>If you have wondered <strong>what beurre blanc is</strong>, the plain answer is this: a warm butter sauce you mount, at the last minute, on a short reduction of shallot, white wine and vinegar. The French name means white butter, and along the Loire it still shows up as <strong>beurre nantais</strong>. Say it closer to "burr blahn", the last syllable nasal, than to the way the letters look in English. It tastes of butter and cooked shallot, with a clean acid edge, and the colour stays the colour of the butter. Hollandaise is held by an egg yolk, and nut-brown butter is toasted. Beurre blanc stays pale, and it has no egg. The butter goes in cold, in pieces, off the boil, and the plate leaves while the emulsion is still smooth.</p>
<h2>What beurre blanc is</h2>
<p>It is a warm emulsion with a short life. You concentrate a small base first: finely minced shallot, a dry white wine and white wine vinegar, until a spoonful or two is left and the acid has calmed down. Then you whisk in the butter. The fat disperses in that little bit of liquid and the sauce turns opaque, the colour of butter, thick enough to stay on a piece of fish.</p>
<p>Flour changes the technique and puts you in a <a href="https://aichef.pro/en/blog/what-is-a-roux">roux</a> or a <a href="https://aichef.pro/en/blog/beurre-manie">beurre manié</a>. A yolk is what holds a hollandaise; here the reduction and the butter have to get along by themselves, so the pan cannot boil and it cannot sit. Kitchens that carry it on the menu reduce ahead and mount when the plate is ready. The piece on <a href="https://aichef.pro/en/blog/what-is-an-emulsion">emulsions</a> explains why some sauces survive a wait and this one is mounted when you serve it.</p>

<h2>Where it comes from</h2>
<p>The sauce as it is taught now belongs to the Loire, around Nantes, and its classic plate is pike. The other name, beurre nantais, says as much. Several stories attach themselves to <strong>Clémence Lefeuvre</strong> (1860-1932), and they do not describe the same moment. One puts her at a banquet at the Château de Goulaine, sending out a béarnaise that had lost its yolk and taking the compliments anyway. Another puts the sauce later, at La Buvette de la Marine in Saint-Julien-de-Concelles. Her grandson said a guest compared her melted butter to mussel sauce and she answered by reducing shallot with vinegar. The details differ. What the stories share is the river, the fish and the gesture of mounting butter on that reduction.</p>
<h2>A working ratio</h2>
<p>You need shallot, a dry white wine you would drink, white wine vinegar and cold unsalted butter. The butter is almost the whole yield, so a tired block shows. Muscadet is the wine people name; any clean dry white will reduce. Grey shallot is the traditional one. An ordinary shallot, cut fine, will do.</p>
<p>For about 200 ml at service, this ratio repeats cleanly: wine and vinegar reduced to a spoonful or two, and several times that weight in butter. If you want it sharper, reduce further. If you want it rounder, add a little more butter. Pouring in extra vinegar, without reducing it, leaves the acid raw.</p>

<p>Cream is not in the table. Some kitchens beat a spoon of cream into the reduction and reduce it by about half before the butter, so the sauce holds a little longer. That is a fair call on a long service, and it belongs on the recipe because the flavour has moved. Stirring cream in at the end, as a patch, just weighs the sauce down.</p>
<h2>How to make it</h2>
<p>Split the job. The reduction can be made ahead. The butter goes in when the plate is ready.</p>
<h3>1. Reduce shallot, wine and vinegar</h3>
<p>Put all three in a small heavy pan, the sort that does not leave a hot spot in the middle. Boil until one or two tablespoons remain, the liquid still glossy on the shallot, not dry and not coloured. A caught shallot tastes bitter in the finished sauce. A watery reduction stays sharp and gives the butter nothing to hold on to. Taste for cooked shallot and tamed vinegar.</p>
<h3>2. Whisk in cold butter</h3>
<p>Take the pan off, or drop the flame to nothing. Add the butter a couple of cubes at a time and do not add the next pair until the last has disappeared. Halfway through, the sauce should look like pouring cream and you can move a little faster, still watching the heat. The moment it wants to boil, pull the pan. A lot of cooks work with the whisk in one hand and the handle in the other, lifting the pan on and off the burner. A heavy pan that heats evenly is kinder to this sauce than a thin one with a hot spot in the middle.</p>
<h3>3. Season, strain if that is the house style, and send</h3>
<p>Salt and white pepper at the end. Some kitchens leave the shallot in, and the sauce has more texture; others strain it, and the shallot stays as a memory. Both are fine if the recipe says so. Mount the butter when the fish is on the plate, not hours before service.</p>
<h2>Why it splits, and how you bring it back</h2>
<p>The sauce splits when the fat and the little bit of water stop sharing the pan. You see it before you taste it: the surface shines like oil and the acid sits underneath, clear. Three things do it, over and over. The pan boiled, or one patch of the base ran away with the heat. The butter went in soft, or all at once. The reduction was cooked dry, so there was no water left to emulsify into, or it was so wet the fat never thickened.</p>
<p>If you still have a base, the rescue is the same gesture in a clean pan: a spoon of reduction or cold water, off the heat, and cold butter whisked in until the emulsion returns. If the fat has fully clarified and the liquid has parted company, make another one. Flour will not fix it.</p>
<p>To hold it for a few minutes, set the pan over hot water that is not simmering, and stir gently every few minutes. A hollandaise will sit in that bath for a while. This sauce separates if you forget it there.</p>

<h2>Lemon, citrus, and lemon-caper</h2>
<p>The Loire sauce takes its acid from white wine vinegar. A lemon beurre blanc keeps the same mount and moves that acid: lemon juice stands in for the vinegar, or shares the reduction with a smaller splash of it. The butter is still cold, the pan still stays off the boil, and the recipe says lemon, because the plate tastes brighter.</p>
<p>Citrus beyond lemon, such as orange, blood orange or yuzu, is the same decision with another fruit. The juice goes into the reduction. A quiet squeeze over a finished classic is a different plate. Name the fruit. A guest who ordered beurre blanc is waiting for shallot, wine and vinegar.</p>
<p>Lemon-caper is that lemon sauce with capers. Rinse them first, or the brine salts the pan before you have tasted it. Stir them in when the butter is already emulsified and the pan is off the heat, so they stay whole. Then taste, and salt only if the capers left room. A nut-brown butter finished with lemon is a beurre noisette, and it is a different sauce from this emulsion.</p>
<p>Leaving the wine out is another named change. Vinegar plus a spoon of water can still hold the butter, and the sauce comes up sharper, with less of the roundness the wine was there to give. Write that on the recipe.</p>
<h2>What it goes with</h2>
<p>Pike is the classic. On a modern menu the same sauce sits happily with sole, turbot, hake, monkfish, a just-seared scallop or warm asparagus. Salmon takes it if the wine can stand up to the fat. It argues with a dark meat glaze on the same bite.</p>
<p>What you prepare ahead is the strained reduction, in a small container, and the butter in cubes, kept cold. That bowl is the visible cost of every portion. If service cannot hold the minute, choose a sauce that can sit, and leave beurre blanc off that menu.</p>
<h2>Mistakes that show up on the plate</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>It tastes of shallot butter, with no acid:</strong> the shallot was sweated in a knob of butter and the vinegar never went in. The acid is part of the sauce. Reduce it again with wine and vinegar before you mount.</li>
<li><strong>The sauce stays thin and splits:</strong> the butter went in soft, or all at once, and melted before it emulsified. Use cold cubes and wait until each pair has disappeared.</li>
<li><strong>It never emulsifies:</strong> the reduction cooked dry and there is no water left for the fat to hold on to. Add a spoon of water or wine and mount again with cold butter.</li>
<li><strong>It stays thin and the vinegar tastes raw:</strong> the reduction was too wet. Keep reducing to one or two tablespoons before you add more butter.</li>
<li><strong>Cream dulls the flavour:</strong> pouring it over a finished sauce does not stabilise the emulsion. If you want that help, the cream goes into the reduction and cooks down before the butter.</li>
<li><strong>By the time you serve, it has split:</strong> it was mounted in the morning and left to wait. Reduce ahead. Mount when the plate is ready.</li>
</ul>
