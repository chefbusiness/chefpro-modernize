<p><strong>Beurre blanc</strong> is the white butter sauce you mount, to the minute, on a short reduction of shallot, white wine and vinegar. The French name means white butter, and along the Loire it still shows up as <strong>beurre nantais</strong>. Say it closer to "burr blahn", the last syllable nasal, than to the way the letters look in English. The butter is not a finish you flick over a finished sauce. The butter is the sauce. It goes in cold, in pieces, off the boil, and the plate leaves while the emulsion is still smooth. It tastes of butter and cooked shallot, with a clean acid edge, and the colour stays the colour of the butter. Egg and a nut-brown toast belong to other sauces.</p>
<h2>What beurre blanc is</h2>
<p>On the station it is a warm emulsion with a short life. You concentrate a small base first: finely minced shallot, a dry white wine and white wine vinegar, until a spoonful or two is left and the acid has calmed down. Then you whisk in the butter. The fat disperses in that little bit of liquid and the sauce turns opaque, the colour of butter, thick enough to stay on a piece of fish.</p>
<p>Flour has no business here. Put it in and you have started a <a href="https://aichef.pro/en/blog/what-is-a-roux">roux</a> or a <a href="https://aichef.pro/en/blog/beurre-manie">beurre manié</a>. There is no yolk either, which is the practical difference from a hollandaise. The reduction and the butter have to get along by themselves, so the pan cannot boil and it cannot sit. Kitchens that carry it on the menu reduce ahead and mount on the call. The piece on <a href="https://aichef.pro/en/blog/what-is-an-emulsion">emulsions</a> is the longer explanation of why some sauces survive a service and this one asks for the minute.</p>

<h2>Where the story comes from</h2>
<p>The sauce as it is taught now belongs to the Loire, around Nantes, and its classic plate is pike. The other name, beurre nantais, says as much. Several stories attach themselves to <strong>Clémence Lefeuvre</strong> (1860-1932), and they do not match. One puts her at a banquet at the Château de Goulaine, sending out a béarnaise that had lost its yolk and taking the compliments anyway. Another puts the sauce later, at La Buvette de la Marine in Saint-Julien-de-Concelles. Her grandson, in an account reported in 2014, said a guest compared her melted butter to mussel sauce and she answered by reducing shallot with vinegar. Tell them as kitchen stories. What they share is the river, the fish and the gesture.</p>
<p>Julia Child’s line is still the one worth remembering on the pass: warm butter, seasoned with shallot, wine, vinegar, salt and pepper. The name itself is older than that recipe. A seventeenth-century book already sets a beurre blanc next to pumpkin, without saying what was in it. The sauce of the pass is the Loire one.</p>
<h2>A working ratio</h2>
<p>You need shallot, a dry white wine you would drink, white wine vinegar and cold unsalted butter. The butter is almost the whole yield, so a tired block shows. Muscadet is the wine people name; any clean dry white will reduce. Grey shallot is the traditional one. An ordinary shallot, cut fine, will do.</p>
<p>Published recipes scatter. One classic American card mounts three sticks of butter on a quarter-cup of wine and a quarter-cup of vinegar, boiled down to two tablespoons. A British one uses 100 ml of wine, 50 ml of vinegar and 200 g of butter. Home recipes sometimes skip the vinegar and stop at 100 g of butter. The table below is a service base for about 200 ml, in the range where the careful cards agree: wine and vinegar reduced to a spoonful or two, and several times that weight in butter. If you want it sharper, reduce further. If you want it rounder, add a little more butter. Do not just pour in more vinegar.</p>

<p>Cream is not in the table. Some teaching kitchens beat a spoon of double cream into the reduction and reduce it by about half before the butter, so the sauce holds a little longer. That is a fair call on a long service, and it belongs on the spec because the flavour has moved. Stirring cream in at the end, as a patch, just weighs the sauce down.</p>
<h2>How to mount it</h2>
<p>Split the job. The reduction is mise en place. The mount belongs to the pass.</p>
<h3>1. Reduce shallot, wine and vinegar</h3>
<p>Put all three in a small heavy pan, the sort that does not leave a hot spot in the middle. Boil until one or two tablespoons remain, the liquid still glossy on the shallot, not dry and not coloured. A caught shallot tastes bitter in the finished sauce. A watery reduction stays sharp and gives the butter nothing to hold on to. Taste for cooked shallot and tamed vinegar.</p>
<h3>2. Whisk in cold butter</h3>
<p>Take the pan off, or drop the flame to nothing. Add the butter a couple of cubes at a time and do not add the next pair until the last has disappeared. Halfway through, the sauce should look like pouring cream and you can move a little faster, still watching the heat. The moment it wants to boil, pull the pan. A lot of cooks work with the whisk in one hand and the handle in the other, lifting the pan on and off the burner. A heavy pan that heats evenly is kinder to this sauce than a thin one with a hot spot in the middle.</p>
<h3>3. Season, strain if that is the house style, and send</h3>
<p>Salt and white pepper at the end. Some kitchens leave the shallot in. Curnonsky wanted it reduced to a distant presence, which means straining. Both are legitimate if the spec says so. Mounting at eleven in the morning is not.</p>
<h2>Why it splits, and how you bring it back</h2>
<p>The sauce splits when the fat and the little bit of water stop sharing the pan. You see it before you taste it: the surface shines like oil and the acid sits underneath, clear. Three things do it, over and over. The pan boiled, or one patch of the base ran away with the heat. The butter went in soft, or all at once. The reduction was cooked dry, so there was no water left to emulsify into, or it was so wet the fat never thickened.</p>
<p>If you still have a base, the rescue is the same gesture in a clean pan: a spoon of reduction or cold water, off the heat, and cold butter whisked in until the emulsion returns. If the fat has fully clarified and the liquid has parted company, make another one. Flour will not fix it.</p>
<p>To hold it for a few minutes, set the pan over hot water that is not simmering, and stir gently every few minutes. It is not a hollandaise bain-marie you can forget.</p>

<p>A mounted meat jus can be excellent and still be the wrong plate. The guest is waiting for something pale and sharp. <a href="https://aichef.pro/en/blog/monter-au-beurre">Mounting with butter</a> uses the same cold cube, on a sauce that was already there. A <a href="https://aichef.pro/en/blog/gastrique">gastrique</a> is a different acid altogether, sugar and vinegar, and it does not stand in for this reduction.</p>
<p>Two variations get asked for as if they were the classic. Garlic is one. A grated clove will sit on top of the shallot and the wine, and the plate tastes of garlic. If that is the ticket, write it. Leaving the wine out is the other. Vinegar, or lemon, plus a spoon of water can still hold the butter, and the sauce comes up sharper, with less of the roundness the wine was there to give. Lemon juice in place of the vinegar is the same kind of decision: a citrus beurre blanc, named on the spec, not a quiet squeeze at the pass.</p>
<h2>On the pass</h2>
<p>Pike is the classic. On a modern menu the same sauce sits happily with sole, turbot, hake, monkfish, a just-seared scallop or warm asparagus. Salmon takes it if the wine can stand up to the fat, which is why so many American cards put the two together. It argues with a dark meat glaze on the same bite.</p>
<p>Mise is the strained reduction, in a small container, and the butter in cubes in the station fridge. That bowl is the visible cost of every portion. If the service cannot hold the minute, choose a sauce that can sit, and leave beurre blanc off that menu.</p>

<h2>Mistakes that show up on the plate</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Sweating the shallot in a knob of butter and skipping the vinegar.</strong> You get shallot butter. The acid is part of the sauce.</li>
<li><strong>Soft butter, all at once.</strong> It melts before it emulsifies. Cold cubes, a little at a time.</li>
<li><strong>A dry reduction.</strong> With no water left, there is no emulsion. A spoon of water or wine, then mount again.</li>
<li><strong>A wet reduction.</strong> The sauce stays thin and the vinegar tastes raw. Reduce before you add more butter.</li>
<li><strong>Cream at the end “so it will not split”.</strong> It can help if it goes into the reduction and cooks down. Poured over a finished sauce, it just dulls it.</li>
<li><strong>A morning mount.</strong> By service it has split or been kept too hot. Reduce ahead. Mount when the table is called.</li>
</ul>
