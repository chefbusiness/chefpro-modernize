A starch slurry is starch dispersed in cold liquid, then poured into a hot sauce while you whisk, to thicken it. In Spanish kitchens the same mixture is called a papilla. Escoffier thickens his jus de veau lié with 30 g of arrowroot per liter, slaked in cold stock, after reducing 4 liters of veal stock to 1 liter. For an everyday sauce with cornstarch, a starting amount is 8 g of starch and 15 ml of cold liquid for every 250 ml of sauce.

## What a starch slurry is

Starch thickens when its grains heat inside a liquid. They take up water, swell, and the sauce gains body. If the grains go in dry and in a clump, the ones on the outside swell, form a skin, and the ones inside stay raw. That is why you disperse them first in cold liquid, until not a lump remains. That mixture is the slurry.

Unlike a [roux](https://aichef.pro/en/blog/what-is-a-roux), there is no fat here and no toasting. A slurry changes the flavor of the sauce very little, which is why you notice at once if you overdo it: the body goes up and the taste of the stock goes down. [Beurre manié](https://aichef.pro/en/blog/beurre-manie) also goes in at the end, but it carries butter and wheat flour. A slurry is starch alone.

## Where the amounts come from

In Le Guide Culinaire (1903), Escoffier's jus de veau lié is the clean example. He starts with 4 liters of clear veal stock (a little over 4 quarts), reduces it by three quarters to 1 liter, and thickens it with 30 g of arrowroot slaked in a few spoonfuls of cold stock. Then he strains it. The arrowroot leaves him a clear jus, which is what he wants.

For an everyday sauce with cornstarch, a starting amount that gives a medium body is smaller: 8 g of starch, a level tablespoon, dispersed in 15 ml of cold liquid, for every 250 ml of sauce (about 1 cup). Simmer for one or two minutes and see whether it [naps](https://aichef.pro/en/blog/nap-a-sauce). If it still runs, add more slurry. If it already coats, stop. The acid in tomato or wine sometimes asks for a little more, because it weakens this starch.

## Ingredients and proportions

For 250 ml of sauce, with cornstarch:

| Ingredient | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Cornstarch | 8 g (1 level tablespoon) |
| Water or stock, well chilled | 15 ml (1 tablespoon) |
| Sauce you want to thicken | 250 ml (about 1 cup) |

For Escoffier's jus, the count is different, because the stock is already reduced:

| Ingredient | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Clear veal stock | 4 liters, reduced to 1 liter |
| Arrowroot | 30 g (a scant 4 tablespoons) |
| Cold stock, for slaking | a few spoonfuls |

Keep the two tables apart. Thirty grams of cornstarch in a liter of sauce that has not been reduced leaves it almost like a custard.

## How to use it, step by step

<figure><img src="/blog-assets/2026/05/roux-cuerpo.jpg" alt="A cook pouring slurry into a simmering sauce and whisking, the surface turning thicker and a little less shiny." loading="lazy" style="width:100%;height:auto;border-radius:8px;margin:24px 0;" /></figure>

**1. Disperse the starch.** In a bowl or a cup, with the cold liquid. Whisk until it is smooth and cloudy, with no powder sitting on the bottom. If it stands, it settles. Whisk it again just before you use it.

**2. Pour it in a thread into the hot sauce.** Medium heat, whisking. If you dump the slurry in and do not whisk, it sets in streaks.

**3. Cook it just enough.** With cornstarch, bring the sauce to a gentle simmer and keep it there for one or two minutes. The raw taste goes, and the body appears. With arrowroot, Escoffier stirs it into the stock he has already reduced, then strains. It does not need a long boil. A long, violent boil thins it and can leave it stringy.

**4. Check the spoon and the salt.** Starch does not season. If the sauce was already right, thickening makes it taste milder. Adjust at the end.

## Why it fails, and how to fix it

Lumps come from dry starch, from a slurry that was poorly whisked, or from not moving the sauce as you pour. If they are already there, whisk hard, away from a violent boil. If they remain, strain it. A lump you strain out is sauce you have lost, so disperse it better next time.

If it does not thicken, the slurry was too small, the sauce was too acidic, or the cornstarch has not yet come to a simmer. Give it that minute. Then decide whether to add more.

If it thickens and then, after ten minutes of boiling, it loosens, you gave it too much heat and too much time. Cornstarch loses strength in that cooking. Take off what you can serve, and do not keep boiling the rest. For a sauce that has to reduce for an hour, start with a roux.

If it looks dull and tastes of paste, you used too much. Lengthen it with a little stock and taste again. The shine of a reduction does not fully come back. That is the price of thickening at the last minute.

## During service

Make the slurry at the moment, or shortly before. It gains nothing from hours of waiting. During service, keep the starch measured and the cold liquid beside it, and thicken only the pot that is about to go out. A sauce thickened at the start and held at a boil will be thinner by the end.

Skip the freezer if you can. On thawing, cornstarch sauces weep. A roux travels better.

## Variations

**Arrowroot.** Escoffier's choice for a clear jus. It gives a cleaner shine than cornstarch. A long, hard boil makes it lose body.

**Potato starch.** It thickens with a small amount and leaves the sauce fairly clear. If you whisk it too much, or boil it too long, it turns gluey. Add it at the end and stop as soon as the sauce naps.

**Cornstarch.** The easiest to find. It leaves the sauce a little cloudy. It wants that short simmer to take away the raw taste.

**Flour stirred into water.** It also thickens, and you have to cook it longer so it does not taste of raw flour. It is a poor stand-in for a roux: you skip the fat and the toast, and you are left with the cereal. In a dark sauce, you can taste it.

## What you use it for

A jus you want clear, a vegetable sauce, a sauté with its own liquid, and a last-minute correction when there is no time to reduce. It does not stand in for the roux of a béchamel or of an espagnole, because those flavors depend on flour cooked in fat.

If the sauce already naps from reduction, leave the slurry out. It takes away shine and adds body the sauce did not ask for.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why do you mix it with cold liquid?**
Because dry starch dropped onto a hot sauce gels on the outside and traps dry powder inside. That is a lump. Cold liquid separates the grains. When they hit the hot sauce, they swell all at once.

**How much starch do you need?**
For a medium body with cornstarch, 8 g and 15 ml of cold liquid for every 250 ml of sauce. That is a starting amount: simmer for a minute and look at the spoon. For a veal jus he has already reduced, Escoffier uses 30 g of arrowroot per liter.

**Is a slurry the same as a roux?**
Both thicken with starch, and the path is different. A roux cooks the flour in fat before the liquid, and it brings the flavor of that cooking. A slurry is pure starch, with no fat, and it goes in at the end. It does not toast.

**Cornstarch, arrowroot, or potato starch?**
Cornstarch wants a short simmer so it finishes thickening and loses its raw taste, and it leaves the sauce a little cloudy. Arrowroot is what Escoffier uses when he wants a clear jus. Potato starch thickens hard and turns gluey if you work it too much. With all three, stop when the sauce naps.

**Can you throw dry starch into the sauce by the handful?**
If you want a smooth sauce, make the slurry first, with no lumps, then pour it into the pot in a thread while you whisk. Leftover slurry is a poor thing to keep overnight: the starch settles and you have to whisk it again. Make only what you need.

**When is a slurry a poor choice?**
When you want the flavor of a toasted roux, when the sauce will be frozen, or when a long boil is still ahead. Cornstarch can thin if it boils for a long time. For a stock that will reduce for hours, the roux goes in at the start.
