Glace de viande is brown stock reduced very slowly until it is thick and glossy, like a syrup. The name means "meat glaze." Hot, it coats a spoon in a layer that does not run off. Cold, it sets like a firm jelly, because the gelatin from the bones is highly concentrated. Auguste Escoffier describes it in Le Guide Culinaire (1903) and uses it to strengthen sauces and glaze meat. He also mounts it with butter and serves it as a sauce.

## What glace de viande is

You start with brown stock that is already strained and unsalted. You evaporate water until what remains is mostly flavor and gelatin. There is no flour. Hot, it shines and falls from the spoon in slow, wide drops. Cold, it behaves like a firm jelly.

[Demi-glace](https://aichef.pro/en/blog/what-is-demi-glace) is reduced less, and it is a different preparation. It contains espagnole, so it has roux and tomato, and it is finished with sherry or Madeira. An easy way to tell them apart is to chill them. Glace de viande sets into a firm dark block with no flour taste. Classic demi-glace sets into a soft jelly.

## Where it comes from

Escoffier makes glace de viande by reducing the brown stock he calls estouffade. As the volume drops, he strains the liquid through cloth into smaller and smaller pans. The heat can be high at the start, but it has to be gentle at the end, because with little liquid left the glaze scorches quickly.

According to Escoffier, the glaze is ready when it coats the spoon in a glossy layer that clings, in other words when it naps the spoon ("qu'elle nappe la cuiller"). For a paler, milder glaze, he suggests reducing ordinary veal stock instead of brown stock.

## Ingredients and ratios

How much glaze you get depends on how much gelatin the stock has, so there is no exact figure. As a guide, you end up with about one-tenth of what you started with: 2 liters (about 8 1/2 cups) of good brown stock gives roughly 200 ml (a scant cup) of glaze.

| Ingredient | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Brown stock or veal stock, unsalted and strained | 2 liters (about 8 1/2 cups) |

If the stock is cloudy or fatty, skim and strain it before you start. Fat does not blend into the glaze. It floats on top and turns bitter if it burns.

## How to make it, step by step

<figure><img src="/blog-assets/2026/05/fondo-oscuro-cuerpo.jpg" alt="Roasted bones and vegetables in a tray, ready for a brown stock" loading="lazy" style="width:100%;height:auto;border-radius:8px;margin:24px 0;" /></figure>

**1. Degrease and strain the stock.** This is easiest when the stock is cold, because the fat sets on the surface and lifts off with a spoon.

**2. Reduce it in a wide pan.** Medium heat at first, skimming. When it has dropped by half, strain it into a smaller pan.

**3. Lower the heat.** In the last stage the liquid is thick and sticks easily. Keep it at a very gentle simmer that barely moves the surface. To check whether it is ready, dip a clean spoon: it should come out coated in a glossy layer that does not run off like water.

**4. Cool it and store it.** Pour it into a shallow container. It sets as it cools. Cut it into portions.

## Why it fails and how to fix it

If it scorches on the bottom of the pan, it turns bitter, and the bitterness spreads through the whole glaze. Sugar or wine will not hide it, so discard the part that tastes burnt. Next time, move it to a smaller pan sooner and lower the heat.

If it still does not set when cold after a long reduction, the stock had too little gelatin: it had too few bones, cooked too briefly, or was made from bones that had already been used. You cannot fix that during the reduction. Next time, make the stock with collagen-rich bones such as veal shank, knuckles, or feet, and cook it long enough.

If it is salty, the stock was salted. Once reduced, it cannot be fixed, which is why stock for glace is never salted.

## During service

Make the glaze ahead. It takes up little space and keeps better than the stock it came from. During service, melt only the amount you need over a water bath and do not boil it, because it dries out and can turn bitter.

A teaspoon melted into a sauce or a thin jus gives it body and flavor. Brushing a roast with hot glaze gives it shine. Escoffier also mounts it with butter or cream to serve it as a sauce.

Covered in the refrigerator, it keeps for about a week. Frozen in small portions, it keeps for several months. If it smells sour or rancid when you take it out, throw it away.

## Variations

**Veal glaze.** Made the same way with veal stock. It is paler and milder.

**Poultry or game glaze.** Made the same way with poultry or game stock. Each tastes of the stock it comes from, so they are not interchangeable.

**Jus de veau lié.** Escoffier also describes it in Le Guide Culinaire, but it is a different preparation: 4 liters of veal stock reduced to 1 liter and thickened with 30 g of arrowroot mixed with a little cold stock. It is a starch-thickened sauce and does not set when cold the way a glaze does.

## What to use it for

Use it to strengthen sauces and jus, to glaze roast meat, and to keep the flavor of a stock on hand in very little space: one teaspoon gives a sauce the flavor of a good splash of reduced stock.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is glace de viande a demi-glace?**
They are two different reductions. Escoffier's demi-glace starts from espagnole and brown stock, and is finished with sherry or Madeira. A meat glaze is stock alone, reduced to a syrup, with no flour and no wine.

**Why does it gel when it cools?**
During the long cooking of the stock, collagen in the bones turns into gelatin. Reduction concentrates that gelatin. In the cold, gelatin sets and the glaze can be cut. Heat melts it again.

**How far do you reduce it?**
Escoffier gives no figure. He says to stop when the glaze coats the spoon in a glossy layer that clings. As a guide, you usually end up with about one-tenth of the starting stock, but it depends on the gelatin. A stock with little gelatin can reduce a long way and still never set.

**Can you use salted broth?**
It is a poor start. Escoffier wants unsalted stock, because reduction concentrates everything in it. Salted broth becomes inedible before it reaches a syrup. A very thin broth, with little gelatin, may never set no matter how far you reduce it.

**Can you use it on its own, as a sauce?**
Not as it is, because it is far too strong. You use it by the teaspoon to strengthen sauces and jus, or melted to glaze meat. To serve it as a sauce, Escoffier mounts it with butter or cream.

**How do you pronounce glace de viande?**
Roughly "glahss duh vee-AHND." You hear the s in glace; the final e is silent.
