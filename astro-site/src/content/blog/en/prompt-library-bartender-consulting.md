<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Why prompts matter with Bartender Consultant Pro</h2>
<p>Precise prompts are the difference between a generic recipe and a bar-ready asset. When you tell the agent “Build a house margarita with our well tequila ($18/L), fresh lime, agave syrup, 32% pour cost, in a 12 oz rocks glass, half-salted rim, with a costed card and 30-second training script,” you get a working document. Vague requests like “clarify a piña colada” yield theory; adding “milk punch method, 12% ABV, vegan, and TTB labeling notes” forces the agent to deliver a spec you can execute tonight.</p>
<p>Regulatory precision matters just as much. US bars follow FDA Food Code and TTB rules; UK bars answer to the FSA and Natasha’s Law; Australia/New Zealand to FSANZ; India to FSSAI. A prompt that names the relevant regulator and the exact obligation — sulfite disclosure, egg allergen warning, ABV tolerance — ensures the output is legally sound for your market. This library’s prompts embed that specificity so you don’t have to guess.</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Tips for getting more out of Bartender Consultant Pro</h2>
<h3 class="wp-block-heading">Specify the Technique</h3>
<p>Always lock in the cocktail technique: shaken, stirred, built, or dry shake. Technique drives dilution, texture, and overall drinking experience. Prompt example: 'Provide a Daiquiri recipe using the shaken method with an exact build in fluid ounces.'</p>
<h3 class="wp-block-heading">Demand a Beverage Cost Breakdown</h3>
<p>Always ask for a line-by-line beverage cost, listing spirit and ingredient prices in dollars. Example: “Cost out a Manhattan with rye at $40 per liter and vermouth at $22 per liter, targeting a 20% pour cost.”</p>
<h3 class="wp-block-heading">Define Your Output Format</h3>
<p>Tell the agent whether you need a spec sheet, a bar manual entry, or a cost table. Example: “Lay out the menu as a table with cocktail name, formula, technique, garnish, and beverage cost percentage.”</p>
<h3 class="wp-block-heading">Target Your Audience</h3>
<p>Specify the venue type and clientele: a high-end cocktail den, a luxury hotel, a beach bar, or a corporate gig. Example: “Menu for a downtown rooftop bar with a young professional crowd, $16 average cocktail price.”</p>
<h3 class="wp-block-heading">Use Professional Jargon</h3>
<p>Drop precise bar jargon—variance, pour cost, contribution margin, batching, fat washing—to elicit operational-level responses. Example: “Calculate the monthly variance on a well of your bar pouring from three gin bottles, accounting for evaporation, spillage, and overpour.”</p>
<h3 class="wp-block-heading">Blend Classics and Originals</h3>
<p>When building a menu, always instruct the agent to combine classics and original creations for a balanced program. Example: “Build a six-drink list: two tried‑and‑true classics (Mojito, Martini) and four signatures highlighting American craft spirits.”</p>
<h3 class="wp-block-heading">Set Clear Constraints</h3>
<p>Always spell out your constraints: max beverage cost percentage, banned items, ABV boundaries, or ingredient count cap. Example: “Zero‑proof cocktail list, pour cost must stay under 18%, strict limit of three main ingredients per drink.”</p>
<h3 class="wp-block-heading">Provide Business Context</h3>
<p>Always share operational context—seat count, kitchen footprint, target spend, volume, and whether you’ll batch. Example: “A 40‑seat lounge with a compact kitchen, building a batch‑friendly, high‑production aperitif list to quickly hold a $15 average ticket.”</p>
<h3 class="wp-block-heading">Iterate and Refine</h3>
<p>Think of the agent as your barback: take the first draft and iterate quickly. Send tweaks like “Lower the pour cost to 19% by pricing in a more affordable vermouth, and retest the flavor balance.”</p>
<h3 class="wp-block-heading">Ask for the “Why”</h3>
<p>Always demand explanations for technique choices and balance decisions so you and your team learn.
