<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Why prompts matter with Bakery Consultant Pro</h2>
<p>In baking, the difference between a good loaf and a great one often comes down to a single percentage point of hydration or a few degrees of dough temperature. A vague prompt like “give me a brioche formula” will get you a generic recipe, but a precise prompt that specifies your flour’s protein and ash, your mixer type, and your fermentation window will yield a formula tuned to your actual production. The agent responds to these details by adjusting the baker’s percentages, pre-ferment ratios, and mixing times, so you avoid the trial-and-error that costs time and dough.</p>
<p>Similarly, when costing a laminated dough, you need to account for bake loss, trim waste, and the price of butter in your market. Without those inputs, the agent can’t give you a per-unit cost that reflects your P&L. The same goes for managing a sourdough culture: telling the agent your ambient temperature, feeding schedule, and flour blend lets it diagnose a sluggish starter or scale up a mother for a 200-loaf production run. Precision in prompts translates directly to precision in the bakeshop.</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Tips for getting more out of Bakery Consultant Pro</h2>
<h3 class="wp-block-heading">Formula Basics</h3>
<p>Always include flour type and strength. For US kitchens, list protein percentage instead of W index. Example: "Sourdough made with 13% protein bread flour, 80% hydration." This ensures bakers can precisely replicate dough handling across batches.</p>
<h3 class="wp-block-heading">Fermentation Specs</h3>
<p>Log bulk fermentation temp and time in °F: 'Bulk at 78°F (26°C) for 4 hours, with stretch and folds every 60 minutes.' Consistent logging lets you troubleshoot and scale recipes, hitting your DDT every time.</p>
<h3 class="wp-block-heading">Starter Inoculation</h3>
<p>List inoculation percentage and feeding schedule: '20% liquid levain, fed twice before final mix.' This transparency lets bakers adjust fermentation speed and acidity to match their production timeline, preventing over-proofing and maintaining consistent loaf quality throughout the bake schedule.</p>
<h3 class="wp-block-heading">Desired Dough Temp (DDT)</h3>
<p>Always state your DDT target, like 76–78°F (24–26°C), for optimal gluten development. This helps bakers calculate friction factor and water temperature, ensuring consistent fermentation from batch to batch. Mention DDT on every formula card to dial in your process.</p>
<h3 class="wp-block-heading">Baking Loss</h3>
<p>Factor baking loss into your food cost. For pan de cristal, a 10% weight loss after baking is normal. Use post-bake weight for accurate pricing, not raw dough weight. This prevents underpricing and protects margins in your costing spreadsheet.</p>
<h3 class="wp-block-heading">Flour Extensibility</h3>
<p>Specify flour's stretch-to-elasticity ratio if available. P/L 0.65 flour yields the open, irregular crumb of a classic baguette. In the US, check farinograph data; share that with your mill to match the right flour and hydration level.</p>
<h3 class="wp-block-heading">Hydration Adjustments</h3>
<p>Tailor hydration to your flour and mixer. '78% hydration ciabatta on a spiral mixer' assumes a specific flour absorption. Always note your mixer type and brand; a diving-arm mixer may require 2–3% less water to achieve the same dough feel.</p>
<h3 class="wp-block-heading">Cold Retard</h3>
<p>Specify retard temp and duration for flavor. 'Retard at 38°F (3–4°C) for 12 hours develops umami and a blistered crust.' This step also improves schedule flexibility; note if the dough is covered or unprotected to prevent skinning.</p>
<h3 class="wp-block-heading">Scoring & Steam</h3>
<p>Describe scoring tool, angle, and steam. 'Score with a curved lame at a 45° angle; inject steam for the first 8 minutes of baking.' This sets the stage for dramatic oven spring, ear development, and an attractive grigne.</p>
<h3 class="wp-block-heading">Share Your Equipment</h3>
<p>Always list your key equipment for recipe adaptation. 'I use a 30-quart spiral mixer and a deck oven.'
