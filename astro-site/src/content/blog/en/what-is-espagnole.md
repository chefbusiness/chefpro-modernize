Espagnole, also called Spanish sauce, is a mother sauce of brown stock, a dark roux, caramelized mirepoix, and tomato. The stock comes from roasted bones. The roux is cooked to a deep mahogany. After a long, quiet simmer the sauce is the base of demi-glace and of the brown sauces that follow. The name is French. The sauce is not a recipe from Spain.

## What espagnole is

Carême set it among the great sauces, and Escoffier kept it as one of the five mothers, beside béchamel, [velouté](https://aichef.pro/en/blog/what-is-veloute), hollandaise, and tomato. The name has always been a puzzle. It is not a Spanish national sauce. The account most cooks repeat is the tomato: an American plant that reached French pots by way of Spain. Another account is a compliment to the Spanish court, when the two kitchens borrowed from each other. Neither story changes the pot.

The sauce stands on two pillars. The liquid is [brown stock](https://aichef.pro/en/blog/what-is-brown-stock), from roasted veal, beef, or game bones, dark and lightly gelatinous. The thickener is a [roux](https://aichef.pro/en/blog/what-is-a-roux) cooked to brown, the *roux brun*. Mirepoix, tomato, and a bouquet of bay, thyme, and peppercorns complete it. Velouté is the pale sibling: unroasted stock, blond roux, no tomato. Espagnole is built for red meat, game, and the long brown sauces.

On an English prep list you will see both espagnole and Spanish sauce. They are the same pot. French lists say sauce espagnole, or simply espagnole.

## How it is made

A professional batch is a sequence, not one stir.

**1. The dark roux.** Melt butter, add an equal weight of flour, and stir over medium heat until the paste goes from blond to a deep mahogany, almost chocolate. That takes 8 to 15 minutes. Do not let it blacken. Black is bitter, and you cannot stir the bitterness out. Keep the roux warm.

**2. The stock.** Pour hot brown stock onto the roux in a thread, whisking so it stays smooth. A working ratio is 60 g of roux per liter of stock. Thicken or loosen from there once you know the plate.

**3. The long cook.** Add a sweated mirepoix and the tomato, as paste, purée, or concassé. Bring it up, skim, add the bouquet, and hold it at a bare bubble for at least 2 to 3 hours. The sauce reduces, the flour taste leaves, and the roast, the sweetness of the vegetables, and the acid of the tomato come into one.

**4. Strain and finish.** Pass it through a fine chinois, lift the fat, and salt only at the end, when the volume has stopped moving.

The texture is velvet. The color is deep mahogany.

## What comes off it

The sauce most kitchens actually ladle is [demi-glace](https://aichef.pro/en/blog/what-is-demi-glace): equal parts espagnole and brown stock, reduced by half. It is brighter and more concentrated, close to a syrup, and a spoonful is enough on meat.

| Sauce | What you add to the base | Where it goes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Demi-glace | Equal parts espagnole and brown stock, reduced by half | The working brown sauce |
| Bordelaise | Red wine reduced with shallot, herbs, and sometimes marrow, then demi-glace | Grilled steak, rib, sirloin |
| Chasseur | Mushrooms, shallot, and white wine | Sautéed meat |
| Madère | Madeira | Braises and sautéed meat |
| Poivrade | Pepper, vinegar, and a game garnish | Venison, boar, and other game |

Espagnole is rarely sent to the table as it comes from the stockpot. A splash of sherry, green peppercorns, or a spoon of whole-grain mustard turns the base into the sauce of the night without a second mother sauce. Its body also naps a Wellington or a duchesse potato that will go under the salamander.

## On the pass

This is the sauce for meat with a strong taste: a roast of beef, a fillet, an oxtail braise, venison, wild boar. It wraps the meat and stays on it. The gelatin from the brown stock is what makes it cling.

Season after the reduction. A sauce you will cut by half cannot take salt in the first pot. Game is where the roast in the stock does the most work: it carries the browned note and softens the iron taste.

If the card says velouté, you are in the wrong pot. Pale stock and a blond roux will not become espagnole because you cooked them longer.

## Mistakes

**A blond roux.** Color in the roux is flavor. A pale roux in a brown stock gives you a muddy sauce with no roasted flour note. Cook it to mahogany, and stop before black.

**No tomato.** The acid is part of the sauce, not a garnish. Without it the fat of the stock sits heavier and the color fades.

**A hard boil.** The long cook is a shiver, not a roll. A violent boil emulsifies the fat back in and clouds the sauce. Skim, and keep the bubble lazy.

**Salt at the start.** The sauce reduces for hours. Salt early and the last hour is a rescue. Salt when the volume has settled.

**Calling the stock the sauce.** Brown stock is the liquid. The roux, the mirepoix, the tomato, and the reduction are what make espagnole. A reduced stock is a glace or a jus. It is a different pot.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is it called Spanish sauce if it is not from Spain?**
There is no single agreed story. The usual account is that Carême named it for the tomato, an ingredient European kitchens first met through Spain after it came from the Americas. Others take the name as a nod to the Spanish court, in a period when the two courts traded cooks and dishes. Either way, the sauce was written down in French kitchens.

**Does espagnole need tomato?**
Yes. Tomato, as paste, purée, or concassé, is one of the marks that set Carême's sauce apart from other meat sauces. The acid balances the fat of the stock and helps hold the dark color. Leave it out and the sauce tastes flatter, and you are no longer making espagnole.

**What is the difference between espagnole and demi-glace?**
Espagnole is the mother sauce, finished after its long cook and ready to use. Demi-glace is a further reduction: equal parts espagnole and brown stock, cooked down by half. It is glossier, richer, and more intense, and a spoonful is enough on a piece of meat.

**How long does espagnole keep?**
Once it is cold and the fat is lifted, four to five days in a sealed container in the refrigerator. Frozen in portions, up to three months. Kitchens keep it as a working sauce so the pass can finish a plate without starting from stock.

**Can you make a similar sauce without gluten?**
Yes. Reduce the brown stock with the bones and vegetables until you have a concentrated glace de viande, or thicken with a dark roux made from rice flour. The texture will not match a wheat-flour espagnole. The roasted taste of the stock still carries the plate.
