<p>If you have wondered <strong>what fumet means</strong>, the plain answer is this: a concentrated cooking liquid. For fish, that liquid is the short white stock you build from clean bones and a few aromatics, started cold and finished while it still tastes clean, often inside half an hour. It is there to disappear into a sauce, a soup or a rice dish and leave the sea behind in the bite. The same word is also used for other concentrated cooking liquids, including ones made from meat or mushrooms. Next to a piece of fish, it means the short white stock. A soup you would drink is a separate recipe, with its own salt.</p>
<h2>What the word covers in the kitchen</h2>
<p>A fish fumet is a white stock. The frames are not roasted, the simmer stays gentle, and you stop while the flavour is still clean. In many American kitchens the same pot is simply called fish stock, and the useful versions of it are the short ones. If you want it stronger, strain, then reduce the liquid. Boiling it down does not change the name. A broth you would serve in a bowl, salted for drinking, is a different recipe: more water or less, a different pinch of salt, and a name on the menu.</p>

<h2>Which bones, and which aromatics</h2>
<p>Use lean white fish: sole, turbot, halibut, cod, snapper, bass, monkfish. Cut the frames so they fit, rinse off the blood, and pull the gills if the head is in the pot. Guts, scales and the dark bloodline cloud the stock and push it toward bitterness. Oily fish, such as mackerel, sardine and tuna, and salmon in a classic fumet, make a strong, fatty pot. A salmon fumet can be its own pot, simmered on the short side and labelled for salmon sauces. It does not replace the white one.</p>
<p>Keep the aromatics small, because the cook is short: onion or shallot, the white of a leek, a little celery, parsley stems, a bay leaf, a few peppercorns. Carrot is optional and easy to overdo if you want the stock sweet. A splash of dry white wine at the start is common and not compulsory. If you use wine, use one you would drink.</p>

<p>Those amounts give a stock with enough body for a velouté or a sauce you will reduce. If the bones are swimming, the result is thin, and a later reduction will not put the sea back.</p>
<h2>The simmer</h2>
<h3>1. Cold water, then the scum</h3>
<p>Bones and aromatics start covered, not swimming. As the pot warms, a grey foam rises. Lift it off before a hard boil beats it back into the liquid. A rolling boil leaves the fumet cloudy and tasting of overcooked protein. You want the surface barely moving.</p>
<h3>2. Taste it, then stop while it is clean</h3>
<p>From the moment the surface moves, start tasting. Twenty minutes already gives a useful stock, and about half an hour is a sensible place to stop if it still tastes clean. After that the bone starts to show, and you cannot cook the bitterness out. It should smell of clean sea, of wine if you used it, and of sweet vegetables, not of old bone. If you want it stronger, strain the frames out and reduce the liquid. Leaving the bones in so that it will "give more" is how the pot turns.</p>
<h3>3. Strain without squeezing, then cool</h3>
<p>A fine sieve, gravity, no pressing. Squeezing the bones puts the sediment back. Cool the fumet quickly, and do not lid it while it is hot. When you pull it for service, bring it to the boil for a few minutes and taste it again.</p>
<h2>Where it earns its place</h2>
<p>A fish sauce made on water tastes of the butter and the acid, and the fish on the plate sits alone. The same sauce on a short, clean fumet still tastes of the sea after you swallow. A fish velouté is this stock plus a pale roux. A mounted butter sauce can start here too, once you have reduced it. If you are judging body, the spoon test is the same one covered under <a href="https://aichef.pro/en/blog/nap-a-sauce">napping a sauce</a>.</p>
<p>In a seafood rice or a stew, the grain or the sauce drinks the liquid. Taste before it goes in the pan and salt it knowing it will reduce. A thin fumet is not rescued by the shellfish on top. A fumet salted as if it were soup becomes too salty the moment it reduces. Sending the pot out as a bowl, without that adjustment, changes both the strength and the salt. If the fumet becomes a dish, that is a second recipe.</p>
<p>Shells are a separate pot. Shrimp, langoustine or crab shells, sweated for a moment without scorching, take the same short simmer. That liquid is a shellfish fumet. It belongs with a seafood rice or a bisque. Mixed into the white-bone pot, tomorrow's sole inherits a stock that does not match the fish.</p>
<p>It is also not a <a href="https://aichef.pro/en/blog/glace-de-viande">glace de viande</a>. A meat glaze is a dark stock reduced until it sets hard when cold. Roasting the frames and then reducing for hours is how a fish pot goes bitter. A <a href="https://aichef.pro/en/blog/what-is-demi-glace">demi-glace</a> belongs with meat for the same reason.</p>
<h2>Mistakes that waste the bones</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Gills, blood and guts:</strong> they make the pot bitter and cloudy. Clean them off before the water is hot. You cannot skim that bitterness out later.</li>
<li><strong>A hard boil:</strong> it beats the fat and the scum back into the liquid, and the fumet turns grey. Keep the surface barely moving.</li>
<li><strong>Past half an hour, just in case:</strong> the clean flavour leaves and the bone stays in the taste. Strain and reduce the liquid if you need more body, with the frames already out.</li>
<li><strong>Roasting the frames like a brown stock:</strong> the colour tastes of burnt skin. Leave the fish bones pale and start them in cold water.</li>
<li><strong>Pressing the sieve:</strong> you put back the sediment you skimmed. Let the liquid fall on its own.</li>
<li><strong>Salting it like a soup:</strong> if you then reduce it in a sauce, the dish comes out too salty. Salt the finished dish, or salt the fumet very lightly and taste it in the pan it is going into.</li>
</ul>

<p>The bones are already paid for in the fish you bought. Throwing them away throws away the base for tomorrow's sauce. Keeping them bloody, with the gills still in, makes a fumet you will throw away anyway. Note which fish the pot came from, because that flavour travels into the sauce and the rice.</p>
