Velouté is a mother sauce made from white stock and a blond roux. The stock is poultry, veal, or fish, started pale and never roasted. The roux is cooked only to a light sand color. The finished sauce is ivory, fluid, and silky, and it carries the taste of the stock. It is not béchamel, which is built on milk, and it is not espagnole, which starts from brown stock.

## What velouté is

In the classical French set that Escoffier kept from Carême, velouté is the pale mother sauce. Béchamel takes milk. [Espagnole](https://aichef.pro/en/blog/what-is-espagnole) takes [brown stock](https://aichef.pro/en/blog/what-is-brown-stock) and a dark roux. Velouté takes a [white stock](https://aichef.pro/en/blog/what-is-white-stock) and a [roux](https://aichef.pro/en/blog/what-is-a-roux) stopped at blond, the *roux blond*: cooked past the raw flour taste, still pale.

The name comes from *velours*, velvet. That is the mouthfeel you are after. No raw flour, no fat sitting on top, no milk to hide a weak stock. If the stock is dull, the sauce is dull. The roux only gives body.

On a classical prep list the French name is velouté. English kitchens keep that word. Spanish cards call it salsa velouté.

## How it is made

For one liter, two things and nothing else.

**The blond roux.** 50 g clarified butter and 50 g soft wheat flour. Cook over medium heat, stirring, until the raw flour taste is gone and the color is pale sand, about 3 to 4 minutes. If it browns, it is no longer a velouté roux. Set it aside still warm.

**The stock.** 1 liter of white stock, poultry, fish, or white veal, hot and fully degreased. It should be as clean as a consommé. Anything cloudy in the stock shows up in the sauce.

Whisk a third of the hot stock into the warm roux until it is smooth, then the rest. Bring it to a gentle boil, drop the heat to a shiver, and cook 20 to 30 minutes, skimming fat and scum as they rise. The sauce should coat the back of a spoon lightly. It should not sit as thick as a béchamel. Too thick: lengthen with hot stock. Too thin: give it a few more minutes. Strain through a fine chinois. Hold it over a water bath. A few cubes of cold butter on the surface stop a skin while it waits.

## Ratios by use

| Use | Flour per liter of stock | Simmer | Texture |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sauce on the plate | 40–50 g | 20–25 min | Light, coats a spoon |
| Base for other sauces | 50–60 g | 25–30 min | Medium, napping |
| Croquette liaison | 70–80 g | 30 min | Thick, pulls from the pan |
| Gratin | 50 g, plus yolks | 20 min | Holds in the oven |

Soft flour, not bread flour. Extra flour, or a hard boil, is how a light sauce turns pasty.

## Sauces that start here

**Allemande.** Velouté enriched with egg yolks and cream, the liaison. Reduce the velouté, temper the yolks and cream, and warm the sauce without letting it boil, or the yolks set. It goes with white meat and poached eggs, and many kitchens use it under fish that will be glazed.

**Suprême.** The poultry version cooks hear most often. Reduce the velouté with a little concentrated poultry stock and finish with cold butter and a touch of cream. Ivory, glossy, and clearly chicken. It is the sauce on a poached breast, a filled suprême, and a vol-au-vent. A proper blanquette of poultry is this family.

**White wine sauce.** Start from a fish velouté. Reduce dry white wine with shallot, often with a little concentrated fumet, and whisk in cold butter at the end for gloss. It is the sauce for poached sole, turbot, and delicate shellfish. Parsley goes in at the last moment, if you want it.

A few more that earn a place on the prep list:

- Mushrooms: velouté, the cooking juices of sautéed mushrooms, and cream. White meat and fresh pasta.
- Mustard: velouté, Dijon, and butter. Quick, and it suits pork and small game birds.
- Capers: fish velouté, drained capers, and a splash of their vinegar. Fried fish and battered vegetables.
- Dill and lemon: fish velouté, lemon zest, chopped dill, and cream. Salmon and trout.

## On the pass

Velouté is a sauce in its own right, not only a parent. Over poached poultry, steamed fish, or boiled vegetables it coats without covering the ingredient. For a gratin, enrich it with cheese and yolk and color it under the salamander. In a croquette, a timbale, or a fish cake it binds and stays moist. In a cream soup, replacing part of the liquid with velouté gives body without a pile of potato or cream.

If the spec says velouté, the stock is unsalted and pale, and the liaison is a separate step. If the spec says [espagnole](https://aichef.pro/en/blog/what-is-espagnole) or a pepper sauce, you wanted brown stock. Reducing a white sauce will not invent a roast you never made.

## Mistakes

**Lumps.** The stock was cold against a hot roux, or the other way around. Keep the stock hot and the roux freshly made, and add the liquid in stages while you whisk.

**Raw flour.** Those 3 to 4 minutes on the roux are the whole point. Pull it early and the sauce tastes of paste, however long you simmer it afterwards.

**Heavy or sandy.** Bread flour, or more than the flour the use asks for, stiffens the sauce. For a sauce on the plate, stay near 50 g of soft flour per liter.

**Fat on the surface.** A stock that was not degreased, or a rolling boil, breaks the sauce. Skim, and keep the simmer gentle.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between velouté and béchamel?**
The liquid. Béchamel is milk, often infused with onion, clove, and bay, thickened with a white roux that is barely cooked. Velouté is white stock from poultry, fish, or veal, thickened with a blond roux cooked a little further, just to a pale sand color. Béchamel tastes of milk and sits thicker. Velouté tastes of the stock and stays lighter.

**Can you make velouté without gluten?**
Yes. Skip the wheat flour. Stir cornstarch or rice flour into a little cold stock, then whisk that slurry into the hot stock and cook until it thickens. That is not a roux. The sauce comes out glossier and a little less silky, and it still works for a gluten-free service.

**How long does velouté keep?**
Covered with film pressed onto the surface, three to four days in the refrigerator. Frozen in portions, up to two months. Thaw it gently over a water bath and whisk. It often looks broken as it warms. A little fresh stock and low heat bring it back.

**Can vegetable stock make a velouté?**
The classical sauce asks for an animal stock. A well-reduced vegetable stock, with leek, celery, roasted onion, and a strip of kombu for depth, gives a useful vegetarian base. The roux and the simmer stay the same. Only the stock changes.

**Why does my velouté taste thin when the stock was good?**
It came off the heat as soon as it thickened. The roux needs the simmer to finish thickening, and the stock needs that same time to concentrate. Keep it at a bare simmer, skim, and salt only at the end, once the volume has settled.
