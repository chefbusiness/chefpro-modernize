To nap a sauce means to coat food with a sauce thick enough to stay on it instead of running into a puddle. That consistency is called nappé. Escoffier considers glace de viande finished when it coats a spoon in a glossy, clinging layer: "qu'elle nappe la cuiller." In practice, you draw a finger across the back of the spoon. If the line stays clean, the sauce naps.

## What it means to nap a sauce

The word comes from the French napper, to coat. American cooks say a sauce "coats the back of a spoon," naps, or is nappé. It is how you check whether a sauce has the right body, whether it is a [demi-glace](https://aichef.pro/en/blog/what-is-demi-glace), a velouté, or a reduced jus. Pastry cooks use the same test: crème anglaise is done when it coats the spoon, at around 180 to 183°F (82 to 84°C).

To check, dip a metal spoon in the sauce, lift it out, and look at the back. If the sauce is thin, it runs off and leaves the metal bare. If it is right, it covers the spoon in an even layer. Draw a finger across the back: if the line holds and the sauce does not flow back over it right away, it is ready. If it is so thick it looks like a paste, you have gone too far and need to thin it.

Escoffier uses the same test, without the finger, for [glace de viande](https://aichef.pro/en/blog/glace-de-viande) and for sauce allemande, which should coat the spatula well. Drawing a finger through it is simply the easiest way to see whether that layer holds.

## Where the word comes from

Napper is a French kitchen term, and nappé is the adjective for that consistency. Escoffier did not invent the word, but in Le Guide Culinaire (1903) he uses it to say when a preparation is finished: glace de viande is ready when it naps the spoon, not after a set time. For demi-glace, the same test tells you whether the reduction has the body of a sauce yet.

Do not confuse nappé with shine, which often comes from butter added at the end. A sauce can be glossy and still thin. That is why you look at the back of the spoon first, and then taste.

## How to check it

There is no table of grams that fits every sauce. The body comes from different places.

| How it gained body | What to look for on the spoon |
| --- | --- |
| Reduction or glaze | A shining coat, slow, that clings |
| [Roux](https://aichef.pro/en/blog/what-is-a-roux), in a béchamel or a velouté | A smooth coat, a little dull if there is no butter at the end |
| Starch slurry | It coats as soon as it has simmered a minute, and it can look more opaque |
| Yolk and cream | A silky coat. If it boils, the grain shows that the yolk has set |

**1. Take a clean spoon.** You need to see the back. A spoon already filmed with old sauce will fool you.

**2. Dip it and lift it.** Count a couple of seconds. If a thin ribbon falls and the metal is left bare, keep cooking, or thicken it.

**3. Draw a finger through it.** If the line stays clean, the sauce naps. If it closes right away, the sauce is still too thin. If no line forms because the sauce is a paste, thin it.

**4. Taste.** A sauce can nap and still be salty, bitter, or taste of raw flour. Always taste it before serving.

## Why the test fails

The sauce runs off because it needs more reduction or more thickener. It can also run on a very hot plate even if it was fine in the pan. If you plate on very hot plates, leave the sauce slightly thicker.

If it is too thick, it was reduced too far or got too much roux or starch. Fix it by adding hot liquid a spoonful at a time.

An oily shine, with a rim of free fat, shows butter that has separated. Emulsify it again, off the boil, before you judge the body.

## During service

Check during prep, and again when you sauce the plate. A sauce that waited over a water bath thickens as it cools a little and looks as if it naps, then runs again when you heat it fully. Judge it at the temperature it will leave at.

If you need to correct it during service, do it in the pan with a small amount and repeat the spoon test. Do not add thickener directly on the plate, because it will form lumps.

On the plate, a sauce that naps stays on the meat or vegetables without spreading. If it runs to the rim, it was too thin or you used too much.

## Ways to give a sauce body

**By reduction.** This is the path of a jus or a glaze. It takes longer, and it concentrates the flavor, including the salt.

**By roux.** This is the path of béchamel, velouté, and espagnole. The thickener goes in at the start.

**With beurre manié or a starch slurry.** These go in at the end, when the sauce is already made and there is no time to reduce it further.

**With yolk.** It gives a fine body and a richer flavor. If the sauce has no flour in it, it cannot boil afterward.

Each method gives a slightly different texture, but the spoon test works for all of them.

## What the point is for

It tells you whether a sauce is ready without relying on anyone's opinion. It is also useful in written recipes: "reduce until it coats a spoon" is much more precise than "reduce for a while," and any cook can repeat the test.

It applies mainly to hot sauces and to some thick cold ones, such as mayonnaise or crème anglaise. A vinaigrette does not need to nap; it only needs to dress.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is nappé the name of a sauce?**
No. Nappé describes how thick a sauce is, not a particular sauce. A demi-glace, a velouté, or a jus can all be nappé, and each tastes different.

**How do you check it without a thermometer or a gadget?**
With a spoon. Dip it in the sauce, lift it out, and look at the back: it should be coated. Draw a finger across it. If the line holds and the sauce does not close it right away, it is ready. If the sauce runs off and leaves the metal bare, it is too thin.

**Does a shining sauce always nap?**
No. Shine and body are different things. A sauce mounted with butter can be glossy and still run across the plate, and a starch-thickened sauce can nap and look matte. Check whether the sauce stays on the spoon.

**What do you do if it runs off?**
If you have time, reduce it over gentle heat. If you do not, thicken it with whatever belongs to that sauce: a little roux if you are still early, beurre manié or a starch slurry if the sauce is already made. Then do the spoon test again.

**What do you do if it is too thick?**
Whisk in hot stock or water a spoonful at a time and check again. A sauce that is too thick does not coat the meat well; it just sits in a heap on top.

**How do you pronounce nappé?**
Roughly nap-AY, with the stress on the second syllable. To nap is the verb cooks use in English. The French verb is napper.
