<h2>What it is, on the line</h2>
<p>To nap a sauce is to see whether it will cling. You dip a spoon, lift it, and look at the back. Nappé means the sauce coats that surface instead of running off in a clear stream. It is a test of body, taken in the pot before the plate leaves the pass. It is not a named sauce and it is not a style of cooking. A velouté, a jus and a glaze can all be nappé or not, depending on where the reduction has got to. You are judging the liquid in front of you, nothing else.</p>
<p>Cooks write nappe, nappé and nap a sauce for the same check, and the spelling changes nothing in the pan. If the spoon comes out almost bare, the sauce is not ready to hold on meat or fish. If it hangs in a thick blanket that will not level, you have gone past a coat and into a paste. Draw a finger through the coat and the line should stay. The head chef wants the middle: a film that remains, still glossy, still tasting of the reduction rather than of starch. Learn that look before the rush teaches it to you badly.</p>

<h2>How it behaves on the pass</h2>
<p>On the pass, nappé is a language between stations. The saucier says the pan is ready, and the chef de partie trusts that the spoon was dipped, not guessed. A sauce that looked right in a deep pot can look thin once it spreads on a hot plate, so you look again when the dish is meant to hold a coat. You are not renaming the sauce. You are saying it has enough body to stay with the food on the way to the guest. A loose sauce that floods the rim fails the plate even when the flavour is good.</p>
<p>Mise en place is where you learn the look, not during the rush. Reduce a ladle of the sauce you will serve and watch the coat build. A reduction and a good roux keep a shine that a starch slurry knocks back, so the way you thickened the sauce changes what nappé looks like. The margin sits in that choice. Chasing body only with slurry is dull. Chasing it only with more reduction costs time and product. The head chef would rather you know which path the dish was written for than invent a fix that changes the sauce the guest ordered.</p>
<p>The guest reads the coat without the word. Sauce that clings to a slice, that stays on a piece of fish, that sits in a ribbon and does not bleed to the edge, feels finished. Sauce that pools like a broth feels careless, even when it tastes right. Your job is to send the body the dish was meant to have. If the spoon test fails, you do not argue with it. You reduce a little more, or you correct with the thickener the house allows, and you test again before the plate goes up. Looking is part of seasoning.</p>

<h2>How people search for it</h2>
<p>People search nappe, nappé and nap a sauce when they want the spoon test, not a recipe with its own title. You will also see nap the spoon, coat the back of a spoon, and nappe consistency typed into the same question. On a British and an American pass the word is the same gesture. Use it when you need to say a jus, a velouté or a glaze has enough body to cling, and when you need to say it is not the name of the sauce.</p>
