To nap a sauce is to coat food with a sauce thick enough to stay on the food. That consistency is called nappé. Escoffier calls glace de viande finished when the sauce wraps a spoon in a shining, clinging coat. His French line is qu'elle nappe la cuiller. In practice, you draw a finger across the back of that spoon. If the path stays clean, the sauce is nappé.

## What it means to nap a sauce

The French verb is napper, to coat. In English kitchens the verb is to nap, and the consistency is nappé. It is a test of body, the same test for a [demi-glace](https://aichef.pro/en/blog/what-is-demi-glace), a velouté, or a jus you have already reduced.

The test fits on a metal spoon. You dip it, lift it, and look at the back. A thin sauce runs off and leaves the metal bare. A sauce at the right point covers it in an even coat. If you draw a finger through and the sauce does not flood the path at once, you can serve it. If the finger opens a trench in a paste that does not move, you have gone too far. That does not coat. It sits in a heap.

Escoffier uses the same idea, without the finger, for [glace de viande](https://aichef.pro/en/blog/glace-de-viande) and for sauce allemande, which should coat the spatula well. The finger is the practical way to see whether that coat holds. He does not describe the finger line.

## Where the word comes from

Napper has been kitchen French for a long time, and nappé is the adjective for the consistency. Escoffier does not invent the word. He uses it as the sign that the cooking is finished. The glaze is done when it naps the spoon. A count of minutes does not decide it. With demi-glace, the same test decides whether the reduction is already a sauce or still a dark broth.

The shine, which people sometimes confuse with this point, often comes from the butter at the end. A sauce can shine and still be thin. That is why you look at the back of the spoon, and then you taste.

## How to check it

There is no table of grams that fits every sauce. The body comes from different places.

| How it gained body | What to look for on the spoon |
| --- | --- |
| Reduction or glaze | A shining coat, slow, that clings |
| [Roux](https://aichef.pro/en/blog/what-is-a-roux), in a béchamel or a velouté | A smooth coat, a little dull if there is no butter at the end |
| Starch slurry | It coats as soon as it has simmered a minute, and it can look more opaque |
| Yolk and cream | A silky coat. If it boils, the grain shows that the yolk has set |

<figure><img src="/blog-assets/2026/05/demi-glace-cuerpo.jpg" alt="A saucier holding a spoon over a wide pan on the pass, checking whether the sauce clings before a plate is wiped." loading="lazy" style="width:100%;height:auto;border-radius:8px;margin:24px 0;" /></figure>

**1. Take a clean spoon.** You need to see the back. A spoon already filmed with old sauce will fool you.

**2. Dip it and lift it.** Count a couple of seconds. If a thin ribbon falls and the metal is left bare, keep cooking, or thicken it.

**3. Draw a finger through it.** A clean path is the yes. If it closes at once, the sauce is still too loose to nap. If there is no path because the sauce is a paste, loosen it.

**4. Taste.** A sauce can nap and still be salty, bitter, or raw with flour. The spoon tells you the body. Your mouth tells you whether to serve it.

## Why the test fails

The sauce runs off because it needs more reduction or more thickener, or because the plate is very hot and loosens it on contact. A sauce that was just right in the pan can run on a very hot plate. Allow for that, and leave the consistency a little firmer if the plate comes off the heat.

The sauce will not flow because it reduced too far, because the roux was too much for the liquid, or because the slurry went in without a measure. Correct it with hot liquid, by the spoonful, not all at once.

An oily shine, with a rim of free fat, shows butter that has separated. Emulsify it again, off the boil, before you judge the body.

## During service

Check during prep, and again when you sauce the plate. A sauce that waited over a water bath thickens as it cools a little and looks as if it naps, then runs again when you heat it fully. Judge it at the temperature it will leave at.

If you have to correct it while someone is waiting, do it in the pan, with a small amount, and repeat the spoon test. Thickener poured blindly onto the plate leaves lumps.

On the plate, a sauce that naps stays on the meat or the vegetable in a ribbon. It does not need a dam of puree to keep it from escaping. If it reaches the rim, it was thin, or there was too much of it.

## Different ways to the same body

**By reduction.** This is the path of a jus or a glaze. It takes longer, and it concentrates the flavor, including the salt.

**By roux.** This is the path of béchamel, velouté, and espagnole. The thickener goes in at the start.

**By beurre manié or by slurry.** These are the late repairs, when the sauce is already made and there is no time to reduce further.

**By yolk.** It gives a fine body and a richer taste, and it means you do not boil the sauce afterward.

Each one naps in a slightly different way. The test is the same.

## What the point is for

It tells you whether the sauce is ready to go out, without an argument about whether it "looks thick." It also belongs on a recipe card. "Reduce until nappé" says more than "reduce for a while." The next person can make the same test.

A vinaigrette should moisten the leaves. Napping belongs to the hot sauces of a plate, and to some thick cold sauces, such as a mayonnaise that wraps the food.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is nappé a sauce you order from the menu?**
Nappé names the body. A demi-glace, a velouté, or a jus can all nap, and the three taste different. On the menu you order the sauce. The spoon only tells you whether it already has body.

**How do you check it without a thermometer or a gadget?**
With a spoon. Dip it, lift it, and look at the back. It should stay coated. Draw a finger through it: the clean path should hold, and the sauce should not close over it at once. If it runs off and leaves bare metal, it is thin.

**Does a shining sauce always nap?**
Shine and body are two different things. Mounted butter can shine and still run across the plate. A thick starch sauce can nap and look dull. Look at whether it stays, and then taste it.

**What do you do if it runs off?**
If you have time, reduce it over gentle heat. If you do not, thicken it with whatever belongs to that sauce: a little roux if you are still early, beurre manié or a starch slurry if the sauce is already made. Then do the spoon test again.

**What do you do if it is too thick?**
Add hot stock or water, by the spoonful, whisking, and test again. A sauce that will not flow sits in a lump on the meat and does not coat it. Nappé flows, and it stays.

**How do you pronounce nappé?**
Roughly nap-AY, with the stress on the second syllable. To nap is the verb cooks use in English. The French verb is napper.
