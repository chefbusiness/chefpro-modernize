You hand an agent what you already know — your formula, your flour prices, last week's sales, the room temperature — and it comes back with a formula sheet, a cost per loaf, a par-level draft, a waste plan or an allergen list you can check, correct and put to work. The oven, the levain and the final call stay with you.

## 1. Formulas and fermentation: the baker's judgment, with a faster draft

Baker's percentages are already how you think, and an agent can hold your formula sheet and redraft it faster than you can rebuild a spreadsheet. Give Creative Bakery a base formula — 75% hydration, 20% levain, 2% salt, for example — and ask for it as a clean sheet with the total flour at 100%, so every line can be scaled and checked against what you know about your flour. From that same sheet you can ask for a batch card for the crew, a costing line per loaf, or a starting point for a new bread.

Redrafting for another batch size is where the time goes. Say you have been baking 30 loaves at 1 lb 2 oz and an account wants 45 at 1 lb 5 oz. Calcula Pax redrafts the quantities — flour, water, levain, salt — in one pass, and you take it from there. It does not know your proofer, your retarder, how your flour drinks water in January, or how warm the bench is at four in the morning. Bulk fermentation and final proof are yours to adjust, always.

Seasonal shifts are the same conversation. Tell the agent the room temperature and the forecast, and ask for starting points: water temperature, bulk time, whether to shorten the retard. Treat the answer as a starting point, not a rule. You will still put your hand on the dough and know.

The production schedule is the other place a draft saves an hour. Ask for a timeline that runs from levain build through mix, bulk, divide, preshape, bench rest, shape, retard and bake, built around your oven capacity and how many decks you can load at once. You will move the blocks around; the blank page is what you are avoiding.

### AI baking recipe generator

Ask Creative Bakery for a new loaf or pastry built from the flours you actually stock and the inclusions you already have — a rye with toasted sunflower, a laminated pastry with quince paste, a sandwich loaf that holds up to a day on the shelf. Ask for it in baker's percentages, with a costing line, so you can see the flour cost per unit before you commit a day to testing it. Then bake it, taste it, and change it. The formula is a draft; the bench is the test.

## 2. Cost per loaf: the real margin of each bread

Many bakeries price by feel or by a multiplier on flour, and the number that decides the month is cost per loaf with labor inside it. Here is a worked example with example numbers, not benchmarks, for a sourdough loaf scaled at about 2 lb of dough (about 1.7 lb baked).

| Item | Per loaf | Unit cost | Cost per loaf |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Bread flour | 1.0 lb | $0.60/lb | $0.60 |
| Water | — | — | $0.01 |
| Levain (stiff, 50% hydration) | 0.2 lb | $0.40/lb (flour cost) | $0.08 |
| Salt | 0.02 lb | $0.50/lb | $0.01 |
| Oven and proofer energy | 1.2 kWh | $0.17/kWh | $0.20 |
| Direct labor (mix, shape, bake) | 0.12 h | $18.00/h | $2.16 |
| Bag | 1 | $0.12 | $0.12 |
| Total cost per loaf | | | $3.18 |
| Retail price | | | $8.00 |
| Gross margin | | | $4.82 (about 60%) |

Look at the two big lines. Labor is $2.16 and flour is $0.60 — labor weighs far more than flour, and it is easy to price as if the opposite were true. At 100 loaves a day that loaf structure is $482 of gross margin, and shaving 0.02 h of labor per loaf is $0.36 a loaf, $36 a day. That is a shape change on the bench, a second bench scraper, a different loading order — not a new flour.

## 3. Production planning: bake to demand

Bake to demand means starting from what you actually sold, not what you hope to sell, and an agent can turn a messy sales record into a par-level draft. Paste your sales by day — a notebook photo typed out is fine, a POS export is better — with notes on weather, holidays, school breaks and local events, and ask for par levels per product for next week. You get a draft to argue with instead of a blank page.

Be clear about what the agent can see: it does not see your POS, your inventory or your invoices, and it does not fetch the forecast. Everything it knows is what you type or paste. So give it the week you remember and the forecast you heard on the radio, and let it propose the numbers. Then you walk the case, look at the day-old rack and decide.

## 4. Waste and second lives

Waste happens at every step of the process, and each point has its own kind of loss and its own fix. The agent will not stop a bowl from being scraped badly, but it will give you a routine and a set of second-life options with the allergens tracked.

## 5. Allergens and labels

Wheat is in almost everything you bake, and the allergens that catch bakers are the ones hiding in toppings, washes and fillings: sesame, milk, eggs, tree nuts and soy. The US list of nine major allergens — milk, eggs, fish, crustacean shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybeans and sesame — is the frame to check every product against, including the ones you think are plain.

Ask Allergen ID for the direct and hidden allergens in each product and for cross-contact risks on shared equipment — the same bench, the same mixer, the same screens. Then check your supplier labels, because a substitution you made last month may have changed the answer. The free [allergen detector](https://aichef.pro/en/restaurant-allergen-detector) on the site is a fast first pass; it checks the EU list of 14, which includes the US nine.

## 6. Local marketing: the bakery on the block and on the phone

Your customers are within a few blocks and on their phones at six in the morning, and the posts that sell bread are the ones written the night before. Product descriptions, a social post for the morning bake, a Google Business Profile update when the hours change or the seasonal special lands — these are short jobs that eat an hour you do not have after a night shift.

Use the marketing agents to draft them from what you actually baked: the rye, the seeded boule, the Friday-only babka. You approve every post before it goes out, and you keep the voice. Nobody knows your corner better than you do.

## How to start this week

1. Pick one bread — your best seller — and cost it line by line, labor included.
2. Write its formula sheet with Creative Bakery, in baker's percentages, and check it against the bench.
3. Paste two weeks of sales and ask for par levels for next week; compare them with what you would have baked.
4. Log waste for a week: what, when, and why. Then ask for second-life options.
5. Schedule three posts for the week and approve them yourself.
