An emulsion is a mixture of two liquids that do not dissolve in each other, such as oil and water, broken into droplets so fine that they look like one liquid. Those droplets need an emulsifier or they join up again. In the kitchen, the strongest everyday emulsifier is egg yolk. Mayonnaise, hollandaise, beurre blanc, and vinaigrette are all emulsions, and they do not share the same patience or the same heat.

## What an emulsion is

Oil and water separate as soon as you stop stirring. An emulsifier sits on the edge of each droplet and keeps the droplets from merging. Egg yolk does this with lecithin and with its proteins. Mustard helps too, especially in a vinaigrette, because it brings mucilage, a sticky substance that also steadies the mix.

Some emulsions can wait. Others have to be served as you finish them.

| Sauce | What holds it | How it behaves |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mayonnaise | Yolk, oil, and an acid, made cold | The most stable of the four. Well made, it keeps for several days in the refrigerator |
| Hollandaise and béarnaise | Yolk and butter, made warm | Serve them warm. A hard boil, or too much heat, separates them |
| [Beurre blanc](https://aichef.pro/en/blog/beurre-blanc) | Butter and a reduction, with no yolk | The most fragile. You mount it shortly before you serve |
| Vinaigrette | Oil and vinegar, sometimes mustard | It separates as it sits. Whisk it again and it comes back |

In the first three, the fat sits as droplets inside the water. That is an oil-in-water emulsion. Butter on bread is the other way around: water dispersed in fat. For saucing, the first kind is the one you are making.

## Where it comes from

The word comes from the Latin *emulgere*, to milk, because milk is a natural emulsion. In the kitchen, the written piece worth keeping is Auguste Escoffier's mayonnaise, in Le Guide Culinaire.

For six yolks he asks for half an ounce of salt, one-fifth of a pint of vinegar, about 1 quart (roughly 1 liter) of oil added in a thread, the juice of one lemon, and three tablespoons of boiling water at the end. The boiling water, he says, helps the sauce hold together. He also leaves a useful warning. Each egg can hold about 2 1/2 oz of oil if the sauce has to wait, and 3 oz if you use it at once. His recipe is built on yolks, so that limit is the one to use per yolk. Going past it is one reason the sauce breaks.

Dissolved salt helps the yolks hold the oil, so he seasons at the start.

## Ingredients and proportions

For two yolks, using the limit he gives when the sauce has to wait:

| Ingredient | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Egg yolks | 2 |
| Salt | 5 g (about 1 teaspoon) |
| Vinegar | 40 ml (about 2 1/2 tablespoons) |
| Oil, at kitchen temperature | 140 g (about 5 oz) if it will wait; up to 170 g (about 6 oz) if you serve it at once |
| Lemon juice | at the end, to taste |
| Boiling water | 15 ml (1 tablespoon), at the end |

Those amounts come from his text, shared between two yolks: the salt and vinegar from the six-yolk recipe, and the cap of about 70 g or 85 g of oil per yolk. The large batch he also writes, about a quart of oil for six yolks, goes past that cap. He says that mayonnaise is rather liquid at first and thickens as it stands.

## How to make it, step by step

**1. Whisk the yolks with the salt and the vinegar.** Use a bowl that is not ice-cold. The vinegar has to be absorbed before the oil goes in. Escoffier is firm about that order. If the oil arrives before the vinegar is taken up, the sauce starts badly.

**2. Add the oil in a thread.** Keep whisking. The first spoonfuls go slowly. Once the sauce has body, you can go a little faster. If you ease off and see free oil at the edge, stop and whisk until it comes back together.

**3. Finish.** Lemon juice, a taste for salt, and the 15 ml of boiling water, whisked in. The water loosens the sauce a little and, he says, helps it from turning. Cover it.

## Why it breaks, and how to fix it

Escoffier lists three causes. The oil went in too fast at the start. The oil was cold or congealed. Or there was more oil than the yolks could hold.

To bring it back, put a fresh yolk in a clean bowl with a few drops of vinegar, whisk it, and add the broken sauce a little at a time, as if it were the oil. If only a small amount has broken, half a coffee spoon of mustard can do the work of that yolk.

Do not make it over ice. He says it plainly: cold harms mayonnaise, and in winter it is a common reason the sauce breaks. If the oil comes from the refrigerator, let it come to kitchen temperature.

## During service

Mayonnaise is made during prep and kept covered in the refrigerator. Take it out a little ahead of time if the cold has left it very stiff, and whisk it. Do not leave it out for hours.

Hollandaise and beurre blanc do not wait the same way. You mount them close to service and hold them warm over a water bath, short of a boil. You whisk a vinaigrette when you use it. Treating all four as if they were the same sauce is the fastest way to serve a broken one.

## Variations

**Hollandaise.** Escoffier reduces vinegar and water with salt and pepper, adds 5 yolks, and mounts the sauce with 500 g of butter (about 1 pound 2 ounces) over gentle heat, with a little water along the way to lighten it. It is a warm emulsion. If it gets too hot, the yolk sets or the butter separates. Béarnaise follows the same path, with a reduction of shallot, tarragon, and vinegar. He compares it to a butter mayonnaise and says it only needs to be warm.

**Beurre blanc.** A reduction of shallot, white wine, and vinegar, mounted with cold butter, with no yolk. That is why it breaks more easily than hollandaise.

**Without egg.** Mustard alone can hold a vinaigrette, and, in a small amount, a mayonnaise that has already broken. A thick mayonnaise with no yolk needs another emulsifier, such as the liquid from cooked chickpeas. That is its own recipe, with its own ratio.

**With olive oil.** It works if the oil is at kitchen temperature and is not so bitter that it covers everything else. Many kitchens blend a neutral oil with a little virgin olive oil at the end, so the aroma is there without the sharp edge.

## What you use it for

Mayonnaise, with cold fish, eggs, vegetables, and as the base of other cold sauces. Hollandaise, with fish, asparagus, and eggs. Beurre blanc, with fish and vegetables. Vinaigrette, for dressing. An [egg-yolk liaison](https://aichef.pro/en/blog/egg-yolk-liaison) is a close cousin. It also uses the yolk, and it thickens a hot sauce you already have. Mayonnaise is built from the yolk outward.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does an emulsion break?**
Because the droplets of fat find each other again and the liquid separates. In mayonnaise, Escoffier names three causes: the oil went in too fast at the start, the oil was cold or congealed, or there was more oil than the yolks could hold.

**How much oil can one yolk hold?**
Escoffier sets the limit at 2 1/2 oz per egg, about 70 g, if the sauce has to wait, and at 3 oz, about 85 g, if you use it right away. His mayonnaise is built on yolks, so use that limit for each yolk. His large recipe, about 1 quart (roughly 1 liter) of oil for six yolks, is looser, and he says it is rather liquid at first.

**Should you make mayonnaise over ice?**
Escoffier says the opposite. Cold spoils mayonnaise, and in winter it is a common reason the sauce breaks. The oil should be at kitchen temperature. If it is cold, warm it slightly.

**Is mustard required?**
In his mayonnaise, mustard is optional. Yolks, vinegar, salt, and oil are enough. Mustard helps, and he uses it as a rescue for a small broken batch: half a coffee spoon of mustard can take the place of a fresh yolk.

**Can you use extra-virgin olive oil?**
Yes, if the oil is not cold. A very green, peppery oil takes over the sauce and can taste bitter. While you are learning the consistency, a mild oil, or a blend, behaves better. A mild olive oil works too.

**How long does homemade mayonnaise keep?**
Escoffier writes that, well made and covered, it keeps for several days. Today, refrigerate it and use it within those days. If the cold leaves it very stiff, let it warm slightly and whisk it before you decide it has broken.
