An emulsion is a mixture of two liquids that do not dissolve in each other, such as oil and water, in which one is spread through the other in droplets so fine that they look like a single liquid. To keep those droplets from joining up again, you need an emulsifier, and in the kitchen the most effective one is egg yolk. Mayonnaise, hollandaise, beurre blanc, and vinaigrette are all emulsions, but each one stands up differently to time and heat.

## What an emulsion is

Oil and water separate as soon as you stop stirring. An emulsifier sits on the edge of each droplet and keeps the droplets from merging. Egg yolk does this with lecithin and with its proteins. Mustard helps too, especially in a vinaigrette, because it brings mucilage, a sticky substance that also steadies the mix.

Some emulsions can wait. Others have to be served as you finish them.

| Sauce | What holds it | How it behaves |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mayonnaise | Yolk, oil, and an acid, made cold | The most stable. Made with raw yolks, keep it refrigerated and use it within 24 hours |
| Hollandaise and béarnaise | Yolk and butter, made warm | Serve them warm. A hard boil, or too much heat, separates them |
| [Beurre blanc](https://aichef.pro/en/blog/beurre-blanc) | Butter and a reduction, with no yolk | The most fragile. You mount it shortly before you serve |
| Vinaigrette | Oil and vinegar, sometimes mustard | It separates as it sits. Whisk it again and it comes back |

In the first three, the fat is spread as droplets through the watery part. That is called an oil-in-water emulsion. Butter is the opposite: droplets of water spread through fat. Emulsified sauces are almost always the first kind.

## Where it comes from

The word comes from the Latin *emulgere*, to milk, because milk is a natural emulsion. Mayonnaise is the best example for understanding how an emulsion works, and Auguste Escoffier explains it in detail in the English edition of his book, A Guide to Modern Cookery (1907).

For six yolks, he calls for half an ounce of salt, one-fifth of a pint of vinegar, a quart of oil added in a thin stream, the juice of one lemon, and, at the end, three tablespoons of boiling water, which he says keep the sauce from breaking. He also sets a limit: each yolk can absorb about 2 1/2 oz (70 g) of oil if the sauce will be kept, and about 3 oz (85 g) if it will be used right away. According to Escoffier, going past that amount is one of the reasons mayonnaise breaks. He also notes that salt, once dissolved, helps the yolks hold the oil, so he seasons at the start.

## Ingredients and proportions

For about 7 oz (200 g) of mayonnaise, within Escoffier's oil limit:

| Ingredient | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Egg yolks | 2 |
| Salt | 2 g (a generous pinch) |
| White wine vinegar | 15 ml (1 tablespoon) |
| Oil, at room temperature | 150 g (about 2/3 cup) |
| Lemon juice | a few drops, at the end |
| Boiling water | 1 teaspoon, at the end |

The amount of oil stays within Escoffier's limit of 70 to 85 g per yolk. In practice, a yolk can emulsify a good deal more oil if you thin the sauce with a few drops of water or vinegar whenever it gets very thick, but at this ratio the mayonnaise comes together easily.

## How to make it, step by step

**1. Whisk the yolks with the salt and vinegar.** Use a bowl at room temperature, not a chilled one. Escoffier insists that the vinegar be fully mixed into the yolks before you start adding oil.

**2. Add the oil in a thread.** Keep whisking. The first spoonfuls go slowly. Once the sauce has body, you can go a little faster. If you ease off and see free oil at the edge, stop and whisk until it comes back together.

**3. Finish.** Add the lemon juice, adjust the salt, and whisk in the teaspoon of boiling water. The water loosens the sauce a little and, according to Escoffier, makes it more stable. Cover it and refrigerate it.

## Why it breaks, and how to fix it

Escoffier lists three causes. The oil went in too fast at the start. The oil was cold or congealed. Or there was more oil than the yolks could hold.

To bring it back, put a fresh yolk in a clean bowl with a few drops of vinegar, whisk it, and add the broken sauce a little at a time, as if it were the oil. If only a small amount has broken, half a coffee spoon of mustard can do the work of that yolk.

You do not need to work over ice either. Escoffier says cold harms mayonnaise and is a common reason it breaks in winter. If the oil is cold, let it come to room temperature before you start.

## During service

You can make mayonnaise ahead and keep it covered in the refrigerator. If it is made with raw yolks, use it within 24 hours and do not leave it out of the refrigerator. Restaurants usually make it with pasteurized yolks.

Hollandaise and beurre blanc are made shortly before service and held warm over a water bath, without letting them boil. Vinaigrette is whisked again just before you use it. Each one is handled differently, so you cannot treat them all the same way.

## Variations

**Hollandaise.** In Le Guide Culinaire (1903), Escoffier reduces water and vinegar with salt and pepper, adds 5 yolks, and mounts the sauce with 500 g (about 1 pound 2 ounces) of butter over gentle heat. While mounting it, he adds a few spoonfuls of water to lighten it. It is a warm emulsion: if it gets too hot, the yolk sets or the butter separates. Béarnaise is made the same way, with a reduction of shallot, tarragon, and vinegar. Escoffier says it is essentially a butter mayonnaise and only needs to be served lukewarm.

**Beurre blanc.** A reduction of shallot, white wine, and vinegar, mounted with cold butter, with no yolk. That is why it breaks more easily than hollandaise.

**Without egg.** Mustard alone can stabilize a vinaigrette. For a thick, mayonnaise-style sauce without yolk, you need another emulsifier, such as milk or the liquid from cooked chickpeas (aquafaba). Traditional Spanish allioli, made only with garlic and oil, is also an egg-free emulsion.

**With olive oil.** It works well as long as the oil is not cold. A very intense extra-virgin oil can overpower the sauce and leave it bitter, so many kitchens use a mild olive oil or a neutral oil and add a little extra-virgin at the end for aroma.

## What you use it for

Mayonnaise goes with cold fish, eggs, and vegetables, and it is the base of many other cold sauces. Hollandaise goes with fish, asparagus, and eggs; beurre blanc, with fish and vegetables. Vinaigrette is for dressing. An [egg-yolk liaison](https://aichef.pro/en/blog/egg-yolk-liaison) also uses yolk, but for something else: thickening a hot sauce that is already made, right at the end.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does an emulsion break?**
Because the droplets of fat find each other again and the liquid separates. In mayonnaise, Escoffier names three causes: the oil went in too fast at the start, the oil was cold or congealed, or there was more oil than the yolks could hold.

**How much oil can one yolk hold?**
Escoffier sets the limit at about 2 1/2 oz (70 g) of oil per yolk if the sauce will be kept, and about 3 oz (85 g) if it will be used right away. In practice, a yolk can take more if you thin the sauce with a few drops of water or vinegar as you go, but his limit is a good guide for a sauce that will not break.

**Should you make mayonnaise over ice?**
Escoffier says the opposite. Cold spoils mayonnaise, and in winter it is a common reason the sauce breaks. The oil should be at kitchen temperature. If it is cold, warm it slightly.

**Is mustard required?**
In his mayonnaise, mustard is optional. Yolks, vinegar, salt, and oil are enough. Mustard helps, and he uses it as a rescue for a small broken batch: half a coffee spoon of mustard can take the place of a fresh yolk.

**Can you use extra-virgin olive oil?**
Yes, as long as it is not cold. A very green, peppery extra-virgin oil overpowers the sauce and can taste bitter. For a milder flavor, use a mild olive oil, or blend a neutral oil with a little extra-virgin.

**How long does homemade mayonnaise keep?**
If it is made with raw yolks, keep it covered in the refrigerator and use it within 24 hours. If the cold stiffens it, let it sit at room temperature for a few minutes and whisk it before using.
