## How to price a restaurant menu

Pricing a menu means costing every plate first, then dividing that plate cost by the food cost percentage you want that dish to carry. Do it dish by dish — one blanket multiplier for the whole menu is how good dishes end up underpriced and slow sellers end up hiding.

1. **Cost the recipe.** Plate cost is the cost of every ingredient that ends up on the plate, after trim and waste — not the price of the case you bought. A whole salmon, a bone-in ribeye and a bag of potatoes all lose weight before service. Cost the yield you actually serve. If you want it done recipe by recipe with your own invoice prices, the free [food cost calculator](https://aichef.pro/en/food-cost-calculator-restaurant) does the arithmetic for you.

2. **Pick a target food cost percentage for that dish.** Not for the restaurant — for this dish. A slow-braised short rib and a bowl of fries should not carry the same target, and they rarely do once you cost them properly.

3. **Divide plate cost by the target percentage.** That gives you a starting price. It is a starting point, not a menu decision yet.

4. **Round to a menu price and recheck the real food cost percentage.** Divide the rounded price back into the plate cost. Round up and your food cost drops; round down and it climbs. Do this every time, because the difference is real money across a year of service.

5. **Check it against your menu mix and your competition.** A price that works on paper still has to sell. Look at what the dishes around it cost, what your guests actually order, and where the plate sits in its section. ## The menu pricing formula

Menu price = plate cost ÷ target food cost %. That is the whole formula — everything else is judgment about which target to use and how to round.

The markup multiplier is the same idea flipped over: markup multiplier = 1 ÷ target food cost %. At a 30% target, 1 ÷ 0.30 = ×3.33. Multiply your plate cost by 3.333 and you get the same answer as dividing by 0.30 (rounded to 3.33, it lands a cent lower). Chefs who talk in markups and chefs who talk in percentages are describing one calculation.

Here is a worked example (example numbers):

- Plate cost: $4.29
- Target food cost: 30%
- Menu price: $4.29 ÷ 0.30 = $14.30
- Gross profit per plate: $14.30 − $4.29 = $10.01

On the menu you would round that to $14.95. Now check it again: $4.29 ÷ $14.95 = 28.7% real food cost, and gross profit per plate is $10.66. You gained 65 cents a plate by rounding in the direction guests expect.

| Target food cost | Menu price | Gross profit per plate |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 25% | $17.16 | $12.87 |
| 28% | $15.32 | $11.03 |
| 30% | $14.30 | $10.01 |
| 32% | $13.41 | $9.12 |
| 35% | $12.26 | $7.97 |

Same plate cost of $4.29 throughout. Compare the rows: moving the target from 30% to 35% takes $2.04 of gross profit off every plate — multiply that by covers and the argument for costing properly makes itself.

## What food cost percentage should you aim for?

There is no single right number. It depends on your concept, the dish itself and the price your guests will accept without flinching.

As a rule of thumb, many restaurants work somewhere around 25-35% on food. Premium proteins and high-cost ingredients usually carry a higher percentage because guests expect to pay for the ingredient, and you buy that plate's margin back elsewhere. Sides, starters, desserts and drinks usually carry a lower one. A pasta dish at 22% and a dry-aged steak at 38% can both be correct on the same menu.

So price dish by dish, then step back and watch the blend across the whole menu. One high-cost plate is a choice. A menu full of them is a problem you will only see in the blend.

## Food cost control formula

Theoretical food cost comes from your recipe costs — what the plates should have cost you if every portion was exact and nothing was wasted. Actual food cost comes from inventory: (beginning inventory + purchases − ending inventory) ÷ food sales.

Run both and compare them. The gap between theoretical and actual is where your money went: waste, over-portioning, theft, or recipe drift — a supplier swapped, a cook eyeballing the oil, a garnish that grew. Small gaps are normal. A gap that widens period after period is a signal, and it is cheaper to chase than to absorb.

Check it every period you count stock. If you only count stock once a quarter, you are looking at a quarter of drift and calling it normal.

## Beyond food cost: labor and prime cost

Food cost is only part of the plate. Prime cost = food cost + labor cost, and prime cost is what actually decides whether a dish earns its place.

This is where a plate with long prep can look fine on food cost and still be a bad sell. Slow braises, house-made stocks, anything that keeps a cook busy for an hour before service — that labor is real. A dish like that can need a lower food cost percentage to stay profitable, because it already carries more of the other half of the prime cost.

Once you know both numbers, menu engineering tells you what to do with the dish: push it, reprice it, or drop it. That decision is about popularity and margin together, never margin alone.

## Rounding and price psychology

Round after you calculate, never before. The moment you round first, you lose the true plate cost and every number downstream is fiction. Calculate the exact price, then choose the number a guest will read comfortably, then check the real food cost percentage on that rounded price.

Endings like .95, .50 or whole numbers are style choices, not math. What matters is consistency: pick an approach and hold it across the menu, because a section where three dishes end in .95 and two end in .50 reads as careless.

One tax point that changes the calculation. In the US, menu prices usually exclude sales tax — the guest pays it on top. In the UK and the EU, menu prices include VAT. If you are in a VAT market, strip the VAT out before you calculate food cost percentage, or you will be dividing your plate cost by a price your restaurant never keeps.
