# How to Reduce Food Cost in 30 Days

**A bonus guide from Food Cost Kit Pro by AI Chef Pro**

---

## 1. Introduction: From Chaos to Control in One Month

It's midnight on a Saturday and you're locking the back door. You did 120 covers, the kitchen flew, and the whole team left everything on the line. On Monday you open the bank account and the numbers don't add up. The sales were there, sure, but the margin is nowhere to be found. Sound familiar?

That disappearing margin has a name: **food cost out of control**.

### What food cost really is

Food cost is the cost of the ingredients you actually used, expressed as a percentage of your net food sales. Put simply: **how much the food you sell costs you**.

There are no shortcuts in the formula:

```
Actual Food Cost % = (Beginning Inventory + Purchases - Ending Inventory) / Net Food Sales x 100
```

And here's the first uncomfortable truth: **food cost is measured against sales, not against purchases**.

A lot of restaurants fall into the trap of looking only at their supplier invoices and dividing them by sales. That isn't food cost; it's a mirage. If you bought $3,000 of product this week but the walk-in is packed to the ceiling, you didn't spend $3,000 on what you sold. You spent quite a bit less. And if you ran the walk-in down because you skipped an order, your real food cost is higher than your invoices suggest.

**Food cost is what you use, not what you buy.**

### What "net food sales" means in this guide

Net food sales are what your guests paid for food (drinks are tracked separately, with their own pour cost: see question 4 in section 9) **minus sales tax (or VAT), minus tips and minus service charges**. None of those three is money from selling food:

- **Sales tax and VAT** belong to the government. You only collect them.
- **Tips** belong to your staff.
- **Service charges** aren't food sales either. In the US, a mandatory service charge is not a tip under federal wage law (Department of Labor, Fact Sheet #15): it's business revenue, and it may be subject to sales tax. In the UK, the Employment (Allocation of Tips) Act 2023, in force since October 1, 2024, requires every tip, gratuity and service charge to be passed on to workers in full, and HMRC's VAT Notice 709/1 treats a compulsory service charge as standard-rated.

Leave all three in and your sales look bigger, and your food cost looks better, than they really are.

### The goal of this guide

In 30 days you'll go from not knowing your real food cost (or just guessing it) to having it measured, under control and below your target. No magic here: this takes discipline, not tricks. But if you do what this guide lays out, in four weeks you'll have a system that tells you exactly where every dollar is leaking.

**Your 30-day goal:** cut your food cost by 3 to 7 percentage points. If you're at 35%, bring it down to 30-32%. If you're at 32%, get closer to 27-28%. Every point you cut is thousands of dollars a year that stay in your pocket instead of going down the drain or into the dumpster.

Let's get to work.

## 2. WEEK 1 — Measure: Put a Number on Reality

This first week you won't change a thing. You'll just **measure**. You can't control what you don't measure, and you can't improve what you don't control.

### Day 1: Opening inventory, in dollars

On the first Monday of the plan, before a single delivery comes in, you take a full inventory. No estimates. No "about half a case." You count everything.

**How to do it right:**

- **By category:** meat, seafood, dairy, produce, frozen, dry storage, oils and high-value spices (saffron, vanilla...).
- **In units you can use:** pounds, ounces, gallons, each. Not "half a case of strip loin": write down 9.5 lb of strip loin.
- **Valued at what it really cost you, without any tax you get back:** the price on your latest invoice. In the US, food bought for resale usually carries no sales tax, although the rules vary by state; if tax shows up on an invoice and you can't recover it, it's part of your cost. In the UK, if you're VAT-registered, use prices ex VAT (most food is zero-rated anyway).
- **Tool:** a spreadsheet or the "Inventory" tab of BONUS-actual-vs-theoretical-food-cost.xlsx. What matters is that it's systematic and repeatable.

**Example opening inventory (Monday morning):**

| Category | Item | Qty | Unit | Unit price | Total value |
|----------|------|-----|------|------------|-------------|
| Meat | Strip loin, USDA Choice | 18.4 | lb | $11.25 | $207.00 |
| Meat | Chicken breast, boneless | 13.0 | lb | $3.40 | $44.20 |
| Seafood | Cod loins | 9.0 | lb | $8.20 | $73.80 |
| Dairy | Butter, unsalted | 6.5 | lb | $4.10 | $26.65 |
| Produce | Green asparagus | 5.5 | lb | $3.80 | $20.90 |
| Dry storage | Extra-virgin olive oil | 3.0 | gal | $27.60 | $82.80 |
| ... | ... | ... | ... | ... | ... |
| **TOTAL OPENING INVENTORY** | | | | | **$4,850.00** |

That number, $4,850, is your starting point. Guard it with your life.

### Days 2 to 7: Log every purchase

Every day, every invoice that comes through the back door gets logged. No exceptions. If the bread guy drops off without an invoice, ask him for one or write it up yourself.

**What to log for each purchase:**

- Date
- Supplier
- Item, with its pack/size
- Quantity and unit
- Unit price
- Extended total (without any tax you can recover)

### How to read a foodservice invoice

Distributors in the US sell by the case, and three things on the invoice trip people up:

1. **Pack/size.** "4/10 lb" means four 10-lb bags, a 40-lb case. "6/#10" is six #10 cans. "4/1 gal" is four 1-gallon jugs. "36/1 lb" is 36 one-pound blocks of butter. The price on the line is usually the price of the whole case.
2. **Catch weight.** Meat, seafood, cheese and some produce ship in cases but are billed on actual weight: "1 CS, 24.6 LB @ $10.60/LB". For these, the case doesn't matter: log the pounds and the price per pound, and on the recipe cost card buy them in lb.
3. **Case price vs. price per unit.** A recipe cost card needs the price of the unit you buy in. If the case is one of the formats in the kit's unit list (a "50 lb bag", a "15 dz case", a "4x1 gal case", a "36x1 lb case"...), enter the case price as it is and the "Conversions" tab turns it into ounces, cups or eggs. If it isn't on the list, divide: case price ÷ contents, and buy in lb or each.

**Example invoice, and how each line goes on your cost card:**

| Invoice line | Pack/size | Billed | Price | Extension | On the cost card |
|--------------|-----------|--------|-------|-----------|------------------|
| Strip loin, Choice | 2 pc, catch weight | 24.6 lb | $10.60/lb | $260.76 | lb at $10.60 |
| Chicken breast, boneless | 4/10 lb | 1 case | $136.00 | $136.00 | 40 lb case at $136.00 |
| Butter, unsalted | 36/1 lb | 1 case | $147.60 | $147.60 | 36x1 lb case at $147.60 |
| Eggs, large | 15 dz | 1 case | $34.20 | $34.20 | 15 dz case at $34.20 |
| Crushed tomatoes | 6/#10 can | 1 case | $27.30 | $27.30 | oz at $0.0433 ($4.55 per can ÷ 105 oz net weight) |

Two more rules. A #10 can holds anywhere from 96 to 117 oz depending on the product, so always cost it by the net weight on the label. And the price you type is the price you actually pay per purchase unit, fees included (a split-case fee on wine or spirits, for example), without any tax you get back. Fryer oil is the classic trap: it's sold by the pound (a 35 lb jug-in-box) and shared by everything you fry. Leave it to the Q-factor, or cost it by weight, the way the Loaded Fries in template 08 do.

### Day 7: Closing inventory

Sunday night, or Monday before the first delivery, you count again. Same method, same sheet, same discipline.

**Example closing inventory (Sunday night):**

| Category | Item | Qty | Unit price | Total value |
|----------|------|-----|------------|-------------|
| Meat | Strip loin | 11.4 lb | $10.60 | $120.84 |
| ... | ... | ... | ... | ... |
| **TOTAL CLOSING INVENTORY** | | | | **$4,320.00** |

### The calculation that changes everything

You now have three of the four numbers. The last one is the week's net food sales, and it comes from your POS.

In the US, menu prices are pre-tax and the POS usually reports sales before sales tax: just make sure tips, service charges and drinks aren't included. In the UK, menu prices include VAT, so divide by 1.20. Careful: **taking 20% off is not the same as dividing by 1.20**, and it's a mistake you see all the time. On £120 including VAT, taking 20% off gives £96, but the real net figure is £100. If you took £18,000 including VAT, your net sales are £15,000.

**Complete Week 1 example:**

```
Opening inventory:        $4,850
Purchases (excl. tax):  + $3,210
Closing inventory:      - $4,320
------------------------------
Cost of goods sold:       $3,740  (what you actually used)
```

```
Net food sales: $12,100

Actual Food Cost = 3,740 / 12,100 x 100 = 30.91%
```

**Your actual food cost for Week 1 is 30.91%.**

This number is your diagnosis. It isn't good or bad yet: it's your reality. From here on, everything you do is about moving it down. Write it into 11-food-cost-percentage-tracker.xlsx, "Dashboard" tab: the "Period (month or week)" column takes weeks as well as months.

If you're above your range, you've got work to do. If you're inside it, you can still do better. Every point you cut is net margin that goes straight to your bottom line.

## 3. WEEK 2 — Cost Your Dishes: Put Every Plate Under the Microscope

You now know how much you use in total. Next, you find out **which dishes cost you more than they should and which ones are saving your margin**.

### Step 1: Find your 10 best-selling dishes

Pull the **product mix** report from your POS (Toast, Square, Clover, Lightspeed and the rest all have one). Which ten dishes sold the most this week? Not in dollars: in **units**. A $22 dish that sells 40 times moves more food, and more cost, than a $35 dish that sells 8 times.

For each one, write down:

- Units sold this week
- Net sales for that dish
- Average net price = net sales ÷ units (after comps and discounts, so it's what the dish really brings in)

**Example weekly top 10:**

| Dish | Units | Avg. net price | Weekly net sales |
|------|-------|----------------|------------------|
| Seared tuna tataki | 62 | $22.73 | $1,409.26 |
| Red wine-braised pork cheeks | 58 | $18.18 | $1,054.44 |
| Wild mushroom risotto | 51 | $15.45 | $787.95 |
| Grilled octopus, celery root purée | 47 | $20.91 | $982.77 |
| Burrata & heirloom tomato salad | 44 | $13.64 | $600.16 |
| ... | ... | ... | ... |

### Step 2: Cost each dish

Costing a dish means calculating **the exact ingredient cost of one portion**. No eyeballing it. You weigh, you measure, you calculate.

**Sample recipe cost card: Seared tuna tataki with ponzu and avocado**

| Ingredient | Qty per portion | Recipe unit | Price per purchase unit | Cost per portion |
|------------|-----------------|-------------|-------------------------|------------------|
| Tuna loin, sashimi grade (center cut, no trim) | 5 | oz | $18.24/lb | $5.70 |
| Avocado | 0.5 | each | $1.04 each | $0.52 |
| Soy sauce (for the ponzu) | 1 | fl oz | $15.36/gal | $0.12 |
| Lemon juice | 0.25 | fl oz | $3.84/qt | $0.03 |
| Toasted sesame oil | 0.25 | fl oz | $35.84/gal | $0.07 |
| Fresh ginger | 0.2 | oz | $2.40/lb | $0.03 |
| Chives | 0.1 | oz | $6.40/lb | $0.04 |
| Salt, pepper, sugar | 0.2 | oz | $0.80/lb | $0.01 |
| **Total ingredient cost** | | | | **$6.52** |

**Dish food cost = 6.52 / 22.73 x 100 = 28.68%**

Do this for all ten dishes. Open **01-recipe-cost-card.xlsx**, the first template in your dashboard, and go to the "Recipe Cost Card" tab. It takes minutes per dish, because the unit conversion (lb to oz, gal to fl oz, a case to each), the trim loss and the suggested menu price are worked out for you. Seasonings like the salt and pepper above usually go into the Q-factor (%) there instead of line by line. By hand it takes a while, but it's the best time you'll invest all week.

### Step 3: Build trim loss into your costs

Not everything you buy makes it to the plate. There's fat, bone, skin, peel and trimmings. In the kit this is the **trim loss %**, and yield % = 100% - trim loss %.

**Reference trim loss by category** (the values preloaded in the "Trim Loss Factors" tab of templates 01 to 08):

| Category | Typical trim loss | Range | What it looks like |
|----------|-------------------|-------|--------------------|
| Beef & red meat | 20% | 15-25% | 1 lb as purchased gives 12 to 13.6 oz trimmed |
| Poultry | 25% | 20-30% | Bones, skin and fat |
| Fish | 35% | 30-45% | Gutted, head-on fish cut on the bone. Whole round fish to skin-on fillet loses 50-60% |
| Shellfish | 45% | 35-55% | Shells and heads |
| Leafy greens | 25% | 20-35% | Outer leaves, stems, woody ends |
| Root vegetables | 12% | 8-18% | 1 lb of potatoes gives 13.1 to 14.7 oz peeled |
| Fruit | 15% | 10-25% | Peel, pits and cores |
| Frozen | 7% | 3-10% | Glaze and thaw loss |

The math behind it: if you pay $10.60/lb for strip loin and lose 20% trimming it, the pound you actually serve costs:

```
10.60 / (1 - 0.20) = 10.60 / 0.80 = $13.25 per usable lb
```

That's the real price of your steak. If you don't account for trim loss, you're understating your food cost. On the recipe cost card you don't do this by hand: you enter the invoice price and the trim loss %, and the card works out the quantity you have to buy.

### Measure your own yield: template 12

The reference values are a starting point, but your supplier, your cut and your cooks change them. Measure the ones that matter with **12-yield-test.xlsx**, "Butcher Yield Test" tab. Weigh the piece as it arrives, trim it, weigh each part and mark it as "Usable", "By-product" (something you use elsewhere, valued at what it replaces) or "Trim (no value)".

**Example (illustrative numbers): a whole strip loin** (the template itself comes preloaded with a whole beef tenderloin)

| Part | Type | Weight | Value |
|------|------|--------|-------|
| Whole strip loin as purchased, $10.60/lb | | 12.0 lb | $127.20 |
| Steaks | Usable | 9.0 lb | |
| Trimmings for burgers and staff meal, at $5.00/lb | By-product | 0.8 lb | $4.00 |
| Fat cap and sinew | Trim (no value) | 2.2 lb | |

- Net usable cost: $127.20 - $4.00 = $123.20
- COST PER USABLE LB: $123.20 ÷ 9.0 lb = $13.69
- Cost factor: $13.69 ÷ $10.60 = 1.29
- TRIM LOSS % FOR YOUR RECIPE COST CARD: 1 - 10.60 ÷ 13.69 = 22.6%

That last figure is the only one you carry over to the cost card. It already includes the credit for the trimmings, so the card, fed with the invoice price, gives you the same cost per steak as the test. Notice that it's higher than the 20% reference: using the reference would have understated the cost of every steak you sell. Duplicate the tab for your next test.

For things you cost by cooked weight (roasts, burgers), use the "Cooking Loss Test" tab. As a reference, the USDA Food Buying Guide gives ground beef with no more than 20% fat a yield of 0.74 lb of cooked, drained meat per pound raw: a 26% cooking loss. When a piece loses weight on the cutting board and again on the grill, the combined loss is 1 - (1 - trim loss) x (1 - cooking loss). With 10% trim and 25% cooking loss, that's 1 - 0.90 x 0.75 = 32.5%, not 35%.

**Also start weighing what you throw away this week:** spoilage, overproduction, mistakes. That's waste, not trim loss. It goes in the waste log (template 09, Week 4), never on the recipe cost card.

The grilled octopus is eating your margin. At 38% food cost, every one you sell is eight points off target. Time to make some decisions.

### Step 5: Basic menu engineering

Menu engineering crosses two things: **contribution margin** (average net price minus plate cost, in dollars) and **popularity** (units sold). The classic method, the Kasavana & Smith matrix, sorts your dishes into four groups:

- **Stars:** high margin, high popularity. Your crown jewels. Feature them, put them where the eye lands first on the menu, and have your servers recommend them.
- **Plowhorses:** low margin, high popularity. You sell a lot of them but make little on each. Trim the plate cost without hurting quality, or nudge the price up carefully.
- **Puzzles:** high margin, low popularity. Great for your numbers, but nobody orders them. They need visibility: specials board, server recommendations, pairings.
- **Dogs:** low margin, low popularity. Off the menu. They don't deserve the space.

How to draw the lines: a dish is **high margin** if its contribution margin beats the average for the dishes you're analyzing (weighted by units sold), and **popular** if it sells more than 70% of what an average dish sells. In the table below, the average margin is $12.90 and the popularity line is 28 units.

**Menu engineering table for your top dishes:**

| Dish | Contribution margin | Popularity (units/week) | Class | Action |
|------|---------------------|-------------------------|-------|--------|
| Seared tuna tataki | $16.21 | 62 (high) | ⭐ Star | Keep it and feature it on the menu |
| Braised pork cheeks | $13.33 | 58 (high) | ⭐ Star | Keep it; a small price increase if the market allows |
| Grilled octopus | $12.96 | 47 (high) | ⭐ Star, with a 38% food cost | Keep it, but fix the plate: renegotiate the octopus, rework the garnish or raise the price $2 |
| Wild mushroom risotto | $11.35 | 51 (high) | 🐴 Plowhorse | Keep it; trim the plate cost or test a $1 increase |
| Burrata salad | $10.44 | 44 (high) | 🐴 Plowhorse | Raise it $1 without fear; your margin will thank you |
| (A high-margin dish that rarely sells) | $15.00 | 12 (low) | 🧩 Puzzle | Promote it: specials board, tasting menu, active recommendation |
| (A low-margin dish that rarely sells) | $5.00 | 9 (low) | 🐶 Dog | Cut it or rework it completely |

Look at the octopus. The matrix calls it a Star, because it sells well and leaves more dollars per plate than the average. Its food cost says it's leaving money on the table. Both are true, and that's exactly why you look at the two numbers together: the matrix tells you whether to keep a dish, and the food cost tells you whether to fix it.

This classification hands you an action map on the spot. You don't need more theory: you need to make moves with these numbers.

## 4. WEEK 3 — Negotiate: Every Cent on the Invoice Counts

You know which dishes hurt. Now you attack the spending at the source: **what you pay your suppliers**.

Lowering food cost doesn't mean buying cheap at any price. It means buying **better**: better quality for the same price, or the same quality for a better price. The difference between a chef who negotiates and one who orders on autopilot can be 3 to 5 food cost points.

### Start with a price tracker: template 13

You can't negotiate prices you don't track. Open **13-ingredient-price-tracker.xlsx**, "Price Tracker" tab, and load your 20 biggest items: the purchase format exactly as it reads on the invoice, the format content, the base unit (lb, L or each) and the price of the format. The sheet works out the price per base unit and its variance against the previous price, and anything that jumps more than the alert threshold (5% by default) is flagged ALERT.

To carry a price over to a recipe cost card, copy the ingredient's "Price per base unit", paste it with **Paste Special, then Values** (in Excel, Ctrl+Alt+V, then V), and use that base unit as the purchase unit on the card. A normal paste drags the formula along with it.

Why bother right now: the USDA Economic Research Service forecasts beef prices up 9.8% in 2026 and egg prices down 30.8% (Food Price Outlook, August 2026). Your beef dishes are losing margin while your egg dishes are gaining it. The price list shows you which is which.

### Tactic 1: Get at least three quotes

For every major category (meat, seafood, produce, dairy, oils), get prices from three different suppliers: your broadline distributor, a specialty purveyor and a cash & carry, for example. Don't stick with the usual one just because "we trust them." Trust shows in good prices, not in smiles.

**How to do it this week:**

- Make a list of your 20 highest-volume items.
- Send that same list to three suppliers.
- Ask for written quotes on the same pack/size, same quality and same delivery schedule.
- Compare them side by side.

| Item | Supplier A | Supplier B | Supplier C | Best price |
|------|------------|------------|------------|------------|
| Strip loin, Choice (per lb) | $10.90 | $10.40 | $10.85 | B: $10.40 |
| Cod loins (per lb) | $8.20 | $8.75 | $7.95 | C: $7.95 |
| Extra-virgin olive oil (per gal) | $27.60 | $25.90 | $28.40 | B: $25.90 |

You don't have to switch everything. But if Supplier B gives you strip loin 50 cents a pound cheaper and you buy 150 lb a month, that's $75 a month and $900 a year. On one item.

### Tactic 2: Consolidate your volume

A 60-seat restaurant has buying power if it concentrates its purchases. Instead of ordering from five different suppliers, pick two or three and negotiate on volume.

**A line that works:** "If I give you all my beef for the month, what can you do on the strip loin and the pork cheeks?"

Your supplier wants a steady, high-volume customer. You want a better price. Everybody wins.

### Tactic 3: Check your purchase formats

Sometimes the extra cost is in the format, not the product.

- **Portion-cut vs. whole:** Are you buying cut strip steaks at $13.90/lb when the whole strip loin is $10.60/lb? If your cook can butcher, buying whole can pay off, but run the yield test first. At the 22.6% trim loss from Week 2, the usable pound costs $13.69 and you save barely 21 cents a pound. At 12%, it would cost $12.05 and you'd save $1.85. The yield test decides, not the sticker price.
- **Fillets vs. whole fish:** Same idea, and here the sticker price fools people. Fillets might cost $8.20/lb and the whole fish $4.50/lb, but a whole round fish yields only 40-50% skin-on fillet. At a 55% loss, the usable pound comes to $10.00 ($4.50 ÷ 0.45): more than the fillet. A fish that yields better, or frames you sell as stock, can flip it. The yield test decides.
- **Bulk vs. vacuum-packed:** For non-perishables or medium-turnover items, bulk is usually cheaper. Decide whether the extra shelf life and convenience of the packaging are worth what you pay for them.
- **Bigger formats:** A 35 lb jug-in-box of fryer oil instead of 1-gallon jugs. 50 lb bags of flour instead of 5 lb bags. The cost per unit drops a lot.

### Tactic 4: Cook with the seasons

A restaurant that changes its menu with the seasons has a huge food cost advantage. In-season produce is cheaper, more plentiful and at its best.

This week, go through your menu. Anything out of season? If so, you're paying a premium your guests don't necessarily value. Swap asparagus in January for roasted cauliflower or winter squash. The guest eats better and you save.

### Tactic 5: Smart swaps

This isn't about cutting quality. It's about finding ingredients that perform just as well, or better, for less.

**Real examples:**

- **Parmigiano Reggiano vs. Grana Padano:** In a risotto, Grana Padano works beautifully and often costs 30-40% less.
- **Tuna belly vs. tuna loin:** For a tartare, the loin is better value than the belly, which is an expensive indulgence.
- **Octopus by origin and size:** The price per pound changes a lot depending on where it comes from and how big it is. Ask your seafood purveyor for alternatives and taste them side by side.
- **Fresh vs. frozen berries:** In pastry and sauces, good frozen fruit performs just as well and doesn't spoil on you.

### Tactic 6: Payment terms

Price isn't everything. Payment terms are money too.

- **Early-payment discounts:** Terms like "2/10, net 30" mean 2% off if you pay within 10 days instead of 30. If you have the cash, it's a direct saving. Always ask.
- **Paying on delivery:** If you pay cash on delivery, negotiate an extra 2-3% off.
- **Quarterly volume rebates:** "If I buy more than $5,000 from you this quarter, will you rebate 3% at the end?"

### Tactic 7: Review every quarter, not every year

Prices move. What's a good price today may not be in three months, and this year beef has made that painfully clear. Set up a quarterly review of your supplier agreements. You don't have to switch every time, but you do have to ask and compare.

### How to talk to a supplier (so it doesn't feel awkward)

A lot of cooks hate negotiating. It feels like haggling at a flea market. It isn't. It's a professional conversation between adults doing business.

**How to structure the call or meeting:**

1. **Thank and acknowledge:** "Mike, we're really happy with your product. The beef is great and your service is spot on."
2. **Set the context:** "We're tightening our costs to be more profitable without touching quality."
3. **Be specific:** "On the strip loin, I need to come down about 8%. Is there something we can work out together? Maybe I give you all my beef, we adjust the format, or we lock in a quarterly price."
4. **Listen to the offer:** Let him talk. Sometimes you get something you hadn't thought of.
5. **Close with a clear agreement:** "OK, so this month we try the strip loin at $9.75 a pound if I commit to at least 150 pounds a month, and we review it in three months. Deal?"

### Common negotiating mistakes

- **Threatening to walk:** "Cut the price or I'm going to someone else." Guaranteed bad blood and a broken relationship. Frame it as a chance to work together instead.
- **Taking the first offer without comparing:** It's not your supplier's fault you didn't look at alternatives. Always compare.
- **Looking only at the price per pound and forgetting trim loss:** A cheaper product with more trim loss can cost you more on the plate. Always compare the cost per portion served, not per pound bought.
- **Neglecting the relationship:** The supplier who saves you with an emergency delivery at 11 a.m. on a Saturday is worth gold. Don't squeeze so hard that you lose that favor. Find the balance.

## 5. WEEK 4 — Control: Routines That Keep Food Cost in Line

You've measured, costed and negotiated. Now you lock all that work in with **control routines** that stop your food cost from creeping back up the moment you look away.

### Daily routine (5-10 minutes)

**1. Receiving: check what you're paying for**

Every time a delivery comes in, someone you trust (you, your sous chef or a senior cook) checks:

- That what's on the invoice matches what comes through the door: item, quantity and pack/size. On catch-weight items, weigh the case.
- That the invoice price matches the agreed price (your price list from Week 3 makes this a ten-second check).
- That the quality is what you expected: freshness, appearance and temperature on refrigerated and frozen goods.

If something's off, refuse it or note it on the invoice and have the driver sign a credit memo on the spot. Not the next day.

**2. Daily waste sheet**

A sheet in the kitchen where everything that gets thrown away is written down, with the reason why:

| Date | Item | Qty | Reason | Estimated cost |
|------|------|-----|--------|----------------|
| Monday | Chicken breast | 0.9 lb | Past its use-by date | $3.06 |
| Tuesday | Butternut squash soup | 1.5 qt | Left over from lunch, can't be frozen | $4.50 |
| Wednesday | Tuna loin | 5 oz | Overcooked, plate refired | $5.70 |

This sheet is a thermometer. If waste spikes one week, something's going on: overproduction, bad forecasting, poor storage, recipes that need adjusting. Enter it every week in 09-food-waste-log.xlsx, "Weekly Waste Log" tab.

### Weekly routine (30-45 minutes)

**1. High-value inventory**

You don't need to count every last grain of salt. But every week, weigh and value:

- Fresh meat and seafood
- Expensive dairy (aged cheeses, premium butter)
- High-priced oils and vinegars
- Anything that costs more than $5 a pound or that makes up a significant share of your spending

These items are usually the bulk of your ingredient cost. Tracking them weekly gives you a near-complete picture of your food cost without the effort of a full inventory.

**2. Quick weekly food cost**

With the high-value inventory, the week's purchases and the week's net food sales, you calculate an estimated food cost. It won't be as precise as the monthly one with a full inventory, but it shows you the trend. If it drifts, you act.

**3. Monthly dashboard review**

At the end of every month, pull everything together in a simple dashboard:

| Item | This month | Last month | Target | Variance |
|------|------------|------------|--------|----------|
| Net food sales | $52,000 | $48,500 | $50,000 | +$2,000 |
| Cost of goods sold | $14,560 | $14,550 | $14,000 | +$560 |
| Food cost % | 28.0% | 30.0% | 28.0% | 0.0 ✅ |
| Total waste | $780 | $970 | $700 | +$80 |
| Highest food cost dish | Octopus (38%) | Octopus (40%) | <30% | ❌ |
| Star dish | Tataki (28.7%) | Tataki (29.5%) | <30% | ✅ |

This dashboard tells you at a glance whether you're on track. Share it with your team. Transparency creates accountability.

### Watch your prime cost too

Food cost doesn't live on its own. **Prime cost** is your cost of goods sold (food and beverage) plus your total labor cost (wages, payroll taxes and benefits). It's the biggest part of your P&L that you can actually control, and the usual target is **under 60-65% of net sales**. The National Restaurant Association puts the median for limited-service restaurants at 65 cents of every sales dollar. For full-service, add a food and beverage cost of 28-35% to the NRA's median labor cost of 36.5% and you land at roughly 65-70% (that's our arithmetic, not an NRA figure).

Why it matters: if you cut three points of food cost but add a prep cook you don't need, you haven't gained a thing. The dashboard in template 11 tracks food cost; once a month, add your labor total from payroll and check the sum.

### Alerts: what to do when food cost goes up

One bad month isn't a disaster. Two months in a row of increases is a red flag. These are the most common causes and how to deal with them:

**Alert 1: Supplier price increases with no change to the menu**
- **Symptom:** Your plate costs go up but your menu prices stay the same.
- **Action:** Update your recipe cost cards with the new prices. If a dish goes over your target, raise the price or change the dish. Beef in 2025-26 is the textbook case.

**Alert 2: Rising waste**
- **Symptom:** The weekly waste log is trending up.
- **Action:** Review your kitchen processes. Are you overproducing? Does a cook need more training? Is storage right? Adjust.

**Alert 3: Theft or unrecorded staff consumption**
- **Symptom:** The inventory doesn't add up and there's no waste to explain it.
- **Action:** Set a clear staff meal policy. Feeding the team (controlled, with set dishes) is one thing; product that "disappears" is something else entirely. Cameras, locks on high-value storage and sign-out logs for product.

**Alert 4: Dishes over target that nobody reviews**
- **Symptom:** The same dish has been losing margin for three months and nothing has changed.
- **Action:** Cut it, rework it or raise the price. Doing nothing is food cost's worst enemy.

**Alert 5: POS discrepancies**
- **Symptom:** Recorded sales don't match what's in the till, or voids and comps are piling up.
- **Action:** Audit your tickets. Make sure everything that leaves the kitchen is rung into the POS. Handwritten tickets that never go through the register are a black hole for food cost.

## 7. Case Study: From 32% on Paper to 35% for Real, and Down to 31.8% in 30 Days

To show you this isn't theory, here's a **composite case built from real consulting work**: the percentages and the sequence of decisions come from actual engagements, combined into a single restaurant. The dollar amounts are illustrative, sized for a 60-seat US restaurant so you can map them onto your own business. It isn't one specific client.

### The starting point

- **Type:** Contemporary Mediterranean restaurant
- **Seats:** 60
- **Average food check:** $50.90 per guest, net (before sales tax and tips; drinks are tracked separately)
- **Covers per week:** 450
- **Weeks of business per month:** 4
- **Monthly net food sales:** 450 x 4 x $50.90 = **$91,620**
- **Food cost they THOUGHT they had:** 32% (an estimate, not a measurement)
- **Cost of goods sold that would imply:** 0.32 x 91,620 = **$29,318.40**

The owner knew food cost was high but had no clear idea why. "We're a product-driven kitchen, it's normal for it to be expensive," he'd say. Spoiler: it wasn't normal.

### Week 1: The measurement that opened their eyes

They took the opening inventory: $6,200 in stock. They logged the week's purchases: $7,800, without recoverable tax. Closing inventory: $5,900. Net food sales for the week: $22,905.

```
Actual food cost = (6,200 + 7,800 - 5,900) / 22,905 = 8,100 / 22,905 = 35.36%
```

**Surprise:** the actual food cost was 35.36%, not the 32% they had estimated. Their 32% was a gut feeling that nobody had ever checked against an inventory count. This week the walk-in went down from $6,200 to $5,900, so they used more than they bought, and the real cost of what they sold was higher than the invoices alone would suggest.

### Week 2: The cost cards pointed at the culprits

They costed their top 10 dishes. The results:

| Dish | Actual food cost | Within target (30%)? |
|------|------------------|----------------------|
| Duck & wild mushroom risotto | 24% | ✅ |
| Seared tuna tataki | 29% | ✅ |
| Braised pork cheeks | 27% | ✅ |
| **Grilled octopus** | **38%** | ❌ |
| **Aged beef steak tartare** | **38%** | ❌ |
| Burrata salad | 23% | ✅ |
| Parmesan risotto | 25% | ✅ |
| **Whole roasted wild striped bass** | **36%** | ❌ |
| House-made croquettes | 18% | ✅ |
| Cheesecake | 15% | ✅ |

Three dishes over target. The octopus, the steak tartare and the striped bass were dragging the margin down. Together they added up to 109 portions a week (47 octopus, 40 tartare and 22 bass), or 436 a month.

**Menu engineering and food cost, applied:**

- **Grilled octopus:** Popular, but 38% food cost. Decision: raise the menu price so the average net price goes up $2.50 (from $20.91 to $23.41) and switch the purée from celery root to potato (cheaper and just as good). New plate cost: $7.20. New food cost: 30.8%.
- **Steak tartare:** Average net price $24.74, plate cost $9.40 (38%). Decision: switch from tenderloin to dry-aged top sirloin cap, just as tasty in a tartare and 30% cheaper. New plate cost: $6.80. New food cost: 27.5%.
- **Wild striped bass:** Few sales (22 a week) and a 36% food cost, the weakest dish on the list. Decision: off the menu. In its place, farm-raised barramundi fillets from a domestic supplier: cheaper, consistent and well received by guests.

### Week 3: Negotiation that paid off

With the numbers from Weeks 1 and 2 in hand, the chef went to the suppliers:

- **Beef:** Consolidated all beef purchases with a single purveyor in exchange for a 6% discount. On $8,400 a month of beef: **$504 a month**.
- **Seafood:** Started buying direct from a seafood wholesaler twice a week, 8% below the distributor's price. On $6,200 a month of seafood: **$496 a month**. (The switch from whole bass to barramundi fillets, with trim loss going from 28% to 5% and the plate cost from $9.50 to $6.20, is already counted in Week 2.)
- **Olive oil:** Moved from 1-gallon tins to 5-gallon containers with a regional supplier, saving $3.20 a gallon. At 20 gallons a month: **$64 a month**.
- **Produce:** Agreed a fixed weekly price with a local farm for seasonal produce, cutting out the middleman: **$150 a month**.

**Total purchasing savings: 504 + 496 + 64 + 150 = $1,214 a month.**

### Week 4: Routines that locked in the change

They put in place:
- A daily waste sheet. They found that on weekends they were throwing away 15% more produce because of overproduction. They adjusted Saturday prep. Savings: $90 a week x 4 = **$360 a month** in waste avoided.
- A weekly high-value inventory (meat, seafood, oils). Weekly food cost was now calculated every Sunday. No more stockouts, and no more emergency runs to the cash & carry at whatever price: **$240 a month**.

**Total savings from the routines: 360 + 240 = $600 a month.**
- A monthly dashboard shared with the team. The cook who kept his station under target got a small bonus (dinner for two at the restaurant). Motivation did the rest.

### Results after 30 days

**The measured starting point** (Week 1, extended to four weeks):

```
Usage:  $8,100/week x 4 = $32,400/month
Sales: $22,905/week x 4 = $91,620/month
Actual food cost = 32,400 / 91,620 = 35.36%
```

**What each lever saved**, with the dollars from the sections above:

| Week | Lever | Cost savings ($/month) |
|------|-------|------------------------|
| 1 | Real measurement: the diagnosis | $0 (but nothing happens without it) |
| 2 | Cost cards and dish adjustments | $1,327 |
| 3 | Negotiation and purchase formats | $1,214 |
| 4 | Waste and inventory routines | $600 |
| | **TOTAL** | **$3,141/month** |

The Week 2 breakdown, dish by dish: octopus $0.75 x 188 portions a month = $141 · steak tartare $2.60 x 160 = $416 · barramundi instead of bass $3.30 x 88 = $290 · re-checking portion sizes on the other seven top-10 dishes = $480. Total: $1,327.

On top of that, raising the octopus's average net price by $2.50 across 188 portions added **$470 a month in revenue** without touching the cost.

**Food cost after 30 days:**

```
Cost of goods sold: 32,400 - 3,141 = $29,259/month
Net food sales:     91,620 +   470 = $92,090/month
Food cost = 29,259 / 92,090 = 31.8%
```

**The drop: from a real 35.36% (not the 32% they believed) to 31.8%. That's 3.6 points in four weeks**, inside the 3-to-7-point range this guide opened with.

**The savings in dollars:**

- **Monthly ingredient savings: $3,141**
- **Projected annual savings: $37,692**
- Plus $5,640 a year in extra revenue from the octopus price adjustment.

And here's what matters most: the system stays in place. In the months that follow, food cost keeps coming down, because every new cost card, every negotiation and every waste log builds on what's already been done. Going from 31.8% to 28% is the work of the second quarter, not of the first thirty days.

**Which levers made the biggest difference?**

1. **Real measurement (Week 1):** Finding out that food cost wasn't 32% but 35.36% was the shock they needed to act. Zero dollars of direct savings, and still the most important of the four: without measurement there's nothing to fix.
2. **Cost cards and dish adjustments (Week 2):** $1,327 a month. Three dishes reworked and one surgical price increase.
3. **Negotiation and formats (Week 3):** $1,214 a month. Consolidating purchases, buying direct from the seafood wholesaler and locking in seasonal prices.
4. **Control routines (Week 4):** $600 a month. The waste sheet and the weekly inventory, which are also what keep the other $2,541 from evaporating within three months.

**The takeaway:** they didn't have to lower quality, lay anyone off or jack up prices across the board. They had to **measure, analyze, negotiate and control**. Exactly what you've learned in this guide.

## 9. Frequently Asked Questions

### 1. How often should I calculate food cost?

Ideally **weekly**, with a high-value inventory, and **monthly**, with a full inventory. The weekly number shows you the trend and lets you react fast. The monthly number gives you the exact picture for your P&L.

### 2. What if my food cost is below target? Can that be bad?

An unusually low food cost (say, 18% in a product-driven restaurant) can mean you're cutting corners on quality or charging too much for the value you deliver. Guests notice, and they don't come back. The right food cost is the one that lets you deliver the quality you promise with a healthy margin. Not lower at the expense of quality, and not higher for lack of control.

### 3. How do I handle a sudden price jump on a key ingredient?

When an item's price shoots up (as beef has in 2025-26, or eggs did before it), you have three options: (a) pass the increase on to the menu price, explaining it openly if you need to; (b) find a substitute of similar quality while the spike lasts; (c) take the dish off the menu for a while and replace it with one that has a better margin. What you can't do is swallow the increase and hope for the best.

### 4. Do drinks, and the tax on them, go into food cost?

No. Food cost measures food. Drinks have their own ratio, **beverage cost** (pour cost at the bar), which you calculate the same way with beverage purchases and beverage sales; for a bar, 18-24% is the usual range, and 04-pour-cost-calculator.xlsx works it out cocktail by cocktail. Tax never goes on either side of the formula. In the US, sales tax is added to the check and isn't a sale. In the UK, food and drink consumed on the premises are standard-rated at 20% VAT, so take it out of both before you calculate. Separate food and drink by the ratio, not by the tax.

### 5. Is food cost software worth it?

If you run more than 40-50 seats and your sales justify the investment, yes. Good software connects your POS, purchasing, inventory and recipe costing and gives you food cost in real time. But be careful: software doesn't do the dirty work. If the data going in is bad (eyeballed inventories, cost cards without trim loss, unchecked deliveries), what comes out is garbage. Discipline first; tools second.

### 6. How do I get my kitchen team involved in food cost control?

Share the numbers with them. Not as a threat, but as a shared challenge. Explain that every food cost point they save is margin that can go to better conditions for them, new equipment or the survival of the business that pays them. Share the dashboard, celebrate the wins and recognize the cook who keeps their station in line. A cook who understands how their actions hit the P&L is an ally, not just an employee.
