## Phase 2 - Zoning & Permits
A1: WEDDING VENUE STARTUP CHECKLIST
A2: PHASE 2: ZONING & PERMITS (18-6 months before opening)
A4: OK
B4: TASK
C4: RESPONSIBLE
D4: TIMING
E4: NOTES
A5: ☐
B5: Zoning check in writing before signing anything
C5: Owner / Code consultant
D5: Before signing anything
E5: Ask the county planning office IN WRITING whether a wedding or event venue is allowed on that parcel and under which permit (conditional use permit or special use permit). A verbal answer is not a permit. Ask about the zoning district, any overlays and whether agritourism rules apply.
A6: ☐
B6: Choose a property with an existing barn or building, 30-60 minutes from the city
C6: Owner
D6: Months 1-4
E6: A hall, restrooms and a kitchen area that already exist save build-out cost and permitting time. The plan targets a property 30-60 minutes from a mid-size or larger city. Confirm what the zoning allows before falling in love with a barn.
A7: ☐
B7: Check water, electricity and septic
C7: Owner / Code consultant
D7: Before signing
E7: Have an engineer check the available electrical capacity, whether the water comes from a public system or a well (if a well will serve your guests, ask your state drinking water program), and whether the septic system can handle your guest count.
A8: ☐
B8: Negotiate the lease: long term, counting renewal options
C8: Owner / Attorney
D8: Months 3-5
E8: The build-out goes on land you don't own, so the term, counting renewal options, should be at least as long as your loan: SBA lenders typically require this; confirm with your lender. Aim for a term long enough to recover your investment.
A9: ☐
B9: Lease contingent on CUP approval (and the certificate of occupancy)
C9: Attorney
D9: At signing
E9: The lease should not bind you if the conditional use permit or the certificate of occupancy is denied. Negotiate a rent-free or reduced-rent period while you build and get permits.
A10: ☐
B10: Purchase option or right of first refusal, landlord consent and TI allowance
C10: Attorney
D10: At signing
E10: Ask for a purchase option or a right of first refusal if the landlord sells. Get the landlord's written consent to the build-out and to using the lease as collateral for your loan, and negotiate a tenant improvement (TI) allowance. If the property is family-owned, the rent should still be at market: check with your CPA.
A11: ☐
B11: Apply for the conditional / special use permit (CUP / SUP)
C11: Architect / Attorney
D11: Months 5-12
E11: Expect a public hearing. The county may attach conditions such as a cap on events, hours, traffic and noise. Timelines vary by county: ask the planning office. Your lease should depend on the outcome.
A12: ☐
B12: Change of occupancy to assembly (Group A-2): building permit to certificate of occupancy
C12: Architect / Attorney
D12: Months 5-12
E12: Turning a barn into a place of assembly usually means a building permit and a certificate of occupancy for assembly use (Group A-2). Ask the building official what the change requires for your building, and don't assume an older building is exempt.
A13: ☐
B13: Ask the building official and the fire marshal in writing
C13: Architect / Attorney
D13: Months 5-12
E13: Ask about occupant load, sprinklers, exits, restrooms and tents, and keep the answers. They set the maximum guest count you can book and drive the cost of the build-out. Rules differ by state, county and city.
A14: ☐
B14: No firm dates without the CUP and the certificate of occupancy
C14: Owner
D14: Until you hold the permits
E14: If the permit is denied or delayed, you will have to refund what you collected. Before the permits arrive, accept only contingent holds with a full refund if they don't, agreed in writing (clause 2A of the contract template, Contingent booking before permits). Once you hold the permits, they become firm bookings.
A15: ☐
B15: ADA Title III: accessible routes, restrooms and parking
C15: Architect / Attorney
D15: Months 8-14
E15: A venue you rent to the public is a place of public accommodation. Put accessible routes, restrooms and parking on the plans from the first drawing (2010 ADA Standards) and confirm the details with your architect.
A16: ☐
B16: Occupant load, exits and emergency plan
C16: Architect / Attorney
D16: With the plans
E16: The fire marshal sets the maximum occupancy. Post the occupant load, keep exits clear and marked, install emergency lighting and write an evacuation plan. Tents have their own fire-code rules, so ask the fire marshal. Never book more guests than your certificate of occupancy allows. Your county or city may ask for more.
A17: ☐
B17: Noise: county ordinance, decibel limit and sound meter
C17: Architect / Attorney
D17: With the plans
E17: Many county ordinances limit amplified sound, usually with a lower limit at night. Ask the county for the limit that applies to your property. A sound meter with a log is your proof if a neighbor complains.
A18: ☐
B18: Outdoor music and curfew under the CUP
C18: Owner
D18: With the permit
E18: The CUP conditions and the county ordinance set the curfew. Ask, and write it into your contract with the couple. If you allow music, make sure ASCAP, BMI and SESAC licensing is covered.
A19: ☐
B19: Insurance: general liability, host liquor liability, property and umbrella
C19: Insurance agent
D19: Before opening
E19: Get quotes from an agent who insures venues. Lenders, landlords and your caterer will ask for certificates of insurance (COI). Add coverage for damage to improvements and for loss of income. Costs vary by state and guest count: get local quotes.
A20: ☐
B20: Well water: testing and permit
C20: Owner / Certified lab
D20: Before the first event
E20: If a well serves your guests, it may count as a public water system. Ask your state drinking water program about testing and permitting before the first event, and don't assume private-well rules apply.

A14: ☐

C14: Caterer
D14: In the agreement

D16: Before the first event
