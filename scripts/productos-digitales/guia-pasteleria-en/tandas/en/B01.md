## 1. Shop with Its Own Kitchen or a Separate Production Kitchen

### The Context of the Decision

You're going to look for a space for your pastry shop, and you'll run into the same paradox every time. The space you like, the one with a storefront window and people walking past, almost never has the square footage to fit a decent production kitchen in the back. And the space with square feet to spare is usually on a street nobody walks down, where a window does nobody any good. You end up trading floor area against visibility, and that trade has a cost almost nobody works into the numbers before signing: the part of the space given over to the production kitchen doesn't sell a single cake, and you pay the same rent for those square feet as for the ones behind the window. If your space rents at the case's $1.24 per square foot a month (about $15 a year, the way US listings usually quote it), the production kitchen costs you $1.24 a square foot just like the display case does, even though nobody can be seen walking into it from the street. That rent is the case's, and it sits well below the $24.79 per square foot a year that CBRE reported as the national average asking rent for retail space in the second quarter of 2026, so put in your own.

### The Options on the Table

With that starting point, there are three roads, not two.

- **Option A**, everything in one space: the front counter up front, with the window and the display case, and the production kitchen taking up the back, closed to the public.
- **Option B**, split the two pieces: a closed-door production kitchen in a cheaper part of town, with no storefront and no need for foot traffic, and a small counter shop in a busy spot that receives the product already finished.
- **Option C**, start without a production kitchen of your own: buy finished product from a third party and give the whole space over to sales.

What changes among them isn't just the square footage. The startup cost changes; the total rent you'll pay each month changes, adding up two spaces or one; whether you need daily transport between the kitchen and the counter changes (a van, a delivery schedule, a cold chain on the road); and how you're permitted changes once the counter becomes a second site supplied by a central kitchen. And there's a second filter that isn't about money but about the product: without your own production kitchen you can't truthfully put “made from scratch in our kitchen” on your window or your labels. If that's exactly your selling point, option C takes it away from you before you start. (There's also a lighter version of option B that's common in the US: renting hours in a licensed commissary or shared kitchen, which WebstaurantStore puts at $250 to $750 a month; chapter 1 of the guide covers it.)

### The Criterion You Decide By

The rule here, as in the rest of this bonus's decisions, is easy to state and easy to skip: you compare total cost over several years, not the monthly rent on the first lease. A space that costs more a month but holds the kitchen and the counter under one roof can come out cheaper than two separate leases as soon as you add up the daily transport and your people's time going back and forth between the two.

And before you compare anything, there's a first filter that rules out option A on its own: if the square footage you're looking at can't fit the seven zones of the production kitchen with a one-way flow (that is, without the product doubling back across its own path between receiving, production, packing and dispatch), option A simply doesn't exist for that space, however much you love the window. It isn't a matter of taste; it's whether the workflow works.

Every decision in this bonus closes the same way: with an explicit recommendation and the condition that would make you change it. Here the default recommendation is option A when the space allows it, because it creates the least day-to-day friction; it switches to option B as soon as the space with enough room is on a street with no foot traffic and the rent you save by splitting into two spaces covers the cost of transport; and it switches to option C only if you're not going to sell “made from scratch” as your selling point.

### The Cell That Settles It

The "Format Variant" sheet in startup-cost-calculator.xlsx gives you the figure for each road with your own numbers plugged in. In the core case the calculator ships with, putting a production kitchen and a shop in one space needs an opening investment of $165,800; the closed-door production kitchen, with no counter, needs $70,585; and the counter shop with no kitchen of its own, buying finished product, needs $57,948. The "Zones and Square Feet" sheet in kitchen-capacity-and-site.xlsx is the one that tells you whether your actual square footage fits the seven zones: in the modeled case, the space is 969 square feet in all and the production kitchen takes 484 of them, half the space, 50% of the floor area.

For contrast with the pack's figures, third-party estimates put a small retail bakery at $50,000 to $100,000 and up (Zenind, 2026). It's an estimate with no line-by-line breakdown you can check, not industry data, and that's why it's cited that way; the case lands above it because it prices a full production kitchen and its display cases line by line.

### The Rule That Applies, When There Is One

Here there is a rule, and it's worth having it straight before you sign any lease. What makes option B workable in the US is the FDA test that decides who has to register a food facility. A retail food establishment doesn't register (21 CFR 1.226(c)) as long as more than half of its food sales, by value, go directly to consumers, and “consumers” doesn't include businesses (21 CFR 1.227; checked in October 2026). Your own counter isn't a business you sell to, so on a plain reading what your production kitchen bakes and then sells over your own counter reaches consumers and doesn't add to the wholesale side of that line. One caution, the same one chapter 9 of the guide makes: FDA applies its test establishment by establishment, and we didn't find the rule's text spelling out how it treats a production kitchen in a separate building that sells only through its owner's own shops. If that's your plan, put the question to FDA or a food attorney before you sign for the space.

That good news doesn't spare you the paperwork. We found no US category that bundles a central kitchen and its shops into a single unit: the food permit is issued after plan review and the pre-opening inspection (Food Code 8-201.11 and Part 8-3), and both look at a physical place, so with option B expect two plan reviews and two inspections, and ask your agency whether each site needs its own permit and how it treats food you bake at one site and sell at the other (that's the third question on the "Which Permit Do I Need" sheet). Keep three conditions in view for the setup to hold: the same owner across the kitchen and the shop, the cold chain between them at 41 °F or below for TCS items (Food Code 3-501.16), and the written allergen notice at the counter that sells unpackaged product (3-602.12(C)). In New York, the Article 20-C license already names wholesale and retail in-store bakery operations; in Florida, ask FDACS whether supplying several shops calls for its Wholesale/Manufactured Food Establishment Permit.

And if you're toying with the idea of a production kitchen alone, with no counter, keep the 50% line from that same rule in view: once more than half of your food sales by value go to cafés, restaurants or stores owned by someone else instead of to consumers, you're no longer a retail food establishment in the FDA's eyes and you generally have to register your facility (21 CFR 1.226 and 1.227). The "Selling Wholesale" sheet seeds a cautious working line of 25%, our own, not the law's, so you see the legal 50% coming long before you reach it, and decision 7 of this bonus works through your first wholesale account. For scale, third-party estimates put a production bakery at $75,000 to $150,000 and up (Zenind, 2026), against the case's $70,585 for a closed-door kitchen.

## 2. Buy the Blast Chiller Now or Wait

### The Context of the Decision

The blast chiller is the most expensive single purchase in the production kitchen, and that's exactly why it's the first one to fall off the budget when the numbers don't work. When it's time to cut, nobody cuts the walk-in or the oven: they cut the blast chiller, because it looks like something you can live without for a while.

The line you always hear at this stage is the same: “I'll make less product the first year and buy it later.” It sounds reasonable, but it hides the real question, which isn't whether you can open without a blast chiller but how much production ceiling you're buying with that sentence. A business doesn't grow in a vacuum: it grows as far as the equipment that limits it allows, and if that equipment is the blast chiller, the business won't break through that ceiling no matter how hard you push.

### The Options on the Table

There are three roads, and you have to look at all three with the same workbook before you decide:

- Option A: buy it on day one, with the capacity that matches the assortment you want to offer from the start.
- Option B: open without one and limit the assortment to what doesn't need to go through a blast chiller, putting off the purchase until sales can carry it on their own.
- Option C: buy one with fewer trays (a scaled-down version) and trade it in for a bigger one when the business itself asks for it.

All three are legitimate. What isn't legitimate is choosing without knowing what you give up with each one, because the blast chiller isn't a nice-to-have for a showpiece kitchen: it's the piece of equipment that decides how many custom cake orders you can take and how far ahead you can get before a big peak.

### The Criterion You Decide By

Three things change among the three options: the pieces a day the kitchen's equipment as a whole can turn out, the share of the assortment that depends on going through the blast chiller, and the capacity you have available at peak time, when everybody orders at once.

The rule isn't to look at the most expensive piece of equipment; it's to look at the one that limits you. The "Capacity by Equipment" sheet gives you the pieces a day each machine in the kitchen allows on its own, and the "Bottleneck" sheet tells you which of them calls the shots and with how much room to spare. In the modeled case, the blast chiller allows 480 pieces a day, and that same number, 480, is what sets the ceiling for the whole kitchen: the blast chiller is exactly the bottleneck. The cushion over a normal day is just 4 pieces, and the next-tightest piece of equipment in the kitchen handles 1,036 pieces a day, more than twice as many. There's no contest: if the blast chiller is your kitchen's bottleneck, buying it small isn't saving money, it's setting the ceiling for the whole business, while the rest of the equipment sits waiting for work that will never reach it.

And there's a second criterion, the peak-season one, that weighs as much as the first: without a blast chiller you can't produce ahead, and without producing ahead the big peak (the one that piles up orders all at once) doesn't fit into the hours of a single kitchen day. The blast chiller is what lets you work today to serve three days from now without the product losing quality along the way; without it, every big peak catches you baking in real time, which is the worst way to bake.

With these numbers, the recommendation is clear for anyone planning an assortment where 40% goes through the blast chiller: buy it on day one, with capacity to spare, not sized to the limit. The condition that would change this recommendation is an assortment that brings that 40% down, with no peak-season order that depends on producing ahead; in that case, and only in that case, option C, a smaller one to grow out of later, becomes defensible.

### The Cell That Settles It

The three numbers that settle this decision are on the "Capacity by Equipment" and "Bottleneck" sheets, both in kitchen-capacity-and-site.xlsx: the pieces a day each piece of equipment allows, the share of the assortment that goes through the blast chiller, and which machine calls the shots, with its cushion. And the amount you weigh that decision against the budget with is on the "Startup Costs by Block" sheet of startup-cost-calculator.xlsx, where the blast chiller shows up with its price and the column that says whether that price includes sales tax: $10,037 before sales tax, the case's example price, anchored on a four-tray Irinox from the MultiFresh line in a European dealer's listing (about $10,865 at Texas's maximum combined sales tax rate of 8.25%, and in the US that tax is a cost you don't get back). For a sense of the US range, in October 2026 WebstaurantStore listed reach-in blast chillers at $5,499 (Avantco SF-10) and $21,729 (Irinox MultiFresh Next S). Change the blast chiller's trays per cycle on the "Capacity by Equipment" sheet and the whole kitchen's pieces a day recalculate on the spot, so before you decide, it's worth moving that number and watching where the bottleneck goes.

### The Rule That Applies, When There Is One

Here there is a rule, and it bites harder than it looks: cooked TCS food, such as pastry cream or a custard base, has to cool from 135 °F to 70 °F within 2 hours and down to 41 °F within a total of 6 hours (Food Code 3-501.14; checked in October 2026). That rules out a household chest freezer as a stand-in for a blast chiller from the start: it gets cold, but not that fast with a full, warm batch inside, and it usually isn't certified to NSF/ANSI standards either, which is the simple way to show your inspector a piece of equipment complies (4-205.10). A chest freezer or a reach-in freezer running at 0 °F holds what's already been chilled or frozen; it doesn't do the pull-down for you.

And what comes out of that process gets a label, with no exceptions in your house: the date it was made and the date it went into the freezer. It's the cheapest record you'll keep, and it's what keeps your date marking honest, since ready-to-eat TCS food held refrigerated for more than 24 hours has to be date-marked and can be held at 41 °F or below for no more than 7 days, with the day it was made counting as day one (Food Code 3-501.17). And if your display case holds product you know was frozen and thawed, don't present it as fresh: that's a misleading-claim risk under the general ban on false or misleading labeling (FD&C Act §403(a)), as decision 4 explains.

## 3. Open with Twenty Items or Forty

### The Context of the Decision

In year one the temptation is always the same: open with the biggest menu you can so you don't miss a single sale. It looks like the cautious choice and it's exactly the opposite: it's the fastest way to multiply waste and blow up your purchasing. Every new item isn't just one more tag in the display case; it's one more supplier to manage, a minimum order you take on whether or not you sell it all, a slot in the case you take away from a piece that does turn over, and kitchen time you pay for even if that item barely sells. That fixed cost per item is invisible on paper and very visible on your P&L three months in.

And here's a figure to see how far sales can concentrate in a handful of items: at one well-known European pastry shop, the example in chapter 3 of the guide (with its source), a single item, a croissant selling roughly 3,000 units a week, brings in close to 30% of the shop's total sales. You don't need to get anywhere near that scale to learn the lesson: a short menu that sells a lot of a few things usually earns more than a long one that spreads its sales across thirty items that barely turn over.

### The Options on the Table

There are three ways to approach the opening assortment:

- **Option A**: open with a short menu and expand it with real data after three months, when you already know what turns over and what sits in the case until closing.
- **Option B**: open with the full menu you had in mind and prune it later, when waste and purchasing have already taught you what's surplus.
- **Option C**: a short core menu you don't touch, plus a seasonal rotation that brings variety without cluttering the core of the assortment.

What changes among the three isn't how good your window looks on opening day, but four very concrete things: the waste you'll generate while you learn what each item sells, the opening purchase of ingredients and packaging you have to finance before you ring up a single sale, the kitchen workload at launch (when the team still hasn't found its rhythm), and how clear your display case is, because with too many items it stops saying anything, and with a few well-chosen ones it sells on its own.

### The Criterion You Decide By

The rule isn't gut feeling or a personal taste for having a bit of everything: the margin weighted by the mix rules, not the margin of the single piece. An item with a great margin that barely sells contributes less to the day's result than one with an average margin that turns over constantly. Deciding item by item on gross margin without looking at how much each one weighs in real sales is the most common mistake when putting together an opening menu.

The second criterion is capacity, and it gets forgotten more than it should: the menu has to fit into the pieces a day your kitchen's bottleneck allows, not just into the physical space of the display case. A menu that fits on the counter but not into the production of the team you're going to hire turns into orders you can't fill or shifts that stretch on until the whole system breaks.

### The Cell That Settles It

The "Menu Decision" sheet in opening-menu-and-recipe-costing.xlsx gives the verdict for each item and the weighted margin each group brings in. In the case worked through in this bonus, the screen leaves 27 items with a Keep verdict, 1 with Review price and 2 with Drop. The weighted margin of the items you keep is $1.03, against $0.03 for the ones you drop: the difference isn't subtle, it's what separates a menu that holds up the business from one that drags it down. The average contribution margin per piece across the whole menu comes to $1.07.

The "Mix and Average Ticket" sheet, in the same workbook, tells you how the ticket changes when you shift the mix between groups: it's the one to look at before deciding whether it's worth keeping a borderline item because it pushes the ticket up, or whether it simply doesn't pay. The recommendation, with these numbers in front of you, is clear: drop the two flagged items and review the price of the third before opening; the condition that would change it is that third item proving, with real turnover data after the first few months, a mix strong enough to earn its slot in the case despite its tight margin.

### The Rule That Applies, When There Is One

Here no rule decides how many items your menu should have: it's a business and production-capacity decision, not a legal requirement, and it's worth saying that plainly instead of filling the gap with decorative regulations.

What does apply, item by item, is something else. Before you put any new piece in the case, you need its allergen information settled, because the Food Code asks the permit holder to tell customers in writing which major food allergens are in unpackaged food (3-602.12(C); nine in the US since sesame joined the list on January 1, 2023; checked in October 2026), and you need to have decided whether it goes in the refrigerated case or the room-temperature one, which depends on whether it's a TCS item held at 41 °F or below (3-501.16; the "Egg & TCS Decision" sheet works it out item by item). Every new item you add to the menu is one more row in both, not just one more slot in the case. If you package it yourself in the shop, it also needs the full label: common name, ingredients by weight, net quantity, your name and address and the source of each major allergen (3-602.11(B)). And if one of your custom cakes takes liqueur, it's an ingredient like any other: it goes on the ingredient list of anything you package, and it's worth mentioning when you take the order. Your inspector enforces your state's adopted edition of the Food Code, so check which one applies to you.

## 4. Third-Party Products at Launch: When It Works and What You Can't Claim

### The Context of the Decision

Buying finished product in year one is the fastest way to open with a broad menu on a small investment, and there's nothing shoddy about it: whole formats in this trade work that way from day one, with a full display case and no production kitchen behind it. The problem is that almost nobody realizes this decision doesn't stay in the back room: it affects what you can write on your own window, and that falls under the rules on false or misleading claims, not under advertising style. Whoever buys in without reading this fine print ends up, months later, with a sign they have to change, or with a complaint that changes it for them.

### The Options on the Table

There are three roads on the table, and it's worth naming them this plainly before you look at the numbers:

- Option A: everything made from scratch from day one. A full production kitchen, a trained team and a high investment from the first month.
- Option B: your own core, plus bought-in extras. You make what defines you as a business and buy the rest to fill out the display case.
- Option C: buy frozen or par-baked product and bake it off in the shop. You finish another manufacturer's product; the kitchen shrinks to the minimum needed to finish the bake.

What changes among the three isn't only how what you sell tastes: the equipment investment changes, the margin per piece changes, the kitchen workload your team has to carry changes, and so does the claim you can make on your window and your labels. Those are four different variables, and the three options move them in different directions.

### The Criterion You Decide By

The first criterion is about numbers, and the rule never changes: you compare the margin per piece of your own product against the bought-in one, counting labor in the cost, not just ingredients. The result often surprises, because the bought-in product leaves more dollars per piece and less to set you apart from the shop next door: it's more profitable per unit and, at the same time, easier for any competitor buying from the same supplier to copy.

The second criterion isn't about cost, it's about positioning, and it weighs as much as the first: if the argument you sell your business on is making it yourself, buying finished product eats it. No margin makes up for telling the customer a production-kitchen story and quietly serving them someone else's tray.

For the first criterion, the "Labor Cost per Hour" sheet in opening-menu-and-recipe-costing.xlsx gives you the total cost per unit of each item you make, with labor already in it, which is exactly the figure to compare with the purchase price of a product someone else made. In the butter croissant worked through in chapter 12 of the guide, the total cost per unit is $1.06, of which $0.82 is labor, more than three-quarters of the piece's cost (across the whole menu, labor is 63.3% of total production cost). Leave that component out when you compare against a purchase price and the math is wrong from the start.

For the second criterion, the reference is one you already know from the equipment workbook: the small bake-off shop variant needs $22,456 of equipment, against $66,980 for the base case with a production kitchen, and $85,997 of full opening investment, build-out and permits included, against the base case's $165,800. The "Format Variant" sheet in equipment-and-suppliers-checklist.xlsx tells you, line by line, which equipment you no longer need in each format, and that's where you see clearly how much setting up a production kitchen really weighs against setting up just a bake-off station: the difference between the two figures is the investment you're buying (or not buying) in the ability to make product, not just in square footage. The kitchen hour, when that kitchen exists, costs $17.94.

Recommendation: if your selling point is making it yourself, option A, or at most B; if your selling point is something else (location, hours, price) and the "Labor Cost per Hour" sheet comes out better with bought-in product, option C is defensible. That changes the moment you add a second item with a recipe of your own that matters to the customer: then you redo the math piece by piece, not in one go for the whole menu.

### The Cell That Settles It

The two sheets that settle this decision are the same ones you've already used to build the menu and the equipment list: the "Labor Cost per Hour" sheet in opening-menu-and-recipe-costing.xlsx, which gives you the total cost per unit with labor included, and the "Format Variant" sheet in equipment-and-suppliers-checklist.xlsx, which tells you how much investment you save (or how much production capacity you give up) depending on the format you choose. You don't need a new sheet: it's a matter of crossing what you've already calculated for your menu with the equipment variant you're weighing.

### The Rule That Applies, When There Is One

Here there is a rule, and it's what explains why this decision weighs more than it seems. We found no federal definition of “made from scratch”, “homemade” or “baked here” when we checked in October 2026, and nobody makes you use them: they're voluntary. But what you put on a sign, a label or your website is held to the general ban on false or misleading labeling (FD&C Act §403(a)), and the federal rule on the word “fresh” (21 CFR 101.95) only covers it when it suggests the food is unprocessed; every other use of “fresh” falls under that same general ban.

The practical consequence is direct: if your product comes in frozen from outside and your kitchen just thaws and bakes it, that doesn't entitle you to say “made from scratch”, even if it comes out hot and smells freshly baked, and “baked fresh daily” on product you only thaw and finish is a misleading-claim risk, not a slogan. Putting it up anyway isn't the sign painter's creative license: it's misleading the customer who reads it in your window. Say what you actually do. The table below sums up, format by format, what you can write depending on what really happens behind your counter.

**What you can write in your window, depending on what you actually do**

| What you do | Can you say “MADE FROM SCRATCH”? | Where you can use it |
|---|---|---|
| You make the whole piece in your production kitchen | Yes, if you want: it's voluntary | At your counter, on your labels and on your website |
| You make the base and buy in the filling or the topping | It depends on what the customer would take it to mean: you have to be able to back it up, or say exactly what's yours (“our own croissant dough”) | Same as above, worded to match |
| You buy frozen product from another manufacturer and bake it off | No: baking off someone else's product isn't making it, and “baked fresh daily” on thawed product is a misleading-claim risk | Not applicable: say what you do |
| You buy finished product and cut it into portions or package it | No: cutting or packaging someone else's product isn't making it (and what you package in the shop needs the full label, 3-602.11(B)) | Not applicable |
| You make it in your central production kitchen and sell it in your own shop | Yes: “made from scratch in our kitchen” is true; “baked here” is true only at the site that bakes | At the central kitchen and at every shop you own, worded for each one |
| You make it for a shop or café owned by someone else | That shop can't claim it makes it | Only you, at your own counters |

*Checked in October 2026 against FD&C Act §403(a) and 21 CFR 101.95. We found no federal definition of “made from scratch”: the claim is free and voluntary, nobody makes you use it, but if you use it, it has to be true.*
