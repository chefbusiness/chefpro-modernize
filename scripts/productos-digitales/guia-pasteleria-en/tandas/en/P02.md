## 5. Three-Year Financial Plan

### The Plan's Assumptions

Before projecting a single dollar, this plan states its assumptions, and that order isn't a coincidence: a plan that shows the result first and explains afterward where it comes from can't be defended in front of a lender. The assumptions that hold up the case's three sheets are the tickets we expect to sell each day, the average ticket before sales tax ($5.87), the days open per year, the ramp-up the business starts with in its first months, with the display case half full and a customer base still to build, and the seasonality that spreads those sales across the twelve months, with the peaks and valleys that belong to a pastry shop. Each of those five assumptions lives on the "0. Assumptions" sheet of 3-year-financial-plan.xlsx, and every figure below comes from there: change one and everything that depends on it changes, so it pays to look at them first.

The other assumption that has to be stated, and that almost nobody states, is the margin: the case's cost of sales is projected with the model's single margin rule (a 32% food cost target, so a 68% gross margin), not with the recipe costing of every cake, piece by piece. It's the conservative assumption because it doesn't depend on guessing what the display case sells each day, but on applying a ratio that already picks up production waste and the real mix of custom orders and counter sales. A plan that tried to calculate the margin by adding up the recipe costing of each item would end up overstating the result in the months when the display case doesn't sell exactly what was forecast, which is almost every month.

### The Three-Year P&L

The table below gives the complete P&L, year by year, and three readings matter more than the rest. The first is that one fixed-cost line weighs more than any other in the account, staff, at $92,776 a year (28.8% of steady-state sales), and you look at it before any pricing decision. The second is that the owner's pay shows up as a line of its own in every reading of the result, never tucked inside another cost: it's the pay of whoever sets up the business, $28,800 a year, and it shows as such. In the US, if you're a sole proprietor, the IRS doesn't see that as a salary but as your net earnings from self-employment, and you pay self-employment tax on them (15.3%: 12.4% for Social Security and 2.9% for Medicare); how your pay is taxed depends on how you set up the business, so settle it with your CPA. The third is each year's net profit, after the workbook's illustrative income tax: $4,469 in year one, when the shop is still ramping up, and $27,836 in the steady-state year, when sales run through their full seasonality.

That steady-state result is a net margin of 8.6%. It's worth checking against someone who knows the trade from the inside: Matias, who runs a fourth-generation family pastry business in Spain, founded in 1902, puts the net profitability of a pastry business that's doing well at 8% to 12% (interview in the Spanish press, November 21, 2025). It's a European figure, but the logic travels: this plan's 8.6% falls inside that band, at its low end, which is exactly what a conservative case has to do: not promise the high end. On the US side, Toast's third-party estimates put the food sector's typical net margin at 3% to 5%, higher for specialty shops, so the case's 8.6% is a demanding target, not a modest one.

### Break-Even

Here you have to tell two break-evens apart, because they aren't the same number. Accounting break-even, the point where the result stops being negative, calls for more tickets a day than cash break-even: 155.6 against 148.9. Cash break-even, the one that really matters while a loan is open, calls for 148.9 tickets a day. The gap between the two isn't a calculation error: the accounting one carries depreciation, which you don't pay out to anyone, and the cash one carries loan principal, which does leave the bank. In this plan depreciation ($16,090) weighs more than steady-state principal ($7,979), and that's why the cash threshold sits below the accounting one.

In the steady-state year, actual sales leave a cushion of 22.9% above that cash break-even (183 tickets a day forecast against 148.9). Read it from the plan down: the sales forecast can fall by about 18.6% (1 − 148.9 ÷ 183) before something breaks, because the 22.9% is measured on break-even, not on sales. As a reference, the one published pair we found that passes the margin test is European: a Spanish guide to starting a pastry business (July 12, 2026) pairs fixed costs of 5,000 a month with break-even sales of about 7,200 a month, in its own currency. Divide one by the other and the margin it implies, roughly seven of every ten dollars of sales left after cost of sales, is consistent with the same single margin rule this plan uses, which confirms the model isn't oversized. On the US side, the closest published reference is a food cost of 28% to 35% of sales for a typical bakery (Toast; third-party estimates, not official figures), which means a gross margin of 65% to 72%: the plan's 68% sits inside that range.

### Cash Flow for the First Twelve Months

The table below follows cash month by month through the first year, and that's where the question a lender asks shows up: not how much you make on paper, but whether the cash makes it alive to the next month. The case opens with the $62,115 of working capital from the startup budget, and the lowest cash balance of the first year is $61,346, reached in month 3, just when the ramp-up still hasn't filled the display case with repeat customers and the startup investment has already left the account. From there it climbs, and it closes month 12 at $87,811: a year-one cash flow of $25,696.

Within that same cash flow there's an effect worth understanding before a slow month scares you, and in the US it isn't tax. A European plan fronts VAT on its production kitchen equipment and gets it back through later returns; here nothing comes back, because the sales tax you pay on equipment and taxable build-out is a cost (about $13,322 at Texas's maximum combined rate of 8.25%, shown for illustration and not added to the case), and the case's counter sales, sold to go, are exempt in Texas. What moves this table is timing: ingredients are paid at 30 days, payroll goes out at $7,731.34 every month (twelve equal payrolls, with no extra-pay months), and the loan payment grows once its six interest-only months are over. That's why the month the cash bottoms out doesn't necessarily match the month with the biggest accounting expense.

### Financing and Debt Service

The case's sources of funds combine owner's equity and a loan: $167,916 of the owner's own money and $60,000 borrowed, which together cover the $227,916 of startup cash. At $60,000 the loan is above the SBA Microloan ceiling ($50,000), so it would be an SBA 7(a) loan or a conventional bank loan; the 6.2% rate and the term (84 monthly payments, the first six interest-only) are assumptions of the case, not a quote. That loan sets the monthly payment of $936.57, the figure you have to be able to pay every month from month 7, once the interest-only period ends, through the last payment. It's also the figure that explains why cash break-even doesn't match accounting break-even: that payment leaves the account month after month, whether or not the business made money that particular month.

The tightest debt service coverage ratio (DSCR) in the whole schedule is 3.24, and here there's a reading that often gets missed: the number that matters isn't the first year's but the worst year's across the whole horizon of the plan. In this case they happen to be the same, year one, the ramp-up year, even with the six interest-only months; but a business can start with room to spare and tighten later if seasonality shifts or if a month of replacing production kitchen equipment lands in a sales valley, and looking only at the opening leaves out precisely the year the loan squeezes hardest.

## 6. Risks and Scenarios

### The Three Scenarios

The model works with three snapshots of the same business: same space, same production kitchen, same team, same prices. The only difference is how much sells each month. In the pessimistic scenario revenue stays at $211,120 and net profit falls to -$42,784: real red ink, not one bad month. In the optimistic one, net profit rises to $97,579, with the same cost structure underneath. The realistic scenario sits between the two extremes, and it's the one to read when you make decisions: the pessimistic and optimistic ones are the assumptions that set the edges of the range, “what if it goes badly” and “what if it goes very well”, not the forecast the business is set up on. The table below breaks down all three with the same team and the same fixed costs.

Why the pessimistic one hurts so much: the team is the same in all three scenarios. The Head Pastry Chef, the Pastry Cook, the Bakery Assistant and the two Counter Associates get paid the same whether the counter sells a lot or a little, and the rent and the loan payment for the equipment don't drop a dollar either. That's why staff cost as a share of sales shoots up to 43.9% in the pessimistic scenario: it isn't that the team costs more, it's that there are fewer sales to spread that fixed cost over. A business with a high fixed structure can't cushion a bad stretch by cutting costs at the same time revenue falls: the whole hit lands on net profit.

### Opening Risks and How to Mitigate Them

Before you ring up your first dollar, there are four risks that can push the opening back by weeks, and all four have a date:

- The space you choose can't take the hood your ovens need.
- City or county permits take longer than planned.
- The equipment reaches the production kitchen late.
- The build-out runs over its original budget.

Each one is mitigated by putting a date and a written answer on it before it can hurt you, and each has its own sheet in the pack. The hood gets settled before you sign: the hood type follows the appliance, its listing and the mechanical code your city has adopted (a deck oven usually goes under a Type I hood with its own grease duct and fire suppression, while an enclosed electric oven may need only a Type II, or none if it's listed to UL 710B), and the first two items on the "Site Visit Checklist" sheet of kitchen-capacity-and-site.xlsx are deal-breakers whose answer you get in writing from the building department. Permits are planned, not hoped for: the 30 steps on the "Permits Checklist (P1-P6)" sheet of permits-and-licenses-checklist.xlsx and its "Timeline and Critical Path" sheet add up planning assumptions with room to spare, because the only legal clock among the four example states is California's (20 working days for plan review, or the plans are deemed approved), and in Florida you apply to the Department of Agriculture (FDACS) 21 days before you open. The "Equipment" sheet of equipment-and-suppliers-checklist.xlsx records each machine's lead time and shows which one drives the opening calendar. Of the lines you're actually ordering, the longest lead time is the roll-in retarder-proofer's: nine weeks, and it's the first thing to order (for reference, one US dealer listed a one-section roll-in as usually shipping in 6 weeks). In this timeline it doesn't set the opening date (equipment ordering and delivery is the only milestone with slack) precisely because it's ordered in parallel with the build-out; the day it's ordered late, it takes over. Build-out overruns are tracked in the pack's own startup cost calculator, startup-cost-calculator.xlsx, comparing each line against what was budgeted instead of waiting for the final invoice to find out.

### Operating Risks

Once the doors are open, the risks change in nature. Four weigh more than the rest:

- The big campaign doesn't fit in the production kitchen.
- Staff cost eats the margin.
- Ingredient prices go up.
- You can't find skilled staff when you need them.

The "Peak Capacity vs Demand" sheet of seasonal-peaks-planner.xlsx flags 6 campaigns that the production kitchen, as it's sized, can't handle without adjusting shifts or farming out part of the production: knowing that before the campaign keeps you from promising a volume that won't come out of the oven. Staff cost is watched against that same 43.9% of the pessimistic scenario: if the display case gets close to that figure without being in the worst month of the year, it's the sign that the staff schedule is oversized for what you're selling. And in the US that line can move without you hiring anyone: the minimum wage is set by your state and often by your city, and it changes (the Department of Labor's state table was last updated July 1, 2026, and Florida's rate was $14.00 until September 29, 2026; check FloridaCommerce for the current one). The case's own wage table, $8.60 to $12.50 an hour, clears the $7.25 that applies in Texas but would be below the legal minimum in California, New York or Florida, so redo it with your own figures. There's no fixed ingredient price to give here, but butter shows how far prices move: the same USDA report that put CME Grade AA butter at $1.3650 a pound on October 16, 2026 showed producer prices for butter in August 32.5% lower than a year earlier (USDA AMS, Dairy Market News), and what drops that fast can climb just as fast. Keep in mind, too, Matias's family business in Mallorca, fourth generation, with 47 employees: its owner explains that in high season production doesn't rise a little, it jumps to a much higher multiple than the rest of the year (interview in the Spanish press, November 21, 2025), and that jump is exactly what can leave a production kitchen sized only for the day-to-day short of ingredients. For the staff shortage, the mitigation isn't a number but a calendar: open the search well ahead of the campaign date, not once it has started, budget for what the market pays (the national median for bakers is $17.86 an hour, BLS, May 2025), and remember that seasonal help under 16 can't do any baking and help under 18 can't run a power-driven mixer or dough sheeter (Department of Labor, Hazardous Occupations Order No. 11 and Fact Sheet #43).

One more operating risk is specific to the US and costs nothing to prevent if you set it up before opening: sales tax follows how you sell, not what you sell. The case's to-go counter sales are exempt in Texas, but the day you serve on your own plates or with your own utensils (in California, New York and Florida, generally the day you add tables), part of the same menu becomes taxable, so the POS has to tag every sale as to-go or for-here from the first day (chapter 19 of the guide).

### The Contingency Plan

If the pessimistic scenario does materialize, the levers kick in in this order: first you cut the low-margin items from the menu, then you adjust the staff schedule to real sales, and only if that isn't enough do you renegotiate the loan term with your lender. You don't pull all three at once, and you don't start with the debt: it's the lever that leaves you the least room to maneuver once you've used it.

Everything above is a complete case, filled in so you can use it as the template for your own business: none of its figures is a forecast of the results of whoever uses it; it's the model each person calculates their own with.
