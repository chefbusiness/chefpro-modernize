## 2. Your Customer and Your Market: Who Buys Pastry and When

### What US Households Spend and What Income Changes

Before you look at a single storefront, look at the number underneath it all: how much US households spend on bread and bakery goods. According to the BLS Consumer Expenditure Survey, the average household (a "consumer unit" in BLS terms) spent $539 on bakery products for home in 2024. The figure moves from year to year ($497 in 2022, $574 in 2023), but it never goes away. It's a broad market figure, not a bakery-shop figure: it counts every loaf of sandwich bread and every box of muffins bought for home, wherever it was bought, the supermarket included. Even so, it tells you something you need to be clear on from day one: the demand is there, and it isn't a passing whim but a habit built into the grocery budget.

What that figure DOESN'T tell you is that you'll live off big purchases. $539 a year spread over the calendar comes to about $10 a week per household, which makes it, by definition, small, repeat spending: the customer walks in, buys two or three pieces and comes back a few days later. It isn't a furniture ticket or an appliance ticket; it's a coffee-shop ticket. Whoever opens a pastry shop picturing customers who drop fifty dollars in one go is in for a surprise, and you plan for that surprise in the number of customers you need each day, not in how much each one spends.

Inside that average there's a point about income that does change one concrete decision. The $539 is an average across every household, and it lumps supermarket sandwich bread together with pastry from a production kitchen, so it doesn't tell you how much a given neighborhood spends at a bakery like yours: how much bread a neighborhood eats doesn't predict the ticket you'll ring up there. What does change with the income of the neighborhood is the price it's willing to pay per piece and how often it places a custom order instead of buying from the display case. Put another way: the location you choose moves your average ticket more than the menu you put on the counter. You can sell the same strawberries & cream cake in two shops fifteen minutes apart and charge different prices for it because the neighborhoods pay differently, and that room to maneuver is worth more than any recipe tweak.

Brought down to the case modeled in this guide's pack, annual sales in a steady-state year are $322,198, with an average ticket of $5.87 before sales tax and 2.6 pieces per ticket. That ticket doesn't come from any industry average: we found no public, checkable figure for the average ticket at a US bakery, so treat any number that circulates under that name without a source as a guess. The case's ticket comes from its own menu mix, its own prices and its own assortment of pieces, and that's exactly what you have to build with your own menu before you trust anyone else's number. When you change your product mix, your ticket changes with it; there's no "market" ticket to converge on.

### Only a Small Share of Spending Reaches the Specialty Bakery

There's no official US figure for how much of that household bakery spending goes through specialty bakeries and how much through supermarkets, big-box stores and everything else, and we won't make one up. Any split you see quoted online should be treated as an order of magnitude at best, never as a basis for sizing your business or for working out how many people you need to attract. What you can use is the shape, not the decimal: the $539 is spent wherever households buy their groceries, so the weekly loaf, the bagged rolls and the box of muffins go into the same cart as the rest of the shopping, in the supermarket aisle or at its in-store bakery, and only a fraction of that money ever reaches an independent bakery that bakes its own products.

The commercial consequence is direct: the specialty shop doesn't compete for the family's weekly shopping. That weekly purchase is already settled in the supermarket aisle, at a low price and with no line. What the production kitchen can win is the occasion: the birthday, Sunday lunch, guests coming over, the First Communion, Christmas. That explains why weekends and peak seasons weigh so heavily in a pastry shop's sales and why an ordinary Monday weighs so little: on Monday there's no occasion, there's routine, and the supermarket already has the routine covered.

There's a mistake that comes up again and again in what you read for free online, and it needs saying plainly: multiplying the households around your shop by a national spending average does NOT give you your market. The $539 is all the bread and bakery goods bought for home, supermarket included, and the $3,945 the average household spent on food away from home in 2024 (same survey) is every restaurant meal, coffee and snack eaten out, not your category. They're different categories with different buying behavior, and mixing them leads to the wrong conclusions about how many people "should" be buying at your shop. The figure that matters here is the specific one for your category and your location, labeled with how reliable it is, not the generic one for the whole shopping cart.

### The Real Competitor Is Frozen Dough

If the supermarket takes the routine purchase, you have to ask who else is taking the morning sale, the coffee with something sweet before work. The answer isn't the bakery across the street: it's the supermarket's in-store bakery and the café on the corner baking off frozen product. The closest official series is the BLS count for frozen cakes, pies and other pastries manufacturing (NAICS 311813): 309 plants and 13,541 jobs in 2024, up from 236 plants and 12,619 jobs in 2018 (BLS Quarterly Census of Employment and Wages, QCEW). In plants, that's 31% more in six years; in jobs, 7% more. Over the same years, retail bakeries, the shops that bake and sell to the public (NAICS 311811), went from 7,787 establishments to 9,473 (22% more) and from 86,819 jobs to 104,527 (20% more).

That long series is what changes the conversation, as long as you read both lines. It counts plants and jobs, not sales, so it gives you a direction, not a market size, and the direction is clear: this isn't one good year for the frozen industry but a trend sustained over six years, adding plants faster than the shops that bake by hand add storefronts, and putting ready-to-bake trays in every in-store bakery, every convenience store with a coffee counter and every café that wants to sell pastries without a kitchen of its own. It's a competitor with industrial capacity, economies of scale and a presence in places where you'll never be able to open a shop. The good news is in the other line: the shops that bake on site are growing too, and they're adding people faster than the plants are.

You don't compete with that competitor on price or on volume: you'd lose on both, guaranteed. You compete on product that doesn't survive industrial freezing and thawing (laminated dough fresh out of the oven, cream whipped that morning, the filling that breaks down if you freeze it), on custom orders that need a personal touch, and on the peaks that call for concentrated volume over a few days, which industry can't deliver with the freshness the customer expects. Everything that can be frozen, shipped and baked off in an in-store bakery has already been taken from you by someone with more scale than you; what can't be done that way is exactly where your business lives.

Here's a storefront lever that's yours and costs nothing: saying where your product is made. “Baked here every morning” or “made from scratch in our kitchen” can only be said truthfully by whoever bakes the product in their own production kitchen, and it's precisely what the in-store bakery and the café baking off frozen trays can't say with the same truth. It isn't a defined legal term in the US; what applies is the general ban on false or misleading labeling (FD&C Act §403(a)), which is why “baked fresh daily” on product you only thaw and finish is a risk, as chapter 17 on channels explains. It's free, it's legitimate if you bake, and it's the simplest commercial argument you have against a competitor that wins on scale but loses on origin.

### Why a Production Kitchen Doesn't Compete on Volume

All of the above has to be translated into real capacity, because a commercial argument is useless if you don't have pieces to sell. The equipment in the case modeled in this guide has a ceiling of pieces a day: that's what there is, and working faster or pushing the team doesn't fix it. When you get close to that ceiling, the only lever left isn't making more pieces (the oven has nothing more to give) but making every piece that leaves the kitchen bring in more margin: a better menu mix, a better price for each occasion, more weight on custom orders against impulse buys at the display case. It's the same logic that separates the production kitchen from the frozen competitor: you don't win on quantity, you win on what each unit leaves behind.

That logic of limited capacity fits another reality of the business that you have to plan for from day one: seasonality. In the modeled case, the highest month of the year is January, carried by the Rosca de Reyes, with $35,979 in sales, 11.2% of the annual total; the lowest is August, with $17,452, 5.4% of the year (the case closes for two weeks that month). January brings in more than twice what August does, and it's worth reading next to the figure for an average day of the year, which brings in $1,074: a business with that curve isn't planned around the monthly average but around real cash, month by month, because the slow month comes anyway and you need cash to carry you through it even if the strong month makes up for the whole year. December (1.24) and November (1.08) pick up Christmas and Thanksgiving, the two big US holiday rushes, which the case counts in its monthly curve rather than as peaks of their own. The table below breaks down each month's weight in the total, and it's worth a calm look before you sign any lease with the good month in mind.

And here comes the location decision that closes this line of reasoning. Before you sign anything, go out and count how many bakeries that bake from scratch there are within the walk (or, in a city where people drive, the short drive) your customers will actually make to your future shop, not how many “bakeries” show up on a map search. That search lumps the real production kitchen together with the counter that sells reheated frozen trays under a sign that says “bakery”: they're different businesses, with different cost structures, and only one of the two can honestly say “baked here every morning”. The official statistics draw the same line: in 2024 the US had 9,473 retail bakeries (NAICS 311811), the ones that bake and sell, 615 of them in Texas, 1,378 in California, 988 in New York and 457 in Florida, against 2,605 baked goods retailers (NAICS 445291) that sell what someone else bakes (BLS QCEW). Count your competition wrong and you start the business with the wrong map in your hand.

## 3. The Opening Menu: Thirty Items and Why These

### How to Decide an Opening Assortment

An opening assortment isn't decided by taste or by what you're best at making. I've seen whole menus built around the head pastry chef's specialty burn through the cash in the first few months because nobody stopped to look at the other two filters. The order matters, and it goes like this: first, what fits in your production kitchen with the equipment and the hands you'll have on day one; second, what holds up in the display case without losing color, texture or margin to what you throw away; and third, what actually leaves a margin, not what sounds good in your head. If an item doesn't pass all three filters in that order, it doesn't go on the opening menu, however well you make it.

This chapter's modeled case works with thirty items spread across five families, and it needs saying right away: they're example data, not the menu you have to copy. Your shop will swap seasonal items for local ones (king cake, pan de muerto, Thanksgiving pies); the costing method is the same. Three of the thirty come straight from the Food Cost Kit Pro (the 05-bakery-cake-pricing workbook, its "Croissants", "Macarons" and "Chocolate Cake" sheets), on purpose, so that if you own both products you're costing the same croissant with the same recipe, there in US units, instead of two versions of it; the other twenty-seven are assumptions built so the model has something to calculate. There isn't a single recipe or kitchen process in here: what this chapter decides is what you sell and at what margin, not how you make it.

And here's the first judgment call, the one that saves the most money in year one: opening with fewer items than you think you need is almost always right. Every item you add multiplies ingredients in the walk-in, time in the kitchen and the risk of waste in the display case, and your first-month customer doesn't choose among thirty options: they choose among the four or five they see first and that you've taught them are always there. Thirty items is already a tight assortment for a case with defined capacity; if your team is smaller, the list gets shorter, not longer.

So you don't decide by eye, the workbook includes a menu traffic light on the "Menu Decision" sheet. Across the modeled case's thirty items, the verdicts split like this: 27 items come out as Keep, 1 as Review price and 2 as Drop. The ones you keep, you leave alone: they stay in the case exactly as they are, because they meet margin and turnover at the same time. With the one that needs a price review, the problem isn't the item itself; it's that its current price isn't capturing the margin its turnover would allow: you raise the selling price and check the mix again, without touching the recipe costing. And with the ones to drop, the decision is literal: they don't go on the opening menu, because neither margin nor turnover holds them up, and keeping them just to round out the assortment means financing a loss with the cash from the ones that work. ### The Five Families and the Role of Each

The five families on the opening menu don't compete with each other for space in the display case; each one does a different job, and confusing those jobs is the most expensive mistake I've seen first-time owners make:

- **Viennoiserie.** It brings the customer in every morning and builds repeat business: the butter croissant, the custard *napolitana* (cream-filled laminated pastry), the *ensaïmada* (Mallorcan spiral pastry), the kind of piece a customer buys without thinking because they already know it's good and that it's always there. It isn't the family that leaves the most margin per unit, but it's the one that keeps the door swinging.
- **Individual pastries.** This is where the ticket goes up: the display piece with the highest unit price, the one the customer adds to the breakfast pastry or buys on its own in the afternoon. It's the engine of the modeled case's average ticket.
- **Cakes and entremets.** This is the custom order and the peak season: birthdays, celebrations, the busiest weeks of the year. It isn't daily counter sales; it's scheduled sales, and that's why you manage its capacity differently from the everyday display case.
- **Breads.** Its job is traffic, not margin: it brings in the customer who only came for a baguette and walks out with a croissant or a pastry as well. And it has one detail you can't overlook: bread is the item you're most likely to sell bagged, and anything you package yourself needs a full label.
- **Seasonal.** Rosca de Reyes (Three Kings bread), almond turrón (nougat), *Mona de Pascua* (Catalan Easter cake), panettone: this is where the year is won or lost. A well-run seasonal push can outweigh the rest of the menu combined in those weeks' sales, and a badly run one leaves you with expensive waste right when ingredient costs peak.

If you decide to put bread on your opening menu, be clear about what it changes and what it doesn't. What it changes: the sandwich loaf, sliced and bagged, is food you package yourself, and packaged food needs a full label with its common name, ingredients by weight, net quantity, your name and address and the major allergens (FDA Food Code 3-602.11), which this guide's regulatory appendix covers. What it doesn't change, unlike under a VAT system: your tax rate. In the four example states, bread and pastries sold to go are generally exempt from sales tax alike, and what makes either one taxable is how you sell it (on a plate, at your tables), which chapter 19 covers. It isn't just a display-case decision, so if you're going to include bread, read those two sections before you set the price.

Now for the column that turns all of this from a list of intentions into a number: the mix column. The percentage of units you expect to sell of each item is an editable cell, not a fixed figure in the workbook. You decide how much weight each of the thirty items gets based on your location, your hours and your customers, and from that column (not from an industry average, which we couldn't find as reliable public data for bakeries) come the average ticket and the food cost of the whole menu. Change the mix and both numbers change. That's why the modeled case's average ticket, $5.87 before sales tax, is the result of one specific mix, not a figure you can carry over to your own pastry shop without running it through your own mix column first.

### A Single Item Can Bring In Almost a Third of the Register

Here's the figure that should most change the way you look at your own menu. At Hofmann, a well-known pastry shop in Barcelona, a single item, the mascarpone croissant, at roughly 3,000 units a week, accounts for close to 30% of the shop's total sales, according to The New Barcelona Post (February 2026). It isn't a rare case or the quirk of a famous name: in pastry, sales concentrated in a handful of items are the rule, not an accident. There are almost always one or two pieces that pull the rest of the menu along, and the rest are there to give variety, not to bring in the same money.

And from that comes the operational consequence that changes how you treat your star item from day one: if one piece brings in that share of your register, its recipe costing, its supplier and your production capacity stop being kitchen details and become business risk. A costing error on your best seller isn't a small mistake repeated thirty times a month; it's a big one repeated thousands of times. A single supplier for its key ingredient, with no alternative, is a point of failure that can stop your most profitable product overnight. And kitchen capacity stretched to the limit for that item is the first thing to break when seasonal demand climbs. Before you settle your opening assortment, figure out which of your thirty (or of the ones you define) is going to play that role, and treat it with the care you give to what holds up the register, not the care you give to one more piece in the case.

On seasonal trends, one point worth keeping in mind, with only the numbers we can stand behind: in the US, the Rosca de Reyes isn't a one-day item. It's the same rosca that Mexican bakeries across the country sell for Three Kings Day, and it shares the calendar with the king cake, whose season runs from Epiphany (January 6) to Mardi Gras; US searches average about 90,500 a month for “rosca de reyes” and 110,000 for “king cake” (DataForSEO, October 2026). If your opening menu includes seasonal items, plan for a season that stretches past its date on the calendar. Smaller sizes and gluten-free or dairy-free versions are worth trying too, but we have no US figure for how big that demand is, so test them before you commit production to them.

### Margin by Family: the Cookie Earns Twice What the Cake Does

The most common mistake when you look at a menu is to focus on each item's margin without looking at how much that item weighs in the mix. A piece with a big unit margin that hardly anyone buys contributes little to the register; a piece with a tight margin that sells all day long can contribute more than the first one. What sorts the assortment decision table is the weighted margin: each item's margin multiplied by its weight in the mix, not the margin on its own. That's why two items with the same contribution margin per piece can get opposite verdicts on the menu traffic light: the one that turns over stays, and the one that doesn't gets a price review or gets dropped.

In the modeled case, the average contribution margin per piece across the whole menu is $1.07, and the weighted average selling price is $2.26 before sales tax. They're averages of the full mix, and here's the distinction you really need to understand before you go on with the rest of this guide: the ingredient food cost of the whole menu is 17.6%, but the served food cost, the one that includes display-case waste and to-go packaging, climbs to 20.3%. Those almost three points of difference are the first proof that an item's recipe costing, calculated with ingredients alone, isn't the real cost of your business. What you throw out of the case and what the box or bag the product leaves in costs you are as much a cost as flour or butter, and anyone who sets prices from the recipe costing alone is working with a number that falls short. For contrast, third-party estimates put a typical US bakery's food cost at 28% to 35% of sales (Toast); the case's percentages come from its own modeled prices and ingredient costs, so check yours on the "Batch Costing" sheet before you lean on them.

That also explains why, at the same percentage margin, the small, repeat piece of viennoiserie or individual pastry usually does more for the register than a cake or a custom entremet. A cake carries far more labor per unit sold: each cake is practically a one-off from start to finish, while the small piece is made by the batch and spreads that kitchen time over many units. That difference in labor per unit is laid out in detail in the recipe costing chapter, line by line; here it's enough to know that a cake's percentage margin doesn't tell the whole story unless you put the labor it took to make right next to it.

The decision rule that closes this section is the same one that's already been showing up in the menu traffic light: you drop what reaches neither margin nor turnover, because it adds nothing to the register and does take up space in the case and in the kitchen. You review the price of what has a low margin but turns over well, because there's room there to capture more margin without losing sales. And you don't touch what already turns over and already leaves a margin: those are the items that hold up your menu, and touching them for no reason is the kind of decision you regret six months later. How this rule becomes menu engineering, with a full matrix and a batch costing method, is the subject of the Food Cost + Menu Engineering Guide; here we've only set the criterion you use to decide what goes on your opening menu and what doesn't.
