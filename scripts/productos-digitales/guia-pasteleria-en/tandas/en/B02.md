## 5. How Many Roscas de Reyes to Bake in Year One

### The Context of the Decision

The Rosca de Reyes (Three Kings bread) packs a huge share of the year's sales into a handful of days, and in year one you size it blind: you have no sales history of your own to go on, so any quota you set is a bet. Bake too few and you leave sales on the sidewalk on the very day the most people walk through your door; bake too many and your star product turns into waste on the afternoon of January 6, just when there's nobody left to sell it to. It isn't a margin you can correct halfway through the campaign: by the time you see you've come up short, the peak is over.

And the size of the jump isn't small. Matias, the Mallorca baker you met in chapters 13 to 15 of the guide, describes it from inside the trade in an interview published in Spain on November 21, 2025: his *ensaïmadas* (Mallorcan spiral pastries) go from 25-30 a day in the low season to 150-200 in high season, individual pastries go from 50-60 to 500-600, and at Christmas his kitchen turns out between 7,000 and 8,000 bars of turrón (almond nougat). It's a European example, but the message underneath applies to your rosca: a peak multiplies production by 5 to 10 times, not by 1.5, and planning it as an ordinary day with a few more customers is the fastest way to come up short or to throw product away.

The size of the occasion is no reason to relax either. In the US the rosca shares its season with the king cake, which runs from Epiphany (January 6) to Mardi Gras, and US searches average about 90,500 a month for "rosca de reyes" and 110,000 for "king cake" (DataForSEO, October 2026). That's interest, not units sold, but it tells you customers go looking for the occasion. If you also offer a filled rosca, with whipped cream or chocolate truffle, that pulls part of your production toward the version that's hardest to hold, as you'll see below. And the competition doesn't come only from other bakeries: the supermarket in-store bakeries and the chains near you chase the same occasion, so check what they charge before you set your price. With them on the shelf, there isn't much room to get the quota wrong out of excess caution either.

### The Options on the Table

You have three ways to approach the quota for the big campaign:

- **Option A, made to order with a booking deadline.** The customer orders and pays (or leaves a deposit) before a cut-off date, and you bake exactly what's been ordered. Zero waste, but it takes customers who trust you enough to order ahead, and if your message about the deadline doesn't get through, people are left without a rosca on the key day.
- **Option B, a fixed quota sold over the counter until it runs out.** You bake a set number and sell it at the counter until it's gone. It serves the customer who decides on the spot, but all the risk of getting the volume right is yours: overshoot and the waste is yours; fall short and so is the lost sale.
- **Option C, a mix of the two.** You set aside one quota for orders and keep another fixed quota for the counter. It splits the risk between the two: you cover the loyal demand that orders in good time and leave room for last-minute sales, without committing all your production to a single route.

What changes from one option to another isn't only the risk of waste. The cash you put up front for ingredients changes too (ordering ahead lets you buy against firm orders, while a fixed quota you buy against a forecast), and so does the need for extra staff on the heaviest production days, which exists in all three options but gets sized differently depending on how much you know in advance.

### The Criterion You Decide By

The rule is simple to state and demanding to apply: the quota is set by your production kitchen's real capacity on those specific days, not by commercial ambition or by what you'd like to sell. First you work out the daily deficit (the gap between what the market would ask of you and what your current team can turn out), and then you decide which lever covers it: extra staff, widening the order option to spread production over time, or accepting a lower quota and taking it as year one's limit.

The second criterion is about cash, and it weighs as much as the production one. The campaign's ingredients are bought weeks before you get paid for them: flour, butter, candied fruit and, if you sell filled roscas, the cream or truffle filling pile up in your storeroom while the money from those sales still hasn't reached the register. If you can't finance that gap without choking, the quota has to come down even if the market's demand would allow more.

Like every decision in this bonus, this one closes with an explicit recommendation and the condition that would change it: if your team can be stretched with short-term extra staff without putting your cash at risk, the quota goes up to the limit set by the reinforced kitchen's capacity; if the extra staff doesn't pay or your cash can't carry the up-front ingredient purchase, the quota stays at what the current team produces on its own, and the rest of the demand goes to the order option with its booking deadline.

### The Cell That Settles It

The "Peak Capacity vs Demand" sheet in seasonal-peaks-planner.xlsx is the one that answers the question with concrete numbers. For the Three Kings Day campaign, that sheet gives a demand of 2,856 pieces a day, a real team capacity that leaves a deficit of 1,656 pieces a day, and it tells you that baking the whole demand (less the 60 pieces you can make ahead) would take 47.4 kitchen hours a day, against the 20 the case already runs during the campaign. That number of hours is what turns a sales problem into a staffing problem: no pastry shop that's just starting out has a team that can put in 47.4 kitchen hours in a single day, so the real decision isn't “how much do I sell?” but “how much do I cover with extra staff, and how much do I send to orders?”

The same workbook, on the "Extra Staff and Cash" sheet, puts a price on the part you decide to cover with extra hands: $1,603.74 of extra staff for the campaign, and $2,079.04 of cash tied up in ingredients while you wait to get paid. That tied-up cash is, in practice, the second criterion turned into a number: if your financing can't cover that $2,079.04 without squeezing the rest of the month, the quota comes down before you touch a single production figure. And on the other side is the reward: the campaign brings in $12,000 of incremental sales before sales tax, the figure that justifies taking on the cost of the extra staff and the tied-up cash if your kitchen can hold the pace.

The calendar of dates and tasks for each campaign (when to start announcing orders, when to close the booking deadline, when the extra staff comes in) belongs in your annual task calendar and your daily task checklists, outside this pack: here you settle the volume decision, not the operating schedule for carrying it out. And if your extra hands are high school students on winter break, remember chapter 14 of the guide: under 16 they can't bake, and under 18 they can't run the mixers or the dough sheeter.

### The Rule That Applies, When There Is One

Here no rule caps how many pieces you can bake for the big campaign: the ceiling is set by your kitchen, not by the law. But there is a rule on how you get ahead with cold, which is the most common lever for spreading out over time what the counter will ask for all at once. Cooked TCS food, such as pastry cream or a custard base, has to cool from 135 °F to 70 °F within 2 hours and down to 41 °F within a total of 6 hours (Food Code 3-501.14; checked in October 2026): that's the blast chiller's job, not a chest freezer's. And every batch you freeze ahead goes out with three dates on its label: the day it was made, the day it went into the freezer and the use-by date you set in your own food safety plan. In the US that label is a house practice, not a federal rule, but it's what keeps your date marking honest, since ready-to-eat TCS food held refrigerated for more than 24 hours has to be date-marked and can be held at 41 °F or below for no more than 7 days, with the day it was made counting as day one (Food Code 3-501.17).

And there's a second limit that overrides any production plan, whether you freeze or not: a rosca filled with whipped cream or chocolate truffle isn't shelf-stable, and it has its own holding ceiling, 41 °F or below (Food Code 3-501.16). In the modeled case, in fact, the "Egg & TCS Decision" sheet in opening-menu-and-recipe-costing.xlsx puts the rosca in the refrigerated case. If that many roscas don't fit in your refrigerated display case, you don't bake that many, however much demand the "Peak Capacity vs Demand" sheet is showing you. The cold capacity of your display case is, together with kitchen hours, the second physical limit that decides the campaign's final quota.

## 6. Taking a First Communion Cake Order with No Track Record

### The Context of the Decision

The First Communion and graduation season isn't a three-day peak: it's a plateau of 20 campaign days, spread from mid-April to mid-June, and in year one it arrives while the production kitchen is still breaking in, with a team that's just been trained and routines that haven't settled. Over those 20 days you don't just produce more, you also tie up cash: there are 55 days in which that campaign's money sits idle, between the ingredients you've bought, the extra staff you've paid and the payment that hasn't come in in full yet. Adding staff to carry that plateau costs $801.87, and that number isn't a whim of the spreadsheet: it's what gets expensive when nobody decided in time how many orders to accept.

The first big First Communion order is almost always accepted out of excitement (someone asks for a large cake, with lots of servings, for a very specific date) and it gets made at night, once the rest of the day's production has already gone out. What you have to decide before you say yes isn't whether you can make that cake: it's how much of it fits without blowing up everything else already promised for that day.

### The Options on the Table

You need to have picked your model before the phone rings with the first First Communion order, because the three options don't compete for the same reason:

- Option A: accept everything that comes in, with no quota and no screening. It's the most exciting in the short term and the one that eats the most nights in the kitchen.
- Option B: accept up to a fixed daily quota, set by the team's real capacity and not by how eager the customer is to book.
- Option C: accept only items that are already on your menu with a cost card done, turning down the special order that asks for something you've never made.

What changes among the three isn't the income on the day of the order: it's what it drags into the next day. The kitchen hours committed at night change, the risk of the customer canceling at the last minute changes, and so does the waste left over if, in the end, they don't come to pick up the cake.

### The Criterion You Decide By

With no track record (and a pastry shop's first year is, by definition, without one) the rule has two legs. The first: you accept what fits in the production kitchen's capacity on THAT day, with the equipment you've already bought. The modeled case's set of equipment allows 480 pieces on a normal 8-hour kitchen day (600 during this campaign, when the "Peak Capacity vs Demand" sheet runs the kitchen 10 hours a day), and neither the excitement of a big order nor the customer's insistence moves that ceiling. The second: you only accept items that already have a cost card. No cost card means no price, and no price means what you're handing over is a gift dressed up as an order.

The second criterion, besides the quota, is the deposit. It isn't an administrative formality: it covers the cost of the ingredients if the customer never comes back, and along the way it filters out the one who has no real intention of picking up the cake. The modeled case assumes a 30% deposit at booking (it's noted on the custom-orders row of the "Channels and Break-Even" sheet): that's the model's assumption, not a standard. A big order without a deposit is, in practice, betting that everyone keeps their word on a Saturday in May with the kitchen running flat out.

To calibrate how much quota and how much extra staff make sense on the First Communion and graduation plateau, there are two figures you read together: the custom-order channel's contribution margin is 68%, on that channel's annual sales of $45,108. With that margin in front of you, you know whether it's worth pushing the quota a little beyond what's comfortable, or whether the campaign's extra staff already pays for itself with what comes in, without squeezing any harder.

### The Cell That Settles It

The numbers behind this decision are spread across two workbooks. The "Extra Staff and Cash" sheet in seasonal-peaks-planner.xlsx gives the days the First Communion and graduation campaign's cash sits idle and the cost of adding staff on those days. The "Channels and Break-Even" sheet in 3-year-financial-plan.xlsx gives the custom-order channel's contribution margin and what that channel sells in a year, which is what lets you compare whether the extra staff pays or not.

With those two figures in front of you, the recommendation is Option B: a fixed daily quota based on the production kitchen's real capacity, to be reviewed only once you have a track record of your own covering at least one full First Communion and graduation season. The condition that would change it: if the custom-order channel's contribution margin fell, consistently, below what it costs to carry the plateau's extra staff, then protecting yourself with Option C (only menu items you've already costed) weighs more than forcing the sales of Option A.

The order form and the order log, with their own schedule, don't need inventing here: they belong with your daily task checklists, outside this pack, and that's where you write down the day's quota, the deposit taken and the pickup date of each First Communion order.

### The Rule That Applies, When There Is One

Here no rule decides your deposits or your cancellations: they're house policy, and they have to be in writing before you accept your first order, not negotiated in the heat of the moment with the customer standing at the counter. For reference, many US bakeries that publish their policies ask 50% to hold the date, often non-refundable; that's common practice, not law, so check your state's consumer rules on non-refundable deposits before you print yours.

What does apply is the allergen information on the order, exactly as in any sale to the public. Under the 2022 Food Code, the permit holder has to notify customers in writing of the major food allergens that are ingredients in unpackaged food (3-602.12(C); checked in October 2026), and there are nine of them since sesame joined the list on January 1, 2023: write the customer's allergies on the order form and tell them, in writing, which major allergens the cake contains. A cake you box ahead and sell ready-made counts as packaged food and carries the full label (common name, ingredients by weight, net quantity, your name and address and the source of each major allergen, 3-602.11(B)). And don't promise “gluten-free” on a cake from a kitchen that works with wheat flour all day unless you can stand behind it: it has a federal meaning, under 20 ppm of gluten (21 CFR 101.91). Your inspector enforces your state's adopted edition of the Food Code, so check which one applies to you.

The date you made the cake matters too. A First Communion cake with a cream or custard filling is a TCS item: it's held at 41 °F or below (Food Code 3-501.16), and in the modeled case the "Egg & TCS Decision" sheet puts the strawberries & cream cake, this campaign's star, in the refrigerated case. If it's kept refrigerated for more than 24 hours, it has to carry a date mark, with 7 days at 41 °F as the ceiling and the day it was made counting as day one (3-501.17); that legal limit isn't its shelf life, which is the one you declare in your own food safety plan. For a First Communion order, that makes the date you made the cake part of the order, not a habit: write it on the order form and on the box, and plan the bake around the pickup date, not the other way round.

## 7. Your First Wholesale Account: What Price, and When Your Permit Changes

### The Context of the Decision

Your first wholesale account turns up on its own, without your looking for it, and it feels like free money: a fixed order every week from the café down the street, with no need to put a single extra piece in the display case and none of the display-case waste that eats into the counter's margin. You agree, you deliver and you get paid, and it feels as if that volume comes practically free out of the same production kitchen you've already built.

What you don't see on day one are three things. The first: the price you give that café is lower than your price at the counter, because it buys volume and doesn't pay for your display case, so the margin takes a hit. The second: payment stops being cash at the counter and becomes payment on terms, as the European example in chapter 17 of the guide shows (Matias's trade splits into 95% direct sales and 5% sold on credit at 30 to 60 days; Spanish press, November 21, 2025). The third, the one that changes the business: selling to other businesses can, past a certain line, take you out of the FDA's retail food establishment exemption and into federal food facility registration, with paperwork you didn't have the day before you said yes to that first account. Your local food permit stays; what changes is everything that gets added on top of it.

### The Options on the Table

There are three roads, and none of them is automatically the right one:

- Option A: don't open the channel. You sell only to consumers, in your own shop, and supplying other businesses stays out of the plan.
- Option B: open the channel with a deliberate cap, set by you, that keeps you well below the line that brings FDA registration into play.
- Option C: open the channel with no cap, accept that wholesale may become the larger share of your sales, and take on the FDA food facility registration that comes with it.

None of them is free. A gives up a steady volume that fills dead hours in the kitchen. B means keeping count of how much you supply each week so you don't cross the line without noticing. C takes on more paperwork in exchange for being able to grow the channel without an artificial limit.

### The Criterion You Decide By

The usual mistake is to look at the channel's gross margin and compare it with the counter's. That's not the right criterion: a channel is judged by its contribution margin, not its gross margin, because what matters is how much that order contributes to covering the kitchen's fixed costs once you've taken off what it costs to serve it. And the question that really settles the decision is a different one: what happens to break-even if I take that channel away? If break-even drops when you remove it, the channel is subtracting: it forces you to sell more every month to reach the same zero profit, and keeping it doesn't make up for the volume it brings.

The second criterion isn't about margin, it's about risk. A single large customer in this channel concentrates both payment and volume in one signature: if that café closes, switches suppliers or stretches its payment terms, you feel the blow all at once, not little by little as at the display case, where the risk is spread across dozens of customers who pay on the spot. And one account of a very different size from the rest, a hotel or a chain, can tip the FDA's 50% line on its own.

With those two criteria on the table, the default recommendation is option B: open the channel, but with the cap set and watched. That recommendation changes if break-even without the channel turns out higher than with it (then the channel contributes and deserves to grow with no cap, option C), or if the channel's weight in your sales shoots up on the back of a single customer, because then the concentration risk weighs more than the margin.

### The Cell That Settles It

The "Channels and Break-Even" sheet in 3-year-financial-plan.xlsx brings the numbers you need to decide with your own data: this channel's contribution margin, 49%; wholesale's weight in total sales, 5%; and monthly break-even in both versions, with all channels open ($23,337.56) and without wholesale ($23,018.22). Since break-even goes up when you add the channel (from $23,018 to $23,338 a month), by the criterion above this channel subtracts: it forces you to sell $320 more each month to reach the same zero. That the recommendation is still option B, and not A, comes from the second thing this decision measures: wholesale fills dead kitchen hours you're already paying for, and with the cap in place that use of capacity makes up for the $320. Without a cap, the math changes sign.

To know whether the cap you set keeps you clear of FDA registration, the "Selling Wholesale" sheet in permits-and-licenses-checklist.xlsx runs the three checks with your own figures and your own accounts, one by one: the share of your food sales by value that goes to businesses, the delivery radius you can hold at 41 °F, and the labels each account gets.

### The Rule That Applies, When There Is One

Here there is a rule, and you need to know it before you sign that first account (checked in October 2026). FDA food facility registration exempts retail food establishments (21 CFR 1.226(c)), and the line that keeps you one only comes into play when you sell food you make to other BUSINESSES, meaning cafés, restaurants, specialty food stores or caterers that aren't yours: a pastry shop that only sells to consumers, at its counter, by custom order or online, isn't affected, however you look at it. Once you sign with that café, you're in, and the "Selling Wholesale" sheet puts you through three checks at once: the FDA line, the cold chain and the labels. Only the first one decides whether you register with FDA; the other two don't change your permit, but they're what your accounts and your inspector expect.

The FDA line can be measured two ways, and one is enough, because both measure the same thing (21 CFR 1.227): either the share of your food sales that goes to businesses stays below half (the sheet seeds a cautious working line of 25%, our own, not the law's, so you see the legal 50% coming long before you reach it), or, in dollars, what consumers buy from you in a typical week stays above what businesses buy that same week. Businesses never count as consumers, however small they are, and what counts is value over the whole year, not kilos or one good week, so check it with your POS figures and your invoices. The cold chain is the second check: every TCS item you deliver has to arrive at 41 °F or below (Food Code 3-501.16), and the maximum delivery radius you type into the sheet, in miles, is your own figure, not a legal one.

The check that breaks most easily is the third one, labels: a single account without them is enough to fail it. Packaged items need the full label (common name, ingredients by weight, net quantity, your name and address, and the source of each major allergen, Food Code 3-602.11(B)), and unpackaged items need an allergen sheet so the café can give its own customers the written notice the Food Code asks for (3-602.12(C)). And once the channel is open, the paperwork isn't optional: keep a record of each account (what you supplied, how much and when), which is your proof that you're still on the retail side of the line, and ask your CPA about resale certificates for what your accounts resell. Wholesale also takes a licensed kitchen: outside the cottage food exceptions a few states allow (chapter 9 of the guide), you can't supply cafés from home. Two state notes: in New York, the Article 20-C license already covers wholesale bakery operations; in Florida, ask FDACS whether your volume calls for its Wholesale/Manufactured Food Establishment Permit.

## 8. Hire a Pastry Cook or Lean on an Assistant

### The Context of the Decision

The production kitchen doesn't run itself. The moment the owner steps out to deliver an order, deals with a supplier or simply takes a day off, someone has to keep production going with the same judgment, and that someone has a line of their own on the wage table: it isn't occasional help, it's a position on your payroll. This is where more pastry shop owners go wrong on price, because the temptation shows up on its own: cover the gap with a part-time support hire, because the difference in pay between that profile and a specialist looks small on paper. You make the cheaper offer, and months later you find out that hire doesn't cover what needed covering. Of all the staffing decisions made on price, this is exactly the one made most often.

### The Options on the Table

There are three roads, and it pays to put them side by side before you make any offer:

- Option A: hire a full-time production kitchen specialist. They take on production independently from day one.
- Option B: cover the same hours with two part-time support hires. It lowers the cost per head, but it splits the responsibility between two people with less technical experience.
- Option C: hire one support profile full-time, with in-house training so they grow inside the kitchen.

What changes among the three isn't just the number on the paycheck. The annual employer cost changes, the kitchen's real independence when the owner isn't there changes, and so does the risk of the whole business depending on a single person who one day may go on leave or quit.

### The Criterion You Decide By

The right comparison isn't pay against pay: it's employer cost against employer cost. Payroll taxes, workers' comp and benefits on top of pay (the case budgets 33%: roughly 10 points of payroll taxes and workers' comp, and the rest benefits; the case keeps workers' comp inside it even though Texas lets private employers opt out) aren't a token percentage: they can reverse an order that looked obvious from the paycheck alone. And there's a second twist: you compare by kitchen hour actually covered, not by head hired. Two part-time contracts add up to the same hours as one full-time contract on paper, but the employer cost of two contracts isn't simply half of one each: federal unemployment tax (FUTA) applies to the first $7,000 of EACH employee's pay, and your state's unemployment tax has its own per-employee wage base ($7,000 in California, for example), so two hires pay that base twice where one pays it once. That's where option B loses part of its initial appeal.

The second criterion, and the one that decides in practice, is about coverage, not cost. An early bake shift in a production kitchen is continuous: it starts at a set time and has to be held from start to finish. Two part-time schedules fitted together almost always leave a seam, a moment in the schedule when no specialist is standing at the oven, and you notice it in the day's production before you notice it in the month-end payroll. If the schedule doesn't close, option B's savings are only on paper.

### The Cell That Settles It

You don't have to estimate the number: it's already calculated. The "Hours and Cost" sheet in staff-schedule-and-labor-cost.xlsx gives, for each person on the team, the annual employer cost and the employer cost per hour worked, and that's where you truly compare option A against option B. The full-time production kitchen specialist, the Pastry Cook in the Skilled specialists grade, has an employer cost of $27,469 a year, $15.43 per hour worked. The half-time support profile, the Bakery Assistant in the Support staff grade, costs $11,894 a year, $13.36 per hour worked. Per hour, the gap between the two is $2.07, much smaller than the annual figures suggest at first glance (most of that difference is simply full-time against half-time), and it's exactly that short distance that makes coverage weigh more than savings. The whole team's staff cost, with this structure, comes to $92,776 a year. The "Weekly Schedule" sheet, in the same workbook, is the one that tells you whether, with the option you choose, the schedule is truly covered shift by shift, or whether an early-morning gap is left with no specialist on hand.

With these numbers on the table, the recommendation is option A: a full-time production kitchen specialist carries production without depending on fitting two calendars together, and the difference in employer cost against option B doesn't make up for the risk of leaving a shift uncovered. This changes if your kitchen's real production volume doesn't fill a specialist's full-time schedule: then option C, a full-time support hire with in-house training, starts to make sense, as long as the "Weekly Schedule" sheet confirms that this person, once trained, covers the full shift. The task sheets for each role, with what each person really does on their shift, belong with your daily task checklists, outside this pack, and they're the reference that gives option C's in-house training concrete content instead of a vague promise.

### The Rule That Applies, When There Is One

Here there is a rule, and it isn't an industry agreement: in the US nothing sets each role's pay except the legal floor and, above it, the local market. The federal minimum wage, which Texas applies as its own, is $7.25 an hour, $15,080 a year working full time (Department of Labor, 2026). The case's lowest pay grade, Support staff, the Bakery Assistant's, comes to $17,886.30 a year, $8.60 an hour: above the Texas floor, but below the state minimum in California ($16.90), New York ($17.00 in New York City, Long Island and Westchester; $16.00 in the rest of the state) and Florida ($14.00 until September 29, 2026; check FloridaCommerce for the current rate), according to the Department of Labor's state table updated July 1, 2026. Paying it there wouldn't be a cheap option; it would be illegal. Check your city's minimum too, because it may be higher than your state's, and keep the market in view: the national median is $17.86 an hour for bakers (BLS, May 2025), well above the case's whole table.

And two more obligations don't allow shortcuts, whichever option you choose. The first is training: under the 2022 Food Code, the person in charge, a certified food protection manager (2-102.12), makes sure employees are properly trained in food safety, including food allergy awareness, as it relates to their duties (2-103.11(O)), and your state may add a food handler card (in California, within 30 days of hiring, valid for three years; in Texas, an accredited course). That holds for option A's specialist and option B's two support hires just as much as for option C, and it goes on the "Training Log" sheet of permits-and-licenses-checklist.xlsx from day one. The second is age, and it matters for options B and C if you're thinking of a student as your assistant: no one under 16 may do any baking or work before 7:00 a.m., and no one under 18 may operate, set up, adjust or clean a power-driven mixer or dough sheeter (Hazardous Occupations Order No. 11, Department of Labor Fact Sheet #43). In practice, whoever covers the early bake shift has to be an adult.
