## 19. Financing, Sales Tax, Packaging and Startup Cash

### Financing and Debt Service

The case modeled in this guide borrows $60,000 at a 6.2% annual nominal interest rate. With those two figures, the monthly payment comes to $936.57. The 6.2% is an assumption of the case, not a quote: your rate depends on your lender, your credit and the market the week you sign. And here's where almost everyone goes wrong when they read their own financial plan: that payment isn't a pastry shop expense. Only the interest part is; the rest is principal repaid, and repaying principal doesn't reduce your profit, it reduces your debt. In year one, interest on this loan adds up to $3,671.10, and that's the figure that does go down into the P&L.

What a lender looks at when it studies your loan isn't the payment itself, but how much room the business leaves you to make it. That room is the debt service coverage ratio, or DSCR: the cash the pastry shop generates divided by what you have to pay in principal plus interest that year. In the modeled case's full schedule, the tightest year of the whole loan leaves a coverage of 3.24, which means you generate more than three times what you need to make that year's payments. A lender doesn't read only the three-year average: it reads the worst year, because that's the one that decides whether you can ride out a slow month without a cash squeeze.

And here's the decision almost nobody thinks about when they build the schedule: asking for an interest-only period, when you pay only interest and no principal, artificially improves the first year's coverage. The case's loan starts with six interest-only months. It sounds good on paper, but what it really does is push that principal into the following years, which arrive with a bigger payment just when you no longer have the opening cushion. That's why the number that matters isn't year one's coverage but the tightest year of the whole schedule: if the interest-only period moves the loan's worst moment from the first year to the second or the third, you have to review that year as strictly as you'd review the first, not relax because the start looks good.

Where does a loan like this come from? In the US the usual door is an SBA-backed loan, and two SBA programs fit a pastry shop. 7(a) loans go up to $5 million (SBA Express, up to $500,000), with terms of up to 10 years for equipment and working capital. The SBA Microloan program lends $50,000 or less, about $13,000 on average, for working capital, inventory, furniture and machinery, but not to pay off existing debt or buy real estate. The case's $60,000 is above the Microloan ceiling, so it would be a 7(a) loan or a conventional bank loan, and its seven-year term (84 monthly payments, the first six interest-only) sits inside the 7(a) limit for equipment and working capital. Equipment financing or leasing and community development lenders (CDFIs) are other doors worth knocking on for part of the kitchen; whichever you use, compare offers on the same basis: rate, term, interest-only months and fees.

There's one more cash front to settle before you sign anything with the bank, and in the US it works differently from the VAT that a European business fronts on its build-out and gets back months later. Here nothing comes back: the sales tax you pay on equipment, fixtures and taxable build-out is a cost (chapter 4). In the modeled case, the lines that can carry it add up to $161,480, which at Texas's maximum combined rate of 8.25% is about $13,322 more on the invoices, shown for illustration and not added to the case. So the case's startup cash need is its pre-tax total, $227,916: $60,000 from the loan and $167,916 of your own money. Of that, $62,115 is working capital, four months of fixed costs, within the three to six months of costs that industry guides suggest keeping in reserve (Toast, for coffee shops; a third-party estimate), and it's the balance you open with. The 12-month cash flow shows what it's for: the balance dips to its low of $61,346 in month 3, while sales are still ramping up and payroll goes out at $7,731.34 every month, and it closes month 12 at $87,811, a year-one cash flow of $25,696. That low point, not the year-end balance, is the number to cover with the same rigor as the loan payment: the first table below breaks the sales tax down block by block, so you can match it against your real payment calendar, not just against the plan's totals.

### Sales Tax Follows How You Sell, Not What You Sell

In a pastry shop, bread, croissants and cakes share the same counter, and in a VAT country each family can carry its own rate. In the US it doesn't work that way, and getting this wrong at the register is the costliest mistake you can make in your first month. Sales tax isn't set by product family: it follows how you sell. In all four example states, pastries, cakes and bread sold to go are generally exempt; what you plate, heat or serve at your tables usually isn't. That comes from each state's own rules, as published by California's tax agency (CDTFA), New York's Department of Taxation and Finance, the Texas Comptroller and the Florida Department of Revenue, checked in October 2026.

The reasoning behind that statement, which you need to have straight in case anyone ever questions it, is that there's no national food tax rule: each state draws its own line, and the four example states draw it in different places. In California, bakery goods sold to go are exempt, even hot bakery goods, and what's eaten on your premises is taxable (CDTFA Regulation 1603). In New York, the bakery products a food store sells (bread, rolls, donuts, cakes, pies) are exempt unless they're eaten on the premises (TB-ST-283). In Texas, bakery items are exempt whatever their size, a whole cake or a single slice, unless you sell them with your own plates or utensils (34 TAC §3.293). In Florida, a shop with no place to eat sells them exempt, except anything kept hot. Put that way, state by state, it's accurate and defensible with anyone.

Then there's the fine print that most opening guides skip, and it's where the money is. In Texas, a napkin, wax paper or a clamshell box doesn't count as a utensil, and the exemption is written for a bakery, a business where more than half of sales are display-case bakery items to be eaten somewhere else. In Florida, once you have tables, five items or fewer are presumed to be eaten on site, so they're taxable unless you package them to go (taped, stapled, wrapped or sealed) and ring them up separately, on their own key or register; and hot prepared food kept on a heat source is always taxable. In California, a pastry and a hot drink sold together for one price make the whole combo taxable, a ham-and-cheese croissant isn't a bakery good, and the 80/80 rule can reach even cold food to go: if more than 80% of your sales are food and more than 80% of that food is taxable, you collect tax on cold to-go food too, unless you keep separate accounting. And in New York, a restaurant-type business, which a bakery-café with tables may be, collects tax on cold food to go unless it's sold in the form, quantity and packaging a food store would use: a dozen bagels stays exempt (TB-ST-806).

The third front, then, is where the customer eats, not what they buy. If the customer sits down at your tables and you serve the order there, you're selling food for consumption on your premises, and in the four example states that sale is generally taxable (in Texas, the trigger is your plates or utensils). If the same order goes out the door to go, the bakery item stays exempt: the same croissant changes its tax depending on whether it's eaten at your table or walks out with the customer. This isn't a fine point for your CPA, it's a setting that has to be built into your point of sale from opening day, with every menu line and every channel assigned the right treatment. The modeled case sells to go from a counter with no seating, so in Texas its counter sales are exempt and the workbook's tax layer reads zero; the table at the end of the chapter shows which treatment each kind of sale carries, so you can repeat the reasoning with your own menu and your own state.

### Your Sales Tax Permit and the POS You Buy Today

Sales tax has a registration and a calendar, and you need to know both before you open. You register with your state's tax agency for a sales tax permit (also called a seller's permit) before your first sale, in the same round of paperwork as your EIN and your employer registration: it's phase P2 of permits-and-licenses-checklist.xlsx. And the state sets your filing schedule when it registers you. Said plainly: there's no single national date or frequency to memorize. Yours comes on the paper the state sends you, and that's the one that goes in your calendar.

What does matter to set up now, even though your first return is months away, is something that slips by unnoticed: your point of sale has to be able to do what the tax rules assume. Florida only exempts to-go items in a shop with tables if they're packaged to go and rung up separately; California's 80/80 rule only spares cold food to go if you keep separate accounting. That means the POS you buy for opening has to tag every sale as to-go or for-here and report the two separately from day one, and it has to do it before your first return, not after. Before you sign, ask the vendor to show you how it handles one item that's exempt to go and taxable for here, and what the report you'd file from looks like. The POS is one of the first purchases you make when you set up the pastry shop, and one of the most expensive to change once the register is ringing up sales, the menu is loaded and the staff is trained on that system.

And a calendar warning, because tax rules are among the few things in this guide that can move under your feet: local rates differ from one city to the next (in Texas, up to 2% on top of the state's 6.25%), and Florida has a proposed rewrite of its rule on food sales (Rule 12A-1.011) that we couldn't confirm had taken effect. Treat what you've just read as the picture in October 2026, something to check with your state's tax agency when you buy the POS and again before your first return, not something to take as fixed because it's in writing. This guide's Regulatory Appendix lists each state's tax agency; look there before you sign any point-of-sale contract.

### Packaging: the Cake Box, Foam and PFAS, State by State

There's a question everyone who opens a pastry shop asks as soon as they start buying boxes: if I put the cake in its box at the counter, which rules am I under? The short answer is that in the US, the rules that reach your counter look at the material, not at who fills the box, and they're state rules (sometimes city ones), not a single national law. The cake box and board, the pastry tray, the cookie bag and the coffee cup are all packaging you choose to fill at your own counter, and that choice is exactly what those rules look at: what it's made of and what was added to it. That idea ties in with everything else in this section: any disposable item you use to hand over the product, or so the customer can take it home, falls in the same category, whether you buy it printed with your logo or plain.

Here's the nuance almost nobody explains well, and it's the one that really saves you a headache. In New York, food packaging with intentionally added PFAS (a family of grease- and water-repellent chemicals) has been banned since December 31, 2022. In California, plant-fiber packaging (paper and paperboard) with intentionally added PFAS, or with 100 parts per million or more of total organic fluorine, has been banned since January 1, 2023 (AB 1200, Health and Safety Code §109000 and following). Grease resistance is exactly what you ask of a croissant bag or a cake board, so this lands squarely on a pastry shop. Translated into what you have to do in your kitchen: you don't test anything yourself. What you do is ask your packaging supplier for written confirmation that the paper and board products they sell you have no intentionally added PFAS, and file it with the rest of your business papers, ready to show if anyone ever asks. Ask for it with the first invoice, not when you've been buying the same packaging for two years: it's a document you request once per product and file, not a recurring chore.

There are two obligations that are entirely yours, and both have to do with what you put the product in. The first is foam: expanded polystyrene (EPS) food containers have been banned in New York since January 1, 2022, for food service providers and stores, and in California, where CalRecycle announced on April 4, 2025 that the ban applies because foam food service ware never reached the 25% recycling rate that SB 54 requires, and the Attorney General reminded businesses on December 2, 2025 that administrative penalties can reach $50,000 a day. Florida goes the other way: for food businesses under its state food law, polystyrene rules belong to the state, not to your city (local ordinances adopted before 2016 are the exception), and in Texas we found no statewide ban, though your city may have its own. The second is the label on what you pack yourself: anything you package ahead of time in your own shop needs the full label (common name, ingredients by weight, net quantity, your name and address and the major allergens, Food Code 3-602.11(B)), and New York signed a law on November 12, 2025 that requires written notice of the nine major allergens on food prepared, packaged and sold on the same premises, bakeries included and food packed after the customer orders excluded. We couldn't confirm when it takes effect, so if you open in New York, ask your inspector before you print your labels, and choose your boxes and your label printer knowing it's coming.

### Food Waste: What the Rules Ask of a Small Shop, Exactly

Food waste is the other subject that hits a newly opened pastry shop head-on, and here the usual mistake goes both ways: some say "those rules don't apply to me" and leave it there, and others assume they'll have to run a donation program like a supermarket's. Neither is right. In the US, the food waste rules with teeth are state and local, and the best-known one among the four example states, California's edible food recovery law (SB 1383), is written for big generators: grocery stores and supermarkets over 10,000 sq ft, restaurants over 5,000 sq ft or with 250 or more seats, hotels with an on-site food facility and 200 or more rooms, plus wholesale food vendors, food service providers and food distributors (CalRecycle, checked in October 2026). Those businesses have to donate the maximum amount of edible food that would otherwise be thrown away, under contracts or written agreements with food recovery organizations, and keep records of what they donate, pounds per month and per recipient included. A counter shop the size of the modeled case, 969 sq ft and no seating, falls in neither size band; if wholesale grows into a real business line, ask your city's recycling program which tier, if any, you fall in.

What stays out of the headline, and still reaches you even though that law doesn't, comes down to three things. California requires businesses to recycle their organic waste (AB 1826; in September 2020 CalRecycle lowered the threshold that triggers it to 2 cubic yards of solid waste, counting trash, recycling and organics together), and your trim, your sweepings and your unsold product are organic waste: ask your hauler what collection you need. The Food Code still decides how long you can keep what you didn't sell: a ready-to-eat TCS item held more than 24 hours needs a date mark and is out at 7 days, counting the day you make it as day 1 (3-501.17), and no discount stretches that. And if you donate, federal law has your back: the Bill Emerson Good Samaritan Food Donation Act protects a business that gives apparently wholesome food in good faith to a nonprofit for people in need, free or at a "good Samaritan reduced price", from liability for the food's nature, age, packaging or condition, except for gross negligence or intentional misconduct (42 U.S.C. §1791, amended on January 5, 2023 to also cover some businesses that donate directly to people in need, restaurants, caterers and retail grocers among them). It doesn't override your state or local health rules. These three are what press on a small kitchen day to day, far more than the donation program the big generators have to run.

And there's a commercial lever that leans on this same thinking and is worth real money in a pastry shop, where what doesn't sell today never sells. The federal donation law's own definition of apparently wholesome food covers food that "may not be readily marketable due to appearance, age, freshness, grade, size, surplus, or other conditions", and the same logic works at your counter: a cake that came out a little lopsided, a misprinted box, a batch of cookies that's a size off can all go out at a lower price, as long as you tell the customer what's wrong with them and stand behind the sale. And there's a second, finer detail that connects with your counter: a day-old rack, with its own sign and its own day-old discount, is standard practice in US bakeries, and what you can't do is move yesterday's croissants back into the case under a "baked this morning" sign (chapter 17: it's a misleading-claim risk). Give the day-old price its own key on your POS, so you can mark down whatever you need to without the discount blurring your real margin figures or the next promotion you plan.

With this you have the full map of packaging and waste, with its source behind each statement; what's left is to put it in dollars, and that's the job of the tables that follow.
