## 10. Food Safety Plan, Allergens and Training: What to Have Ready on Inspection Day

### A Simple Food Safety Plan Is Enough, but It Needs a Certified Person in Charge

Let's start with the good news, because in this area good news rarely arrives with the section number attached: nobody expects a pastry shop to run the paperwork of a food plant. What you do need is a food safety plan built on HACCP principles for what you actually make: which hazards your products carry, which steps control them (cooling, cold holding at 41 °F or below, date marking, allergens), what limit each step has to hit, who checks it and where it gets written down. It doesn't have to be the full documentation system of a manufacturing plant, but it does have to exist, on paper, before you open. And it follows the edition of the Food Code your state enforces, not necessarily the 2022 model: per FDA's 2024 adoption list, Texas and Florida enforce the 2017 edition, New York's retail food stores the 2001 one, and California has its own code. The 2022 rules are the direction of travel, so building to them is the safe choice.

The condition that trips people up is this one: under the 2022 Food Code, the person in charge must be a certified food protection manager, someone who has shown what they know by passing a test that's part of an accredited program (2-102.12, checked in October 2026). The only exception is for establishments your regulatory authority decides are minimal risk, and a production kitchen full of cream fillings shouldn't count on being one. A generic line like "the team handles food safety" doesn't work: what the inspector wants to see is a name, that person's certificate and a plan that names them. Texas already asks for a certified food manager, and New York City requires a supervisor with a Food Protection Certificate on site whenever food is received, prepared, stored or served, or the place is open. It's the first document in the file and the easiest one to forget, because it doesn't involve buying or installing anything: it involves booking the exam, sitting down and writing a name into the plan.

On the templates and sample plans that circulate online and through trade groups, the status is clear, and it's worth having it straight before the inspector explains it to you: they're optional. They help, they point you in the right direction and they save you writing time, but adopting one doesn't excuse you from having your own plan, built around your production kitchen, your display case and the way you really make things. A generic template doesn't replace a named, certified person in charge or a plan with your own products in it; at most, it's the outline you build yours on.

In the case modeled for this guide, the food safety plan is budgeted at $800, and the P5 phase (food permit, food safety and training) brings together 6 steps in all. That figure covers writing the plan, naming the person in charge and the other pieces you'll see in the table below; it doesn't cover the day-to-day logs, which are a different thing and come with a different product. The temperature logs, the daily traceability and the cleaning records that feed the plan once the shop is running are in the HACCP Food Safety Kit Pro, and this guide doesn't rebuild them: here you'll find what you need to have written and signed before you open, not how to fill it in every morning.

Keep two layers apart in your head from the start, because they come back throughout the chapter: one is the regulatory map, with the rule and the section that require each document, and this guide builds it; the other is the daily routine once your shop is up and running, and that's your daily checklists and logs, which the HACCP Food Safety Kit Pro has ready. Mixing them up is what leads people to buy the same thing twice or, worse, to buy neither, because it feels like everything's done once the plan is signed.

### The Food Handler Card Is a State Rule: What You Always Need Is a Training Log

Here's a confusion to clear up with the law, not with an opinion, and in the US it comes in two pieces. The first piece: there's no national food handler card. What the Food Code asks is that employees be properly trained in food safety, food allergy awareness included, as it relates to their assigned duties, and it makes seeing to that part of the person in charge's job (2-103.11(O)). The second piece: whether each employee also needs a card is a state rule, sometimes a city one, and the four example states show how far apart they sit. California requires a food handler card within 30 days of hire, valid for 3 years even if the employee changes jobs (Health and Safety Code §113948); Texas requires an accredited food handler course, and a city can't charge for or demand its own card when you present an accredited one (Health and Safety Code §438.046(c)); New York State has no card, though New York City has its Food Protection Certificate for supervisors; and in Florida you check FDACS (retail) or DBPR (food service) for the training rules. Put the two pieces together and it's clear that the card, where it exists, is the floor, not the whole job.

What applies everywhere, and this is what has to replace "the card" in your head, is the employer's side: staff who handle food have to be trained for what they do, and that responsibility sits with you and your person in charge, not with each employee on their own. A card in someone's wallet isn't the point; the point is that you, as the owner of the kitchen, can show that everyone who handles product is trained for the work they do.

Translated into what you'll face on inspection day, the deliverable is a training log, person by person, with three minimum data points: what the training covered, how many hours and the date. A certificate or card from whichever accredited provider works perfectly as evidence inside that log; what doesn't work, because it isn't what the rules ask for, is a pile of cards with nothing that ties them to each person, their duties and a date. If you have the certificates but not the log that organizes them by person, content, hours and date, the file is still incomplete.

In the modeled case, the training log covers 5 people with training on file and 0 overdue at the check date; food safety training is budgeted at $240. Each certificate or card is given a validity of 730 days in this case, and it needs saying plainly: that figure is a house criterion, not a deadline any rule sets for you. Validity comes from your state or the issuing program (a California food handler card lasts 3 years), and two years is simply a reasonable, shorter pace to refresh training and keep proof current. What the rules ask is that training be in place and that you can show it, so 730 days is a sensible management decision, not a legal obligation you can quote to an inspector as if it came from a regulation.

### Kitchen Allergens Versus Display-Case Allergens

Two things need separating once and for all here, because in practice they get mixed up and on inspection day they never are: at the display case you have a duty to inform the customer, and in the production kitchen you have a production job, which is not to use the same equipment, containers or surfaces with an allergen and then with a food that doesn't contain it unless they've been fully cleaned in between and you've checked nothing is left. They're two different layers, and each has its own basis.

The first layer, the display case, is set by the 2022 Food Code: the permit holder has to notify customers, in writing, of the presence of major food allergens as ingredients in unpackaged food (3-602.12(C), checked in October 2026). In the US there are 9 major allergens: milk, eggs, fish, crustacean shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybeans and, since January 1, 2023, sesame (FDA). For unpackaged food, declaring them isn't optional, and a spoken answer at the counter isn't the format the rule asks for: what you need is a written notice that your staff, the inspector and any customer can see, such as an allergen chart for the case or a card next to each item. If you package a product yourself in the shop, its label carries the common name, ingredients by weight, net quantity, your name and address and the source of each major allergen (3-602.11(B)), with nutrition facts unless an exemption applies; and New York signed a law in November 2025 on allergen notices for food packaged and sold in the same store, bakeries included, so check when it applies to you. If you open in the UK, the list is 14 allergens, food you prepack for direct sale needs a full ingredient list, and for unpackaged food the information can be given in any form, even orally, as long as a notice tells customers how to get it.

That's the last we'll say about the display case in this chapter, and for a specific reason: the item-by-item allergen declaration for your display case is built and kept in the HACCP Food Safety Kit Pro, where the day-to-day of your menu lives. What this guide builds is a different piece, one that usually doesn't exist in any kitchen before it opens: the kitchen allergen matrix, which crosses every ingredient with every allergen and with the day's production order, so you know at what point in the process the tree nuts come in, when the wheat comes in, and which surface or container has to be clean before the next batch touches it. That matrix is yours, it comes out of your menu and your process, and it doesn't duplicate the display-case one: you'll never have two full allergen declarations for the same product list, because each one answers a different question.

That line between kitchen and display case is also what decides which display case you buy. Of the items on your menu, 15 need a refrigerated display case, and the reason isn't flavor or presentation: it's whether the filling or the topping is stable at room temperature. In Food Code terms, it's whether the item is TCS, a food that needs time and temperature control for safety, which is decided by its pH and water activity (1-201.10); if it is, it's held at 41 °F or below (3-501.16). A cake with pastry cream, whipped cream or a filling that can't sit out falls into those 15 items; a dry pastry or an unfilled dough doesn't. That same line, filling versus room-temperature stable, is what organizes your production kitchen: it decides when in the day you make each batch and how long it can sit out before it has to go into the cold. The "Egg & TCS Decision" sheet in opening-menu-and-recipe-costing.xlsx works it through item by item. It isn't a decorative fact: it sets the size of the case you're going to buy and the order of your daily production.

And all of this rests on a production basis that many small kitchens ignore until the inspector points it out: allergen cross-contact travels through equipment, containers and surfaces. FDA tested it in retail settings with peanut, milk and egg: full cleaning, meaning wash, rinse, sanitize and air-dry as the Food Code recommends, was effective at removing allergens and limiting their transfer; scraping surfaces before cleaning helped; and dry wipes did worse than wet cloths or sanitizing wipes (FDA, allergen removal and transfer study, page current as of April 7, 2026). It's a production job, separate from the display-case notice: you can have a perfectly labeled display case and still put a customer at risk if the rolling pin you used with nuts goes unwashed into the dough you're going to sell as nut-free.

### Gluten-Free Is a Lab Threshold, Not a Free Claim

The words "gluten-free" don't depend on which flour you use or on your good intentions: they depend on a number. Federal labeling rules (21 CFR 101.91, checked in October 2026) allow "gluten-free" only if the food has no ingredient that is a gluten-containing grain (wheat, rye, barley or their crossbred hybrids), or that is derived from one and hasn't been processed to remove the gluten, and if any unavoidable presence of gluten in the food is below 20 parts per million, that is, under 20 milligrams per kilo. There's no softer version to fall back on: "no gluten," "free of gluten" and "without gluten" are held to the same standard, and a food that carries any of them without meeting it is misbranded. The nuance people forget is in the words "in the food": it isn't measured in the ingredient, it's measured in the finished piece, after everything your production kitchen has put it through, its air, its surfaces and its utensils.

The consequence for a pastry kitchen is direct and has no shortcut: if you work with wheat flour in the same space, even at a different bench and on a different shift, you have gluten in the air and in the dust that settles on every surface. You can't call any item made in that kitchen gluten-free without first confirming, with a lab test, that it's below 20 ppm. Swapping the flour in a recipe for rice flour or any other gluten-free flour isn't enough: if the kitchen is the same and the air is the same, the cross-contact risk is still there, and it's that risk the test has to rule out, not the recipe.

A word, too, on the private seals offered by gluten-free associations and certification programs: they're voluntary and additional. They can help you commercially and give you a working protocol, but they don't replace the federal rule or excuse you from testing. You can have the seal and still fall short if you don't hold the threshold, and you can meet the threshold without any seal at all. They're two different things, and one doesn't cover the other.

The business decision that follows from all this is the most expensive one in the chapter, so here it is without hedging: if your kitchen can't sustain gluten-free with an area separated from the rest of production and with lab tests that prove it, then you sell something else and you don't advertise gluten-free. You don't need to invent a selling point to fill that gap on the menu: you leave it out. Advertising "gluten-free" when you can't prove it with a test below the threshold is, of everything this chapter covers, the risk that costs the most, because it isn't a missing page in a binder: it's a public statement about a product that can do real harm to someone who trusts it. The same goes for your display-case cards and your website: if you say it, you have to be able to back it. ### How to Get Three Comparable Quotes

The most expensive mistake isn't picking the wrong supplier: it's comparing quotes that can't be compared. If you ask one for a price per pound and another per case, if you ask one about lead time and not the other, if one quotes with delivery included and the other doesn't, what you end up with isn't three prices, it's three impressions, and you decide blind.

The method is mechanical and allows no shortcuts: ask all three for the same pack size, the same unit, the same lead time and the same payment terms. If you're comparing butter, have all three quote the same block or sheet format, in the same unit of sale, with the same lead time and the same payment terms; if you're comparing flour, the same bag, the same quantity and the same delivery frequency. The moment you break that symmetry, the comparison stops being worth anything.

What you always ask for in writing, without exception, from the three suppliers in each category:

- Price per purchase unit.
- Minimum order.
- Lead time.
- Delivery charges: whether they're included, above what order value, and what they cost if you fall short of that minimum.
- Payment terms: cash on delivery, net 30 or net 60, and the early-payment discount, if there is one.
- Who the sales rep is who answers for your account, with a name and a direct phone number, not a generic email address.

Keep those six answers on the same sheet, one row per supplier, and the comparison takes care of itself at a glance. It's the same discipline you'll apply in the recipe costing chapter when you compare the cost of a batch: if the inputs aren't consistent, the result is worthless, whatever the spreadsheet says.

### How to Model Butter and Cocoa Price Swings

Setting a butter or cocoa price in a document today and calling it done guarantees that, a few months from now, the document will be lying. Both trade on volatile commodity markets, and what's worth teaching you isn't what they cost today, but where to find the public reference price and how often to check it so your recipe costing doesn't fall out of date.

For butter there is a public reference you can cite: the CME (Chicago Mercantile Exchange) spot price for bulk Grade AA butter, which USDA's Dairy Market News reports every week. In the week of October 12-16, 2026, it closed at $1.3650 a pound (about $3.01/kg) on October 16, with a weekly average of $1.3110, up $0.0230 on the week before (USDA AMS, Dairy Market News, Volume 93, Report 42). And the range of movement isn't anecdotal: the same report shows producer prices for butter in August 32.5% lower than a year earlier.

And here's the warning that can save you the most money: that quote is for bulk Grade AA butter, a commodity, not the price of the high-butterfat European-style butter you buy for laminating, like Plugrà at 82% butterfat. They're two different products and two different markets (USDA's own report comments on 80% and 82% butterfat butter separately), and putting them on the same sheet gives you a number that's no use to anyone. Use the CME price as a trend gauge (up, down, flat) and ask your supplier for the real price of your pack size every time you reorder.

For cocoa, on the other hand, this chapter gives no single figure, and that's not an oversight: for the same year, several different values circulate depending on the source, and none of them can be cited with the confidence a paid product demands. What you do with cocoa is the same as with butter in method, though not in numbers: ask your supplier for an updated price at every contract renewal, and don't trust any figure you saw months ago somewhere else.
