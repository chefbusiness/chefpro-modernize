## 1. Executive Summary

### The Project on One Page

This document is the complete case of a pastry shop, with the figures already loaded into the pack's eight tools: every number that follows comes from a cell in those sheets and can be opened to check it, so from here on we won't repeat that explanation in every section. The project is a European-style pastry shop with Spanish and Mexican holiday specialties: its own production kitchen with a front counter on the street, in a mid-size city in Texas, with the floor area split among the production kitchen, the front counter, the walk-in cooler, dry storage, and the staff restroom and lockers. The model works with a space of about 969 square feet and a team made up of the Head Pastry Chef, the Pastry Cook, the Bakery Assistant and the two Counter Associates who run the counter. It's the page a lender reads before deciding whether to keep reading: what's being opened, where, in how many square feet and with what team.

### The Investment and How It's Financed

The investment before working capital comes to $165,800. Working capital adds $62,115, and the two together make the total investment: $227,916. In the US that's also the total cash needed at startup, because there's no VAT to front on the build-out and the equipment and claim back months later, the way a European business does: the sales tax you pay on equipment and taxable build-out is a cost, not an advance. That's why there are three figures here and not four, and each one answers a different question: what it costs to set up the business, how much cushion the first months need, and how much money has to be available on the day you sign.

Of that $227,916, the owner's equity is $167,916 and the loan requested is $60,000. What this summary doesn't add to the case is sales tax: the lines that can carry it (equipment, fixtures, build-out, packaging, plans and marketing) add up to $161,480, and at the maximum combined rate in Texas, 6.25% state plus up to 2% local, or 8.25%, that would be about $13,322 more on the invoices. It doesn't come back the way VAT does, so in your own plan it goes inside the cost of each line (the startup cost calculator ships with its sales tax rate at 0% so you can type in yours), and which lines your state taxes is a question for your CPA.

For contrast, the published US figures for opening a bakery sit far apart because they describe different businesses. Zenind (2026) puts a small retail bakery at $50,000 to $100,000 and up and a production bakery at $75,000 to $150,000 and up; Toast puts the average bakery startup at $10,000 to $50,000; and RestaurantOwner.com's figures, cited by DoorDash, put the median restaurant opening at $375,500. They're third-party estimates with no line-by-line breakdown you can check, not industry averages, and they're cited here only as a frame of reference for this model's $165,800: above the small retail range, at the top of the production bakery range and well below a restaurant with seating, which is where a shop with a full production kitchen and a counter but no seating ought to land.

### The Three-Year Forecast

Revenue in the steady-state year is $322,198, with net profit of $27,836 and a net margin of 8.6%; year one, still ramping up, closes at $4,469. The business reaches cash break-even at 148.9 tickets a day and the plan forecasts 183, a cushion of 22.9%. The financial forecast table that comes with this summary breaks down, line by line, how revenue turns into profit.

Matias, who owns a long-established pastry business in Spain, puts the net profitability of a pastry business that's doing well at 8% to 12% (interview in the Spanish press, November 21, 2025). It's a European figure, but the logic travels: this plan's 8.6% falls at the low end of that band, which is where a case built on conservative assumptions ought to fall. On the US side, Toast's third-party estimates put the food sector's typical net margin at 3% to 5%, higher for specialty shops; next to that, the case's 8.6% is a demanding target, not a modest one.

The financial plan's "Break-Even" sheet is the one to open before you sign anything: it shows the accounting threshold, the cash threshold and how sensitive both are to a dollar less on the average ticket.

### What We're Asking For and Why

The loan requested is $60,000, to complete the startup investment described above alongside the $167,916 of owner's equity that's already committed. That's above the SBA Microloan ceiling ($50,000), so it would be an SBA 7(a) loan (up to $5 million, with terms of up to 10 years for equipment and working capital) or a conventional bank loan. The rate and term in the workbook (6.2%, 84 monthly payments, the first six interest-only) are assumptions of the case, not a quote: the final rate, term and collateral get settled with the lender on this same case, adjusted to what each lender offers. What this plan does fix is where every dollar goes, which is the sentence a lender needs to read: how much goes to the build-out and the equipment, how much to working capital, and how the sales tax you pay on those purchases is budgeted as a cost instead of a refund, as the startup investment table below shows.

This document is a template filled in, from start to finish, with the case of a modeled pastry shop: every figure belongs to that specific case, so that whoever uses it can replace each number with their own on the same structure of sheets. It isn't a forecast of your business's results: it's the template you calculate yours with.

## 2. Market and Location

### The Market the Project Is Entering

The US market for bakery goods isn't small: according to the BLS Consumer Expenditure Survey, the average household spent $539 on bakery products for home in 2024 ($574 in 2023, $497 in 2022), on top of $3,945 on food away from home, which covers every meal and snack eaten out and isn't this category. Those $539 are spent wherever households buy their groceries, the supermarket included, so they're a market-size reference, not a bakery-shop figure; and there's no official US figure for how much of that spending reaches specialty bakeries, or for how it changes with household income, so this plan doesn't use one. What the official series do show clearly is the direction: retail bakeries that bake and sell to the public (NAICS 311811) went from 7,787 establishments and 86,819 jobs in 2018 to 9,473 and 104,527 in 2024, 22% and 20% more, while frozen cakes, pies and other pastries manufacturing (NAICS 311813) went from 236 plants and 12,619 jobs to 309 and 13,541 over the same years, 31% more plants and 7% more jobs (BLS Quarterly Census of Employment and Wages, QCEW). Those series count plants and jobs, not sales: they give the direction, not the size of the market.

That isn't a direct threat to the project; it's a map. Industrial frozen product wins on volume and low prices, and it wins with product that holds up in the freezer, on the truck and in bake-off at any supermarket in-store bakery or café counter. The project doesn't go in to compete there: it goes in to sell what that process can't replicate without losing quality, and to sell custom orders, which is ground where industry doesn't play.

### The Location and the Customer

The modeled case sits on a street with passing foot traffic, with neighborhood customers who buy for the day and order ahead for the occasion. Before anything was signed, the location was checked on the ground, street by street, within the walk (or, in a city where people drive, the short drive) that really feeds the display case: which other food businesses already occupy that radius, which time of day brings the most people past the door, and whether the profile of the people walking by fits a custom-order ticket or only an impulse buy. That check, not an industry average, is what supports the sales figure.

The average ticket of the modeled menu is $5.87 before sales tax, with 2.6 pieces per ticket. It isn't an industry average, because there's no public, checkable figure for the average ticket at a US bakery: it comes from the menu's own mix and prices, calculated piece by piece on the "0. Assumptions" sheet of the financial plan. Change the menu and this number changes, so it's the first one to review if the model doesn't match the real register.

### The Seasonality of Demand

The year's curve isn't flat, and planning it as if it were is the first cash mistake. The highest month, January, carried by the Rosca de Reyes, weighs 11.2% of the year's sales, and the lowest, August, 5.4% (the case closes for two weeks that month): more than double from one month to the other. December (1.24 times an average month) and November (1.08) pick up Christmas and Thanksgiving, the two big US holiday rushes, which the case counts in its monthly curve rather than as campaigns of their own. A year like this isn't planned by monthly average; it's planned by cash: buy, produce and staff for the month at hand, not for an average of twelve that doesn't exist in any real month. The table below, with each month's weight, is the one to look at before you sign a supplier order or add a shift.

The six campaigns of the year (Día de Reyes, Valentine's Day, Mother's Day, Easter, First Communions and graduations, and All Saints' Day and Día de los Muertos) together weigh 7.0% of total sales. Not all of them weigh what their name suggests: Valentine's Day and Mother's Day are record-spending holidays nationally, with $29.1 billion and $38 billion expected in 2026 (NRF), and in the case they come to 0.3% and 0.4% of the year, while Three Kings Day alone weighs 3.7% in five days. That concentrated weight is exactly what has to be translated into production kitchen capacity: how many pieces the team can turn out on those days without breaking the shift. That math, with the staff and the production times, is done in the operations section of this plan, not here.

### Competition and Barriers to Entry

In this location, not one type of business competes but four, and each one takes something different from the project. The bakery that bakes from scratch competes for exactly the same customer and the same price range: it's the direct competition. The small bake-off shop takes the impulse customer who only looks at the price of a single piece. The supermarket in-store bakery takes the daily routine purchase, the one that isn't looking for quality but for convenience and opening hours. And the café that bakes off frozen trays takes the customer who confuses “freshly made” with “freshly baked”, without the business having to say where the dough came from.

Against the last three, the project has a real barrier to entry that costs nothing: saying where the product is made. “Baked here every morning” or “made from scratch in our kitchen” can only be said truthfully by whoever bakes in their own production kitchen. It isn't a defined legal term in the US, but the general ban on false or misleading labeling applies (FD&C Act §403(a)), so the bake-off shop, the supermarket and the café that buy ready-made dough can't honestly use it, and “baked fresh daily” on product they only thaw and finish is a misleading-claim risk. That claim is what the middle- and higher-income customer looks for when walking in to buy a cake to order, not for every day.

## 3. Concept and Menu

### The Concept and the Value Proposition

Before touching a single ingredient, the pastry shop's concept is defined in business terms: what gets made, what stays out, and why that line fits the capacity of the production kitchen and the location where it opens. The modeled case bets on a menu of seasonal and custom-order products on a fixed base of viennoiserie and counter cakes, and it leaves out a full bread-bakery operation (four breads stay on the menu to bring in traffic) and a café with table service: two lines that would call for other equipment, another team and, with seating, a different sales tax treatment and, in New York or Florida, a different permitting agency, and that would dilute the production kitchen's focus in its first year. Stating what the project won't do weighs as much in the plan as what it will, because it's the first question any lender asks when reading the file: whether the investment requested fits the business that will really be operated. Behind that proposal there's a menu with a limited number of items, organized in five families (viennoiserie, individual pastries, cakes and entremets, breads, and seasonal) that spread the risk between display-case product with daily turnover and higher-value custom orders. That split by family is also a defensive decision: when a single product carries too large a share of revenue, any stumble with that product hits cash directly. A European example shows how far that can go: at Hofmann, a well-known pastry shop in Barcelona, a single item, its mascarpone croissant, at roughly 3,000 units a week, accounts for close to 30% of the shop's total sales (The New Barcelona Post, February 2026). The modeled case spreads the weight across families precisely so it doesn't depend on a single star piece.

### The Opening Menu and Its Margin

Margin isn't calculated piece by piece and left at that: you measure the average contribution margin per piece across the whole menu, which in the modeled case comes to $1.07, and you set it against the real cost of serving each item, not just the cost of buying it. The opening menu has an ingredient food cost of 17.6%, that is, what the ingredients cost laid out on the bench, and a served food cost of 20.3% once production waste and to-go packaging are added: the gap between the two percentages is exactly the operating safety margin that separates recipe costing in theory from day-to-day reality. For contrast, third-party estimates put a typical US bakery's food cost at 28% to 35% of sales (Toast); the case's percentages come from its own modeled prices and ingredient costs, so they're the first ones to check against yours. ### The Sales Channels and Their Contribution Margin

The same menu sells through four channels, and they aren't all worth the same to cash even when their margins look alike. The table below details each channel's weight, its contribution margin and its days to collect, but the figure that sums up the whole is the weighted average contribution margin across channels, which in the modeled case is 66.5%. Direct sales at the counter hold up the business, and not only for their margin: they're paid on the spot, while wholesale to local cafés and restaurants carries payment terms of 45 days. Those terms are the financial consequence that matters: two channels with the same contribution margin aren't worth the same to cash if one pays at the moment of sale and the other at thirty or sixty days, because during that time the pastry shop keeps paying for flour, staff and rent with money it has already handed over as product. Matias's own payment split shows how far cash can concentrate in a single channel: 95% sold directly to the customer and only 5% on credit, at 30 to 60 days (interview in the Spanish press, November 21, 2025). The modeled case takes its wholesale share, 5%, from that same split, and the lesson applies here too: the more direct sales weigh in the total, the less the menu's growth strains cash.

### The Pricing Policy

The whole plan uses a single margin rule, not two: first you set the target food cost, 32% in this case, and from it you derive the target gross margin, the remaining 68%. You don't set the two figures separately, or work backward from the margin to deduce the cost: that would lead to two numbers that don't reconcile anywhere else in the plan. That same logic, setting the ingredient cost and letting the margin follow from it, is common practice in the trade, and the US reference cited above agrees with it: a food cost of 28% to 35% of sales means a gross margin of 65% to 72%, and the plan's 68% sits inside that range. With that rule applied across the whole menu, the weighted average selling price comes to $2.26 per piece and the average ticket to $5.87, both before sales tax, which in the US is added at the register and, in the case's state, doesn't apply to bakery items sold to go without the shop's plates or utensils (Texas, 34 TAC §3.293). The prices and portion weights on the opening menu are example data from the modeled case, not a closed list: every production kitchen has its own cost structure and has to recalculate it with its own numbers before putting a single price in the display case.

## 4. Operations Plan

### The Space and Its Zones

The case's space is 969 square feet, and the split by zones follows the one-way flow principle: product always moves forward, from receiving ingredients to the display case, without ever crossing the trash path or the staff path. That's why the production kitchen isn't right up against the street door and why the trash area has its own way out. US codes don't hand you a list of zones by name, but the health department's plan review asks for the layout, the equipment and the finishes before you build (Food Code 8-201.11 and 8-201.12), and the case's plans answer it with seven zones: the sales floor, the production kitchen, dry storage, the walk-in cooler, finished goods storage, the staff restroom and lockers, and trash and recycling. The table below splits the 969 square feet among those seven zones with the exact area of each.

Of those 969 square feet, 484 are production kitchen: exactly half of the space. It's the ratio anyone assessing the project looks at first, because it tells you at a glance whether the business is a factory with a counter or a shop with a support kitchen. Here the split is deliberately even: the shop produces as much as it sells, the kitchen has no more floor than the volume it bills calls for, and the sales floor doesn't fall short for showing what comes out of the back.

### Production Capacity and Its Bottleneck

The business aims to turn out 476 pieces a day at cruising speed. The set of equipment bought in the case allows up to 480, so there's room, but it's a thin margin: the piece of equipment that sets that ceiling, the blast chiller in this case, is the bottleneck of the whole line, and above that figure there's no way to produce more without investing in a second unit. The "Bottleneck" sheet of kitchen-capacity-and-site.xlsx is the one to review before you settle the menu or accept an unusually large custom order, because a rush of cake orders can eat up in a single day the cushion that separates an ordinary day from the equipment's ceiling.

During a campaign that cushion doesn't hold: demand climbs above what the equipment can deliver on an ordinary day (for Three Kings Day, 2,856 pieces a day demanded against the 1,200 the kitchen can turn out during the campaign), and the deficit isn't covered by buying more machines overnight but with the levers listed in this plan's section on risks: reinforced shifts, temporary extra staff and deciding which items get made first. The extra staff has its own rule in the US: no one under 16 may do any baking or work before 7:00 a.m., and no one under 18 may operate a power-driven mixer or dough sheeter (Department of Labor, Hazardous Occupations Order No. 11 and Fact Sheet #43), so the early bake shift is adults only. None of this is last-minute improvisation if it's planned in the operations plan from the start.

### The Team and Its Cost

The case's team is 5 people: a Head Pastry Chef, a Pastry Cook, a Bakery Assistant and two Counter Associates. In full-time equivalents (FTEs) that's 3.5, because some of those five people don't work full time all year. Staff cost comes to $92,776 a year, with the 33% the case budgets for payroll taxes, workers' comp and benefits already inside it: roughly 10 points of that are payroll taxes and workers' comp (the employer's 7.65% FICA, federal and state unemployment taxes and workers' comp insurance, which Texas lets private employers choose not to carry), and the rest is benefits such as health insurance and paid time off. In the US no industry-wide agreement sets pay: the floor is the minimum wage (federal, state and, in many places, city, and you pay the highest), and the case's own wage table, from $8.60 to $12.50 an hour, clears the $7.25 federal minimum that Texas applies ($15,080 a year full time). In California ($16.90), New York ($17.00 or $16.00, depending on the region) or Florida ($14.00 until September 29, 2026; check FloridaCommerce for the current rate) that same table would be below the legal minimum, and nationally the median pay is $17.86 an hour for bakers and $15.00 for fast food and counter workers (BLS, May 2025), so a lender will expect your own local figures here. In the steady-state year, that staff cost is 28.8% of sales: it's the ratio to watch month by month on the P&L, because if it climbs above that, the business stops producing the margin it's modeled on.

Not all of the team starts on the same day. The Head Pastry Chef and the Pastry Cook are hired before opening, because the production sheets have to be locked down and the first batches run before the first customer walks in. Not all of the team is needed from day one, but this plan budgets the whole team from month one on purpose: it's the conservative assumption, and that's why staff cost is the same in all three years of the table. If you stagger the hires (the Bakery Assistant and the second Counter Associate once volume supports those hours), year one comes out better than forecast, and that margin stays as a cushion rather than a promise. Whoever you hire, the Fair Labor Standards Act sets the frame: a record of each hourly employee's hours for every day and every workweek, in any form as long as it's complete and accurate, with payroll records kept for three years and time records for two, and overtime at time and a half for every hour over 40 in a workweek (Department of Labor, Fact Sheet #21).

### The Startup Timeline

The whole project, from the day it gets underway to the day the doors open, takes 8.5 months, with 11 milestones on its critical path. Those durations are planning assumptions, not legal processing times for any agency: every permit has its own response time, and the timeline adds them up with room to spare, not in a rush; the only legal clock anywhere near it is California's, where the health department has 20 working days to review the plans or they're deemed approved. Behind those milestones sit 30 permit steps in six phases, P1 to P6: zoning, the hood type and the landlord's written OK before the lease is signed; the business setup, the state sales tax permit and stamped plans; plan review, the building permit and the business license; the build-out, the fire marshal and the electrical permit; the food permit and pre-opening inspection, with a certified food protection manager as person in charge; and, last, hiring, sales tax setup and insurance. They're on the "Permits Checklist (P1-P6)" sheet of permits-and-licenses-checklist.xlsx, and the twelve milestones, M1 to M12, on its "Timeline and Critical Path" sheet.

The criterion for setting the opening month isn't “as soon as everything is signed” but arriving broken in for the strong season: you open outside a campaign, with time for the team, the production kitchen and the display case to find their rhythm before the year's sales peak arrives. In the case that means September, a few weeks before All Saints' Day and Día de los Muertos, which works as a dress rehearsal, and more than three months before Three Kings Day. The timeline itself warns you if, depending on how the milestones are met, the calculated opening date ends up falling inside a campaign: that's the signal to move one of the steps forward, not to open with the project half broken in.
