### The Sales Tax Trap: What the Dealer's List Price Leaves Out

Here's the specific flaw that causes the most headaches once you start comparing quotes. US dealers and manufacturers list their prices before sales tax (Unox's US site, for example, shows its oven prices with taxes excluded), so the tax only shows up on the invoice. And tax isn't the only thing the list price leaves out: there's freight (heavy equipment ships by truck, so ask whether liftgate and inside delivery are included), installation and start-up by a licensed electrician or plumber, and the electrical spec, because the same oven comes in different voltage and phase versions. The case's two convection ovens are the clearest example: the European models behind their prices are three-phase units at 380-415 V and 400 V, while some of Unox's US models are listed at 480 V three-phase, and a model that doesn't match your electrical service isn't a bargain, it's a return. Three quotes, three ways of presenting the number: one includes freight, another says "plus freight", a third leaves the installation to you. Put them all in the same column without reading the fine print and your total can drift by several thousand dollars without a single math error, just a reading one.

That's why the equipment sheet doesn't have just one price column: it has a column that says, line by line, what basis that price is on: pre-tax, including sales tax, or not stated. In the case it reads "pre-tax" on every line, because US dealers list before tax (the two ovens included, at $2,772.00 and $2,077.20 before tax). The other two options aren't an oversight on our part: they're there for the quote that rolls everything into one delivered figure, or the used-equipment seller who doesn't say, and I'd rather you see them flagged that way than take a price one way or the other without checking.

Across the whole package, the sheet's sales tax rate ships at 0%, so its cash-out line equals the pre-tax total: $86,501. That's the case's figure, not yours: at the Texas maximum combined rate of 8.25%, the tax on the full list comes to about $7,136 more (about $5,526 on the critical lines alone), before freight and installation, which the sheet doesn't price. And unlike VAT or GST in other countries, US sales tax on equipment doesn't come back: it's part of what each machine costs you (chapter 4). If you plan your cash counting only the pre-tax number, you'll come up short of cash in exactly the weeks you need it most, the ones before you open.

The negotiating rule that follows from all this is easy to state and easy to forget the moment you sign: always compare pre-tax against pre-tax and delivered against delivered, never a delivered, installed price against a bare list price thinking you're comparing apples to apples, and always ask for the quote broken out: equipment, freight, installation and start-up as separate lines, sales tax on its own line, and the voltage and phase written next to each model. A serious dealer gives you that without your having to insist; the one who resists breaking it out is the first sign you need that third quote.

### The Ventless Setup as a Business Advantage

Going without a Type I hood isn't a technical detail the installer decides at the end: it's a business decision you make much earlier, the moment you start looking at spaces. A Type I hood with its grease duct to the roof and its fire suppression can be a major expense on its own (one published estimate puts a complete hood at $5,000 to $28,000 installed, plus $3,000 to $12,000 for suppression), and that requirement is what narrows the range of spaces you can consider from the start: many retail spaces, especially in buildings with apartments above, have no workable way to run a duct up to the roof, or doing it blows up the build-out before you've bought a single machine. Going ventless widens the number of spaces in your search and, in many cases, gets you access to more reasonable rents, precisely because that space doesn't work for the restaurants that do need a Type I hood. In the US this is a real option as long as your ovens qualify: hood type follows the appliance, its listing and the mechanical code your jurisdiction has adopted (IMC 507), so an enclosed electric convection or rack oven may need only a Type II hood, for heat and steam, or none at all if it's a single light-duty electric oven (3.7 kW or less) or an electric appliance listed to UL 710B, while deck and pizza-style ovens usually go under a Type I hood with fire suppression. Get that answer in writing from the building or mechanical department before you sign.

And it isn't just the space: it changes the reference equipment investment too. The ventless variant of this same modeled case, on the "Format Variant" sheet of equipment-and-suppliers-checklist.xlsx, comes to $60,480, against $66,980 for the base case with a storefront; the gap is exactly the $6,500 stainless steel exhaust duct line. That difference isn't free savings: along the way you lose part of the product and the capacity you'd have with the base case's equipment, because some of what a kitchen with a Type I hood can make depends on that very system (anything fried, to start with, and the deck oven, which usually needs one). Nor is it a saving across the whole opening: in chapter 1, the startup cost calculator's ventless format comes out at $167,180, a little above the core case's $165,800, because all-electric ovens and their Type II hood cost more. Before you go for this variant, be very clear about the menu you want to offer, because it isn't a universal solution that works equally well for every pastry concept: it works for some, and for others it's a limitation that the savings on rent and equipment don't make up for.

If your project also needs a roll-in retarder-proofer, keep in mind that it's one of the categories with the widest price range in the whole package, and the case's $8,500 is an assumption well below what a US dealer listed in October 2026: $27,937.80 for a one-section roll-in Doyon KDPR11, $33,099.00 for the two-section KDPR21, and $12,553.75 for a proofer-only cabinet with no retarding (Doyon DRIP1). It's another line where getting three quotes, and not trusting the first one, makes the difference between keeping the investment in check and blowing it up.

### Used Equipment and the Lead Times That Move Your Opening

Used equipment has its place in this package, but not on every line equally. It makes sense for sturdy, long-life equipment, where a serious inspection buys you years of trouble-free service: kitchen worktables and shelving, some refrigeration, pieces that don't work under the daily pressure of critical production. It makes much less sense for the machinery that will be running all day and that your ability to serve the menu depends on directly, because there a breakdown in the first week closes your display case, it doesn't just ruin your day. And for any piece of used equipment there are three things you always ask for, no exceptions: the original purchase invoice; the maintenance history; and, on the unit itself, the certification mark (NSF/ANSI or another ANSI-accredited program: equipment certified that way is deemed to comply with the Food Code's equipment rules, 4-205.10) along with the data plate showing model, voltage and phase, because your plans list every piece by make and model (8-201.12). If it runs on gas, add a licensed installer and whatever gas permit your building department asks for. Without those papers, what looks like a bargain can leave you owning a unit you have to prove complies on your own, and no discount, however big, makes up for that.

Of all the variables in this chapter, delivery lead time is the one that gets the least cool-headed attention and squeezes hardest at the end. The critical lead time in this package is nine weeks, and the retarder-proofer sets it: it's the first thing to order, even if it's the last thing you install. (The lead times in the sheet are assumptions; for reference, one US dealer listed a one-section roll-in retarder-proofer as usually shipping in 6 weeks from the manufacturer.) That number is your opening date written in weeks, and every week a dealer says "we've got it" without confirming it in writing comes off that margin.

The working rule this section closes with is the one that saves the most money and grief: you order equipment as soon as your plans are final, not when the build-out ends. Your plans already list each piece by make and model for plan review, so the order depends on the plans, not on the permit: equipment and build-out run in parallel, not one after the other, and if you wait for the last wall to go up before calling the oven dealer, you've already lost weeks you won't get back. ## 9. Permits, Health Department and Licenses: the Complete Path

### Your Permit: a Local Food Permit, Not an FDA Registration

Let's start with the mistake that costs the most money and grief in this business: no, your pastry shop doesn't register with the FDA. Federal food facility registration exempts retail food establishments (21 CFR 1.226(c)), and the definition fits a pastry shop with its own kitchen that sells to the public: its primary function is selling food directly to consumers, which the rule measures by value, as long as annual sales directly to consumers are larger than sales to all other buyers, and "consumers" doesn't include businesses (21 CFR 1.227; checked in October 2026). And if you sell online too: if more than half of your food sales, by value, go directly to consumers, at your counter or shipped to their door, you're generally still a retail food establishment, so your web shop doesn't put you on FDA's list.

What you need instead is more down to earth, though no less mandatory: a food establishment permit, a retail food permit, from whoever regulates food retail where you are. In most places that's your county or city health department; in some states it's a state agency, as you'll see below with New York and Florida. If you're starting from home, what governs you is your state's cottage food law instead, which closes this chapter. In the case modeled for this guide, the health department plan review and food permit application is budgeted at $120, a placeholder you'll replace with your county's fee.

Now the part nobody tells you up front, and the one that really matters: sending in the application doesn't let you open, and neither does getting your plans approved. The food permit is issued after plan review and the pre-opening inspection (Food Code 8-201.11 and Part 8-3), and you need it in hand to open the doors. And it doesn't replace the other track: the building permit, the certificate of occupancy (with a change of use if the space wasn't a food business before) and the business license come from your city or county, with their own process, their own fees and their own zoning code in each of the thousands of jurisdictions across the country. There's no national rule for that part, so you always ask your city and county. Health and building are two separate tracks, and you need both.

To give you a sense of the real size of the whole file for the case we've modeled in this guide: 30 steps in six phases, 8 of them flagged as varying by state. P1 asks whether the space can work before you sign anything: zoning, the hood type and duct route, the landlord's written OK, the electrical service, and the seven zones with their sinks. P2 sets up the business: LLC or sole proprietor and DBA, EIN and bank account, state sales tax permit and employer registration, stamped plans, and a lease with a permit contingency. P3 is plan review, the building permit and change of use, the business license and the sign permit. P4 is the build-out: construction, the equipment order with lead times in writing, the fire marshal (hood suppression, Class K extinguisher), the electrical permit and an ADA counter. P5 brings the food permit and pre-opening inspection, the FDA registration check for wholesale, the food safety plan with a certified food protection manager as person in charge, the training log, the written allergen notice and timekeeping. And P6 is opening: hiring at your state's minimum wage or above, minors on the early shift, a POS that tags sales as to-go or for-here, labels on what you package in the shop, sales tax setup and insurance. You have the full breakdown, step by step, on the "Permits Checklist (P1-P6)" sheet of permits-and-licenses-checklist.xlsx, which also shows which steps depend on where you are; certificates and cards go on the "Training Log" sheet (chapter 10), and the twelve milestones, M1 to M12, on the "Timeline and Critical Path" sheet (chapter 20).

### When FDA Registration Comes Back: the Gateway and the Three Checks

Everything above holds as long as you sell to your customers from your counter. But there's a gateway that can bring FDA registration back into the picture, and it goes in front of everything else, because if it doesn't apply to you, the rest of this section doesn't either: you sell food you make to other BUSINESSES (a café, a restaurant, a specialty food store, a caterer, anyone that isn't your own shop). Selling to consumers, even online and shipped to their door, isn't wholesale. If your kitchen only feeds your own display case, skip this section and go on to the next one.

If you're in because you do want to sell to other businesses, the "Selling Wholesale" sheet puts you through three checks: the FDA line, the cold chain and the labels. They don't weigh the same, and it's worth knowing why: only the first one decides whether you register with the FDA. The other two don't change your permit, but they're what your accounts and the inspector expect, and the sheet only gives you a green light when all three pass.

The FDA line can be measured two ways, and one is enough, because both measure the same thing (21 CFR 1.227; checked in October 2026): either the share of your food sales that goes to businesses stays below half (the sheet seeds a cautious working line of 25%, our own, not the law's, so you see the legal 50% coming long before you reach it), or, in dollars, your direct-to-consumer food sales in a typical week stay above your food sales to businesses that same week. This has a consequence that isn't intuitive: a kitchen that sells a lot over its own counter can wholesale a lot in absolute terms and still be a retail food establishment, as long as consumers take more than half of its food sales by value. And the flip side: what counts isn't kilos or one good week, it's value over the whole year, so check it with your POS figures and your invoices, not by eye.

The cold chain is the second check: can you deliver every wholesale order yourself, or with your own staff, keeping TCS items (cream fillings, custards, cheesecake) at 41 °F or below until the account receives them (Food Code 3-501.16; checked in October 2026)? The maximum delivery radius you type into the sheet, in miles, is your own figure, not a legal one: how far you can drive and still arrive at 41 °F or below. The sheet compares it with the distance to your farthest account, because it's the farthest one that decides.

And labels are the check that breaks most easily of the three: a single account without them is enough to fail it. Packaged items need the full label (common name, ingredients by weight, net quantity, your name and address, and the source of each major allergen, Food Code 3-602.11(B)); unpackaged items need an allergen sheet so the café can give its own customers the written notice the Food Code asks for (3-602.12(C)), and the paperwork isn't optional either: keep a record of each account (what, how much and when), and ask your CPA about resale certificates for what you sell to accounts that resell it. Watch the 50% line whenever one account grows, because a single hotel or chain can tip it. And two state notes: in New York, the Article 20-C license already covers wholesale bakery operations; in Florida, ask FDACS whether your volume calls for its Wholesale/Manufactured Food Establishment Permit.

### A Central Kitchen and Your Own Shops: One Owner, Several Sites

And here's the growth lever that changes the math on everything above if your plan is to open more than one shop: a single production kitchen baking for your own storefronts. As long as it's all under the same owner, what you bake and then sell over your own counters reaches consumers, not businesses; your own shop isn't a business you sell to, so on a plain reading it doesn't add to the wholesale side of your FDA line the way a café down the street does. One caution before you build a plan on that: FDA's test is applied establishment by establishment, and we didn't find the rule's text spelling out how it treats a production kitchen in a separate building that sells only through its owner's own shops. If that's your setup, put the question to FDA or a food attorney before you sign for the space.

Now the obligation that comes attached to that good news, because there always is one: each shop goes through the process on its own, even if the whole network is yours. Plan review and the pre-opening inspection look at a physical place, so expect each new shop to go through them, and ask your agency before you open the second one whether it needs its own permit and how it treats food you bake at one site and sell at another (that's the third question on the "Which Permit Do I Need" sheet). In New York, the Article 20-C license already names wholesale and retail in-store bakery operations; in Florida, ask FDACS whether supplying several shops calls for its Wholesale/Manufactured Food Establishment Permit. Keep three conditions in view for the setup to hold: the same owner across the kitchen and every shop, the cold chain between sites at 41 °F or below for TCS items (Food Code 3-501.16), and the written allergen notice at every counter that sells unpackaged product (3-602.12(C)). If a shop changes hands, or belongs to a partner's separate company, it stops being yours for this purpose: it's a business, and what you send it goes back to the rules of the previous section.

### Four Example States, and How to Find Yours

Let's get specific. Here are four states as examples, not all fifty: the food permit is issued by a county or city health department in most places and by a state agency in others, and each state enforces its own edition of FDA's model Food Code (per FDA's 2024 adoption list, California has its own code, Texas and Florida enforce the 2017 edition, and New York's retail food stores the 2001 one). A fifty-state table, let alone one by county, would be out of date within months, so what follows is how to read the system, not a closed map. Learn to read one agency's page and you can read them all.

Texas is the case's state and makes a good template. Your permit comes from the local health department, city or county, where there is one, and from the Department of State Health Services (DSHS) where there isn't. Plans go in before you build: the Food Code (8-201.11) asks for plans and specifications before construction or remodeling, and it's the local department that reviews them. Fees are local too: Austin charges $312 for plan review of a new food establishment and $178 for the pre-opening inspection (FY 2025-26), and Williamson County's plan review runs $882. And the order matters more than the fee: you submit plans before the build-out, you pass the pre-opening inspection when it's finished, and only then is the permit issued; nobody opens on an application. California is the strictest about timing: plan review by the county or city environmental health department is mandatory before construction, the building department won't issue a building permit without the health department's approval, and the health department has 20 working days to review the plans or they're deemed approved (Health and Safety Code §114380).

New York and Florida show how far the system can vary: there, a bakery that bakes and sells to go is licensed by the state agriculture department, not the health department. In New York, that's an Article 20-C food processing establishment license from the Department of Agriculture and Markets, which names "retail in-store bakery operations, including bake-off operations" outright; add seating and serve food, and the local health department comes in. Agriculture and Markets doesn't describe a plan review of the space the way a health department does, so ask before you build. New York City is a case of its own: the city's health department lists bakeries among food service establishments, and for mixed shops it looks at revenue (more than half from prepared food means the city's food service establishment permit; less, the state license). In Florida, bakeries fall under the Department of Agriculture and Consumer Services (FDACS), while what's eaten on site is licensed by the Department of Business and Professional Regulation (DBPR); for a bakery-café we couldn't confirm which one takes the lead, so ask both. FDACS's plan review is voluntary ($55.10: $25 plus $30.10 for the first hour), you apply 21 days before opening, the inspector calls within 3 to 5 business days, and the inspection typically happens within two weeks.

Finding your own agency takes five minutes at the computer. Search for "[your county] environmental health food facility plan check" (or "[your city] food establishment plan review"), and check your state department of agriculture as well, because in some states, as in New York and Florida, that's who licenses a bakery that bakes and sells to go. Once you're on the agency's page, check three things before you take anything as settled: which agency permits your format (selling to go and adding seating can put you under different ones), whether plan review is required, what it costs and how long it takes, and which edition of the Food Code your state enforces, because that's what your inspector will hold you to. One warning that saves you a wasted trip: fees vary a lot, from a voluntary $55.10 review in Florida to several hundred dollars in a Texas county, so check the agency's own fee schedule or call before you go; don't assume it's the same everywhere. For scale, this pack's financial model budgets the health department plan review and food permit application at $120, below what Austin charges: a small line, but replace it with your county's fee so your cash doesn't come up short in the first month.

### The Home Route: Cottage Food, State by State

If you're going to bake at home, the first change is legal before it's practical: your home kitchen becomes a cottage food operation under your state's law, and what it takes to get started depends on the state. In California it's a registration (Class A) or a permit (Class B) with your county environmental health department, plus a food safety course within three months; in Texas, a registration with DSHS, but only if you sell TCS items, and local authorities can't require a permit, license or fee; in New York, a home processor registration with Agriculture and Markets, free and with no expiration, though processing can take up to 16 weeks; and in Florida, no license or permit at all. Outside Florida, and outside Texas for items that don't need refrigeration, you register: you don't just assume you're covered.

What decides what you can sell from home is your state's list, and the four states write it in very different ways: California allows only foods on its Approved Cottage Food List (non-potentially hazardous foods); Texas allows any food that isn't on its exclusion list; New York has a closed list (breads, muffins, cookies, brownies, scones, double-crust fruit pies, simple cakes); and Florida allows cottage food products that don't need time and temperature control. The consequence of all this is the one that kills more orders than any other: by default, only shelf-stable baked goods qualify, and a strawberries & cream cake does NOT, because cream fillings need refrigeration. In California, New York and Florida it stays out; in Texas, since September 1, 2025, it can go in if you register with DSHS, label it with the production date and safe-handling instructions and deliver it at 41 °F or below. So it isn't a national ban on cream cakes from home: it's that, unless your state has opened that door, you're outside the list by default.

And that a state opens the door isn't a hypothesis: Texas did it with Senate Bill 541, in force since September 1, 2025, which widened cottage food to any food that isn't excluded, up to $150,000 a year, refrigerated items included with registration. With the same clarity, here's what Texas did NOT open: wholesale of TCS items, and online sales you don't deliver yourself. Opening up the product list isn't the same as opening up the channels, and that's why your state's own page, not a summary, is what you check every year.

The channel rules come in three, and it pays to keep all three in your head because they don't work the same way in each state. Wholesale to cafés, shops and restaurants is closed outright in Florida, allowed only for non-TCS items sold to a registered cottage food vendor in Texas, only with a Class B permit in California, and allowed within the state in New York. Shipping by mail or courier is fine in California and Florida; in Texas, only if you, an employee or a household member delivers; and in New York, only within the state. And selling across state lines is closed in New York; elsewhere, check your state's page before you ship. California adds a cap on staff: one full-time employee who isn't family.

The four states cap cottage food by annual sales in dollars, not by weight: in 2026, California $88,878 for Class A and $177,756 for Class B (adjusted every year), Texas $150,000 and Florida $250,000, where all sales count; New York's page lists no cap. On top of the law, the "Cottage Food Route" sheet adds two checks of its own: a weekly volume ceiling for a home kitchen of 100 kg (about 220 lb, or roughly 14 kg a day), and a volume-for-space house criterion in kilos per usable square foot a week. Neither one is a legal figure: they're ours, there to make you measure what the law doesn't. The pack's example helps you see how the three checks relate: 129 usable square feet dedicated to baking and 60 kg (132 lb) a week come to 0.46 kg per usable square foot a week, under the sheet's house criterion of 0.74. In practice, a normal home kitchen almost never reaches the volume ceiling; what trips people up is the third check, the one that is the law: sales records that prove you're under your state's cap. Without them you can't show it, whatever your volume, so keep them up to date; don't pull them together the day someone asks. And don't forget the label: in Texas, word for word, "THIS PRODUCT WAS PRODUCED IN A PRIVATE RESIDENCE THAT IS NOT SUBJECT TO GOVERNMENTAL LICENSING OR INSPECTION.", plus allergens, and for TCS items the production date and safe-handling instructions; in New York, "Made in a Home Kitchen"; in Florida, "Made in a cottage food operation that is not subject to Florida's food safety regulations." You outgrow cottage food when you pass your state's sales cap, want to sell items your state excludes, want to wholesale where your state forbids it, want to ship out of state or want a storefront with a display case and staff: then it's time for a licensed kitchen, and decision 10 in the bonus walks you through that jump.

**The three cases, so you can see where the line falls (permits-and-licenses-checklist.xlsx, "Which Permit Do I Need" sheet)**

| Your situation | What you need | Why |
|---|---|---|
| You sell only to consumers, at your counter and on your website | A retail food permit from your local (or state) food agency | You're a retail food establishment, exempt from FDA registration. Selling online to consumers, shipped to their door, generally keeps you retail while consumers take more than half of your food sales by value. |
| You also sell to cafés or stores, consumers still take more than half of your sales, and you pass the three wholesale checks | The same retail food permit, PLUS full labels or allergen sheets for every account and a cold chain you can show | Still outside FDA registration, with more paperwork. The "Selling Wholesale" sheet runs the three checks one by one. |
| You sell to businesses and fail one of the three checks | FDA registration if businesses take half or more of your food sales; otherwise, fix what fails | Failing the 50% line is what triggers FDA registration; failing cold chain or labels is a fix before your next delivery. Watch the line when one account grows: a single hotel or chain can tip it. |
