## 20. Timeline, Opening Day and the First Ninety Days

### The Critical Path and Your Opening Date

The critical path is the chain of milestones that can't slip: if any one of them moves a day, opening moves a full day, because everything after it depends on that milestone finishing first. It isn't the longest path by accident; it's the path that rules the whole calendar. In the case modeled for this guide, that chain has 11 milestones on the critical path, and the whole project, from the day you start looking for a space to the day you open the doors for the first time, takes 8.5 months. That number doesn't come from a hunch or from a deadline that sounds reasonable: it comes from adding up the duration of each milestone in the chain, one after another, with no slack between them. The milestones that aren't on the critical path do have room, and that room is exactly what lets you give them attention without the overall clock suffering.

Chaining those 11 milestones puts the modeled case's opening in month 9 of the calendar year, September. I insist on this because it's the idea that's hardest to take on board when you're setting up your own pastry shop: the opening month isn't a date you pick by looking at the calendar and saying "I want to be open by then." It's the result of a calculation. You start counting from the day the project starts, add the duration of each milestone on the critical path, and month 9 is where the last domino falls. If you want to open sooner, the only real lever is to shorten one of the milestones in that chain of 11 (not the others, because they already have room of their own and shortening them moves nothing up), and if you can't, month 9 is your date, whether you accept it or not.

Inside that chain there's one decision worth explaining on its own, because it's the one that can save you the most weeks in the whole calendar: ordering and delivering the production kitchen equipment doesn't wait for the build-out to finish. The two run in parallel. As soon as you know what you're buying (the oven, the blast chiller, the mixer, the roll-in retarder-proofer, the display case), you place the order, even if the space is still under construction. Those are critical pieces precisely because their manufacturing and delivery lead time is long and doesn't depend on you: it depends on the manufacturer, its order backlog and freight. If you wait until the build-out is finished to start calling dealers, that lead time gets added to the end of the calendar instead of overlapping with the build-out, and those weeks come straight off your opening date with nothing you can do to win them back. And it works because the order depends on your plans, not on your permit: your plans already list each piece by make and model for plan review (chapter 8). Ordering in parallel is the decision that saves the most real time in the whole project, more than any tweak to the paperwork.

What you do have to watch, and this goes for every duration in the timeline, is that they're planning assumptions, not legal processing times: how long your permits take depends on your county and your city, and no timeline can promise you how many weeks yours will take. The only legal clock anywhere near this chart is California's, where the health department has 20 working days to review your plans or they're deemed approved. What the timeline does give you is the logic of dependencies (what has to be finished before something else can start), and that logic is the same whether the permit process speeds up or slows down.

### Opening Outside a Peak and Reaching Three Kings Day Already Broken In

There's one criterion that outweighs every other when you read the timeline: opening inside a big peak is the worst possible scenario. And it is for a very concrete reason, not superstition: it's the day you'd be breaking in the equipment, a brand-new team and your suppliers all at once, right when you have the most to produce. A production kitchen that hasn't run a single real shift, a team that hasn't worked together for one day and suppliers you haven't placed even a test order with, all converging on the busiest day of the year. That's why the timeline doesn't just give you a date: it warns you if that date falls inside a peak, because that alert is what lets you react in time, either by moving one of the milestones that do have room or by accepting, with your eyes open, that you're going to open under fire.

The strategy that holds up is the opposite one: open with enough room before the big peak to arrive with the production kitchen already broken in and the schedule already tested. Let the first peak, the one that decides whether your year starts well or badly, find you with machinery you know, a team that already knows where everything is and suppliers who have filled at least a couple of orders without surprises. Which peaks your calendar has, and how to plan them ahead, is the subject of chapter 15, on the six peaks of the year, and that's where you should cross your opening date with the full commercial calendar for the year.

The modeled case's timeline, opening in September, leaves a few weeks before All Saints' Day and Día de los Muertos (October 30 to November 1), a short peak that works as a dress rehearsal, with Thanksgiving and Christmas after it in the case's monthly curve, and more than three months before Three Kings Day (January 2-6), the peak behind the case's January, 11.2% of the year's sales (chapter 2). That gap is exactly what separates a calm opening from a blind one. The timeline itself, the table right below this text, is what warns you whether your real date, yours, falls inside or outside that window. It's the cheapest check you can run before you sign a construction contract: look at which month your planned opening falls in and compare it with the peak calendar before you commit to anything.

### Your Hours and Your Sales Mix as First-Day Business Decisions

There's a commercial lever many new pastry shops don't use because they don't think of it as one: your days and hours. Before you print them, check the places that can limit them, and ask in writing: your lease (whether it, or the shopping center's rules and CC&Rs, says anything about the hours you keep), your city's zoning for that address, and its noise ordinance, which tends to matter more for a 4:30 a.m. early bake, its rooftop exhaust fan and its deliveries than for the counter (chapter 6). With those answers in hand, the hours you choose are a business decision, not a legal accident, and Sunday is where that decision matters most.

But in the US, the rule that really moves with your business model isn't about hours: it's about what your shop mainly is, and it's measured by your sales mix. Several of the rules in this guide draw their line at "more than half" or at a share of sales. Texas writes its sales tax exemption for a bakery, a business where more than half of sales are display-case bakery items to be eaten somewhere else, and those items stay exempt unless you sell them with your own plates or utensils; California's 80/80 rule can tax even cold food to go once more than 80% of your sales are food and more than 80% of that food is taxable, unless you keep separate accounts; in Florida, once you have seating, five items or fewer are presumed to be eaten on site; and New York City decides between its own food service permit and the state license by whether more than half of a mixed shop's revenue is prepared food. Open a bakery with a café and, over time, let the café outsell the counter of cakes and pastries, and your tax treatment, and in New York City even your permit, can change without your having touched the sign on the door. That's not a question of hours; it's a question of business model: how you design your menu, your sales floor and your revenue mix from day one decides which side of those lines you're on. Keep it in mind when you decide how much floor goes to café tables and how much to the display case, because that split isn't only a layout decision: it's what your sales tax setup rests on, and it's why your POS tags every sale as to-go or for-here from the first day (phase P6, chapter 9; chapter 19 for the details).

From there comes a business decision you make on day one, not after a year of being open: Sunday is cake day. It's the day with the most custom orders picked up and the most last-minute buying, and deciding whether you open that day, and with which staff (because opening on a Sunday without the right Counter Associate is worse than not opening), shapes your schedule from the first week. The modeled case keeps the counter closed on Sundays, and that's an assumption to test against this, not a recommendation. How that schedule is organized, which shifts cover Sunday and how it's split across the team is covered in chapter 14, on shifts; what belongs here is that the decision to open on Sunday, with what hours and with whom, is made before you open the door, not improvised when there's already a line at the display case.

### What to Measure in Month Zero and Month Three

Without indicators there's no correcting anything. You can have the best production kitchen in town and lose money simply because nobody is looking at the numbers that would show where the business is bleeding. That's why this guide doesn't close with an opening date and nothing more: it closes with a startup dashboard, the one right below this text, which sets what gets measured from day one, how often it gets measured and who is responsible for taking that number. That last part isn't a minor detail: an indicator without an owner doesn't get measured, however well it's designed on paper. If nobody is assigned to write down display-case waste at closing, or the number of custom orders picked up against the ones promised, that figure simply doesn't exist three months later, and without it there's no adjustment possible.

Month zero starts on opening day and gives you the first real picture of the business, the one that replaces every assumption you've worked with on paper until now. Month three is the point where you have enough data to stop flying blind and start correcting with judgment: with three months of history you can adjust your menu mix (which items sell and which take up display space without turning over), the daily production of each item and the schedule itself, moving hours from where there are too many to where the counter falls short. That month-three adjustment isn't done by eye: it's done with the tools in this pack that are designed to be filled in with exactly the data you've been writing down since month zero, and it's in them that the adjustment turns into a concrete decision on staffing, production and menu.

And here's the border that closes this guide: from this point on, your business is open. Everything that comes after (the weekly production plan, tracking custom orders day by day, the daily display-case and kitchen logs) isn't this guide's job anymore: once you're open, your checklists and logs tell you what to do each day; this guide is everything you have to decide before. The engineering of your menu, with the costing method that decides which items you keep and at what price, is in the Food Cost + Menu Engineering Guide. And the food safety records your kitchen has to keep from the first day of real production (temperatures, traceability, cleaning) come in the HACCP Food Safety Kit Pro. This guide has brought you to the door with the regulatory map, the timeline and the numbers model; what happens inside, from now on, those other tools cover.

## Regulatory Appendix (US), Updated October 2026

### What's in Force and Which Edition Applies

Cut-off date for this appendix: October 2026. It's a snapshot of the rules on the day this edition closed, so that a year from now you can check for yourself what still stands of everything you've read. It doesn't explain each rule again (that's done in its chapter); it only says where the rule lives, which edition applies and where to check it. In the US the question is rarely whether a rule has been repealed outright: far more often it's which edition your state has adopted, which agency enforces it where you open, and whether a figure in it has been updated for the year.

The food safety pillar of the whole book is the FDA Food Code 2022, the federal model. It isn't law on its own: each state adopts an edition, and your inspector enforces that one. Per FDA's 2024 adoption report, California applies its own Retail Food Code (which FDA lists against the 2005 edition), Texas and Florida the 2017 edition, and New York's Department of Agriculture and Markets the 2001 edition for retail food stores; by 2024, seven states had adopted the 2022 edition (Colorado, Connecticut, Illinois, Mississippi, Ohio, Pennsylvania and Utah), with Hawaii and Virginia following in 2025. The section numbers this guide cites (2-102.12, 2-103.11(O), 3-501.14, 3-501.16, 3-501.17, 3-602.11(B), 3-602.12(C), 4-205.10, 8-201.11 and 8-201.12, among others) are the 2022 ones, and building to them is the safe choice. On the federal side, the exemption from FDA food facility registration for retail food establishments (21 CFR 1.226(c), with the definition in 1.227) stands as read in the eCFR, up to date as of October 14, 2026.

On packaging, the rules are state rules and their dates are already behind us. New York has banned expanded polystyrene (EPS) foam containers since January 1, 2022, and intentionally added PFAS in food packaging since December 31, 2022. California has banned EPS food service ware since CalRecycle announced on April 4, 2025 that the 25% recycling rate SB 54 requires hadn't been met (the Attorney General reminded businesses on December 2, 2025, with administrative penalties of up to $50,000 a day), and it has banned PFAS in plant-fiber packaging since January 1, 2023 (AB 1200). Florida reserves the regulation of polystyrene to the state (§500.90, read in its 2021 edition), and in Texas we found no statewide ban. On food waste, California's edible food recovery rules (SB 1383) reach only large generators, its commercial organics recycling law (AB 1826) has used a threshold of 2 cubic yards of solid waste since September 2020, and the federal Bill Emerson Good Samaritan Food Donation Act (42 U.S.C. §1791) protects good-faith donors, as amended on January 5, 2023 to cover some direct donations too. On accessibility, the sales counter follows ADA Standards 904.4.1. And on the hood, the International Mechanical Code sections were read through one state's adoption, Minnesota's, of an older edition, because the current IMC (2021 or 2024) wasn't open to us: the mechanical code your jurisdiction has adopted is the one that governs.

On taxes, there's no national food tax rule: each state draws its own line, and for a bakery it follows how you sell, not what you sell. California (CDTFA, Regulation 1603, with its 80/80 rule), Texas (34 TAC §3.293, as the Comptroller explains it, with a 6.25% state rate plus up to 2% local, 8.25% at most combined), New York (TB-ST-283 and TB-ST-806, plus advisory opinion TSB-A-24(19)S on production equipment) and Florida (TIP 10A01-22) are current as read; Florida has a proposed rewrite of Rule 12A-1.011 that we couldn't confirm had taken effect. On federal payroll and owner taxes, the IRS pages read in October 2026 give FICA at 7.65% (with a 2026 Social Security wage base of $184,500), FUTA at 0.6% after the credit on the first $7,000, self-employment tax at 15.3% and the default tax treatment of a one-member or multi-member LLC.

Labeling is federal and current: the nine major allergens, with sesame added on January 1, 2023 (FALCPA and the FASTER Act); "gluten-free" under 20 ppm (21 CFR 101.91); and the general ban on false or misleading labeling (FD&C Act §403(a)). We found no federal definition of "homemade" or "scratch-made" (nor did we confirm that none exists), which is why the guide asks you to say what you actually do. New York signed a law on November 12, 2025 on allergen notices for food packaged and sold in the same store, bakeries included; we couldn't confirm when it takes effect. And a scope warning you can't skip: only four example states have been checked, so the state rows in the table below can't be read as a census of all fifty. Check your own state's agencies.

Last, wages: there's no industry-wide pay agreement in the US, so what moves are the minimums. The Department of Labor's state table used here was updated on July 1, 2026; Florida's rate was scheduled to rise on September 30, 2026, which we couldn't confirm with FloridaCommerce, the state agency that publishes it. Texas applies the federal $7.25.

If you're opening in the UK, one block is enough here: the UK works with 14 allergens, food you prepack for direct sale needs a full ingredient list, and for unpackaged food the information can be given in any form, even orally, as long as a notice tells customers how to get it (gov.uk allergen guidance for food businesses, https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/allergen-guidance-for-food-businesses/allergen-guidance-for-food-businesses, checked October 2026). Your Equivalents Guide maps the rest of the terms.

### The Dates We Already Know Will Move

There are five blocks here, with the seven rows of the table below, and all of them will move on their own without a word of the rest of the text changing, which is why they're grouped here instead of scattered across twenty chapters: when the time comes, you update this section and the rest of the guide stays true.

The first is the minimum wage, at every level, and it's renewed whenever your state or city sets a new rate. The case's reference, the federal $7.25 that Texas applies, $15,080 a year for a full-time position, lives in the "Settings" sheet of staff-schedule-and-labor-cost.xlsx (and in the "0. Assumptions" sheet of 3-year-financial-plan.xlsx) along with the rest of the payroll figures; when a new rate is published where you open, you change that cell and the sheets that depend on it recalculate on their own. Florida's own schedule set a step for September 30, 2026, which is why its rate needs checking now.

The second is California's cottage food law, which moves twice: its sales caps are adjusted every year for inflation (for 2026, $88,878 for Class A and $177,756 for Class B), and CDPH has given notice of an amendment to its Approved Cottage Food List that adds chocolate and nut spreads, with the new list expected around November 16, 2026. Both dates have to be checked together.

The third is New York's allergen notice for food packaged and sold in the same store: signed on November 12, 2025, with an effective date that one law firm puts around November 2026 and that we couldn't confirm in the law's text.

The fourth is the edition of the Food Code your state enforces: when it adopts the 2022 edition, as Hawaii and Virginia did in 2025, the section numbers and wording your inspector uses can change.

And the fifth is the calendar of the peaks that move every year: Easter Sunday 2027 falls on March 28, so Holy Week lands in March, and Mardi Gras 2027, the end of king cake season, falls on February 9.

The figures the workbooks calculate with (the minimum wage, the peak months) live in editable cells, never sewn into a paragraph of prose; the rest live in chapter text and sheet notes, and the table below tells you which ones to revisit. That's why changing a date or a rate doesn't force you to rewrite any other chapter, only this table.

### The Old Rules Free Guides Still Quote

There's a concrete problem behind this section: free guides written before these changes are still online, and whoever reads one and plans a kitchen or a product line around it is leaning on a rule that has since changed. In some cases the old version is looser than the current one, so the mistake doesn't show until an inspection, a customer's reaction or an order you turned away for nothing.

Here are five, each with what replaced it:

- "Potentially hazardous food" is the old name for what the Food Code now calls TCS food, food that needs time and temperature control for safety (1-201.10). It's the same idea, decided by pH and water activity, and some state texts still use the old term (California's cottage food list speaks of "non-potentially hazardous" foods): what changed is the name, so don't let the two terms make you think there are two rules.
- The certified manager: older editions of the Food Code asked for at least one employee with supervisory responsibility to be a certified food protection manager; the 2022 edition makes the person in charge one (2-102.12). Your inspector enforces your state's edition, but building to the 2022 rule is the safe choice.
- Eight major allergens: since January 1, 2023 there are nine, with sesame (FASTER Act). An allergen chart without sesame is out of date, and so is any written notice built from it.
- Texas cottage food without cream fillings: since Senate Bill 541, in force since September 1, 2025, Texas allows TCS items such as cream cakes and cheesecake from home if you register with DSHS, label them with the production date and safe-handling instructions and deliver them at 41 °F or below, up to $150,000 a year. Guides written before then still say no.
- California's $75,000 and $150,000 cottage food caps: those are the base figures in the law, adjusted every year for inflation; for 2026 they're $88,878 (Class A) and $177,756 (Class B), per CDPH.

Outside this section, the rest of the guide uses the current version of all five: if you see the old one quoted as current somewhere else, that content hasn't been reviewed since the change.

### How to Check a Rule's Current Version in One Minute

The procedure is always the same, and you don't need a screenshot to follow it. For a federal regulation, search its number ("21 CFR 1.227", "21 CFR 101.91") in the eCFR, the Electronic Code of Federal Regulations, which shows the date its text is up to date as of (October 14, 2026, when we read it). For the Food Code, open FDA's report on adoption by state (the fourth row of the table below) and find your state's agency and edition. For everything else (permits, cottage food, sales tax, wages), go straight to the state agency's own page in the table, not to a summary of it.

Every official page carries a line that tells you how fresh it is: the eCFR's "up to date as of", the IRS's "Page Last Reviewed or Updated" (October 10, 2026 on its LLC page), the Texas Department of Insurance's "Last updated" (June 8, 2026 on its page for employers) or FDA's "Content current as of" (April 7, 2026 on its allergen cleaning study). If that line is missing or very old, nobody has touched the page in a long time; that doesn't prove the rule changed, but it's a signal to call the agency. And below it, look at the section you need, not just the rule as a whole: a section can be amended, renumbered or moved while the rest stays in force, and the Food Code itself changes its wording between editions (the certified manager rule did, between the older editions and the 2022 one).

The advice that saves this edition's mistakes is exactly that: check the section, not the code, and check the edition your inspector enforces, not the newest model. A code can be current as a whole and the specific section you care about can read differently in your state's edition, or have moved, which is why this appendix, with its October 2026 cut-off, should be rechecked section by section and state by state when you come back to it in a year.
